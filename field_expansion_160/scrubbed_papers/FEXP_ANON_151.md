## Abstract

In this study, phenolic compounds (PC), volatile compounds (VC), antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties of the Thymbra spicata L. plant grown in the Mardin Yeşilli region were investigated. The properties of compounds such as rhoifolin, leucoside, naringenin, rosmarinic acid, γ-terpinene, and carvacrol, which were found to be higher than other compounds as a result of PC and VC analysis of Thymbra spicata L. plant, and the properties of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs diclofenac and ketoprofen were compared with computer-based calculations. In this context, the Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion, and Toxicity (ADMET) profiles of the mentioned molecules were calculated, Density Functional Theory (DFT)-based quantum chemical calculations were performed, and molecular docking analyses were performed. In addition, the pharmacological properties, biological targets, and biological activities of these compounds were comprehensively evaluated. This study provides important data for understanding the therapeutic potential of these plant components.

Chromatographic methods (GC–MS and LC-Orbitrap HRMS) were used to look at PC and VC profiles, spectrophotometry (ABTS, DPPH, CUPRAC) to look at antioxidant activities, and the disk diffusion method to look at antimicrobial properties.

While the most detected volatile compounds were γ-terpinene (35.34%), carvacrol (17.55%), caryophyllene (10.53%), and p-cymene (9.15%), the most detected phenolic compounds were rosmarinic acid (3334.9 ng/mL), naringenin (2610.00 ng/mL), leucoside (1956.42 ng/mL), and rhoifolin (1288.55 ng/mL). The ethanol extract of Thymbra spicata L. stopped the growth of K. aerogenes in particular. The IC50 value for DPPH was 158.01 µg/mL, the IC50 value for ABTS was 185.03 µg/mL, and the C0.5A value was 87.13 for the ethanol extract of Thymbra spicata L . The molecular docking results that the strongest binding energy was between TNF-α and naringenin (-6.77 kcal/mol), IL-6 and rosmarinic acid (-5.55 kcal/mol), and NLRP3 and carvacrol (-6.88 kcal/mol). Naringenin, rosmarinic acid, diclofenac, and ketoprofen completely violated the restrictions established by Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge. Rosmarinic acid is the most reactive antioxidant compound with low energy deficit (Eg) and high softness (S). ɣ-terpinene and carvacrol, on the other hand, are more stable and durable wit high hardness (ɳ) and low softness.

Naringenin, rosmarinic acid, and carvacrol demonstrate significant anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties owing to their high binding affinity for TNF-α, IL-6, and NLRP3, positioning them as promising candidates for the treatment of inflammatory disorders. Naringenin and rosmarinic acid effectively target TNF-α, whereas carvacrol demonstrates potential in the management of NLRP3 inflammasome-associated diseases. The strong binding of rosmarinic acid to IL-6 shows that it can change the immune system. Thymbra spicata L., abundant in bioactive compounds, may provide benefits compared to conventional NSAIDs and exhibit potential as monotherapy or adjunctive therapies. Their strong anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, and pharmacological properties, along with their safety, make naringenin, rosmarinic acid, and carvacrol good candidates for more preclinical and clinical research.

## Introduction

Aromatic plants, also known as aromatic herbs, herbs, and spices, have been utilized since ancient times for folk medicine and food preservation. These plants possess antimicrobial, antioxidant, antiparasitic, antiprotozoal, antifungal, and anti-inflammatory properties. Being natural and environmentally friendly, aromatic plants are widely regarded as safe and sustainable products [ 1 ]. Thyme is the common name for many taxa belonging to the genera Thymbra and Thymus. Thymbra and Thymus species are frequently seen in areas where the origin of the genus Thymus is considered to be located in the western Mediterranean region [ 2 ]. Thymbra spicata L. is one of the main ingredients of “Zaatar”, widely used in the Middle East and Mediterranean region, its fresh or dried leaves are used as a spice in salads and food. “Zaatar” is a popular blend used to relieve cramps, muscle aches, hyperglycemia, and inflammation. It is found in Southeastern Anatolia of Türkiye, in the coastal parts of Thrace, and in the Aegean and Mediterranean regions. In various regions of Türkiye, herbal tea is used as a spice in the treatment of colic, bronchitis, asthma, cough, diarrhea, and rheumatism [ 3 – 14 ]. Inflammation is a biological defense mechanism that enables living cells to protect themselves against diseases such as bacteria, fungi, viruses, physical agents, and defective immunity, and it is a common and important basic pathological process [ 15 ]. During inflammation, reactive oxygen species (ROS), reactive nitrogen species (RNS), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukins (Ils), cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) are highly produced in host cells as bioindicators of inflammation [ 16 ]. IL-6 promotes the maturation and activation of neutrophils, the development of macrophages, the differentiation and maintenance of cytotoxic T cells and NK cells. It also increases the production of TNF-α and IL-1β. In addition, IL-6 promotes the release of acute-phase proteins (C-reactive protein, amyloid A protein, and fibrinogen) by hepatocytes [ 17 , 18 ]. NLRP3 (protein 3 containing NOD-, LRR-, and pyrin domain) is an intracellular sensor that detects a wide range of microbial motifs, endogenous distress signals, and environmental irritants, resulting in the formation and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [ 19 ]. The phytochemistry of plants is used as an important alternative to plant-based medicines in the treatment of different ailments with minimal side effects and high public acceptance [ 20 , 21 ]. Inflammation, which also has a significant impact on human health and plays a role in many other diseases, has received more and more attention in the treatment of phytochemicals in recent years [ 22 ]. Because plant-based phytochemicals have anti-inflammatory properties, they have focused on key targets in drug development by antagonizing, neutralizing, or blocking specific products, such as enzyme-inhibiting activities [ 23 ]. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are the most preferred class of drugs used in the treatment of various pathological conditions such as pain, fever, inflammatory diseases, and rheumatoid arthritis. However, the side effects from these drugs may include the need to replace synthetic drugs with plant-based drugs that pose minimal risk [ 24 , 25 ]. Diclofenac and ketoprofen is a proven, widely prescribed NSAID with analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antipyretic properties and has been shown to be effective in treating a variety of acute and chronic pain and inflammatory conditions. As with all NSAIDs, diclofenac and ketoprofen exert their effect by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis by inhibiting COX-1 and COX-2 [ 26 , 27 ]. Over the past few years, AI has become a transformative force in the field of drug discovery and development, radically changing many aspects of the process. It covers topics such as identifying and prioritizing new therapeutic targets, predicting drug-target interactions, designing new drug-like molecules, and evaluating the clinical efficacy of new drugs [ 28 ]. Although various reports have been published on the analysis of Thymbra or Thymus essential oil (EO) composition, antifungal, antioxidant, antibacterial, antimycobacterial activities, there are no reports for this species and for plant species of this genus grown in the Mardin region of Türkiye in which their major compounds are discussed with computer-based calculations. This reveals the originality of the study [ 29 – 31 ]. In recent years, Thymbra spicata L. has been used in traditional medicine to treat various diseases, and its volatile and phenolic chemicals have attracted the attention of scientists due to their possible health benefits. Although significant research has been done on the biological activity of phenolic and volatile compounds of Thymbra spicata L., there are not many studies that comprehensively examine these compounds using computer-aided techniques. In this study, the key molecules identified in the plant were comprehensively evaluated using computer-based methods (ADMET, DFT, molecular docking analyses, biological activity and target interactions). The aim of this study was to evaluate bioactive molecules (rhoifolin, leucoside, naringenin, rosmarinic acid, ɣ -terpinene, carvacrol) of Thymbra spicata L. for their anti-inflammatory potential using computational techniques and to compare them with reference drugs such as diclofenac and ketoprofen to support preclinical studies. To achieve this goal, molecular interactions between target structures and ligands such as TNF-α, IL-6 and NLRP3, which play critical roles in the inflammatory process, were analyzed by computational methods (molecular docking, ADMET, DFT calculations). The biological activities and interaction mechanisms of these compounds were comprehensively investigated and supported the analyses. This approach aims to reduce the cost and duration of experimental studies while contributing to the identification of effective candidate molecules in preclinical studies.


## Materials and methods


## Plant material

Plant samples used in this study were collected in Yeşilli district of Mardin. Location: 37°19′15″ 40°48′26″ E, 37°20′42″ N 40°49′15″ E, 400–600. In all analysis, Thymbra spicata L. aerial components (without flowers) were employed. The collected plants are cleared of weeds. Plant specimens were verified by Assoc. Prof. Dr. Fatma Mungan Kılıç a plant taxonomist. The collected plant specimens were numbered (MARIUM 43) in the Herbarium of Mardin Artuklu University (MARIUM). The above-ground parts (2000 g) were cut into 2–3 cm pieces using pruning shears on May 20th (at 11 am) and dried in the shade, then the dried specimens were ground into powder using a grinder.


## Preparation of the plant's ethanol extract

Thymbra spicata L . samples were washed with distilled water. In the shade, Thymbra spicata L . samples were dried, and ground. After adding 200 mL of ethanol (EtOH) (purity ≥ %99.9 EMSURE® ACS, ISO, Reag. Ph Eur) to 20 g of plant sample, it was mixed with a magnetic stirrer for 24 h at 25℃. The supernatant portion was distinguished by employing strainer paper. The solvent was then removed in the evaporator (Heidolph rotary evaporator) at 25 °C at a pressure of 1 atm. [ 32 , 33 ].


## Determination of the antimicrobial activity of the plant

The bacterial strains used in the experiments were obtained from Klebsiella aerogenes American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) 13,048 , Escherichia coli ATCC 11229, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 13883 , Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, Candida albicans ATCC 10231 Microbiologics. The disc Diffusion method was applied to determine antimicrobial activity. Inhibition zone diameters (mm) for Klebsiella aerogenes, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, Candida albicans bacteria were measured after 24 h of incubation at 37 °C. Each test was performed with 3 repetitions at different times, and the mean values were determined as MIC values. Amikacin (AK-30 μg) antibiotic discs were used as positive controls [ 34 , 35 ]. In antimicrobial assays, solutions of dried ethanol extract of Thymbra spicata prepared with distilled water at a concentration of 500 µg/mL were utilized.


## Antioxidant activity of Thymbra spicata

In antioxidant analysis, a stock solution of Thymbra spicata ethanol extract was prepared at a concentration of 1 mg/mL and used. DPPH radical scavenging activity of the Thymbra spicata ethanol extract was detected by modifying the method of Blois (1958). For this technique, 250 µM DPPH solution was generated by utilizing EtOH Trolox solutions and Thymbra spicata solutions made at various dosages (20, 40, and 80 µg/mL) were mixed with 3 mL of DPPH radical solution. Following a half-hour incubation period, absorbances at 517 nm were measured.

ABTS • + scavenging activity of the Thymbra spicata ethanol extract was detected by modifying the method of Re et al. (1999). To produce the ABTS •+ radical solution, 2.45 M potassium persulfate solution and 2 mM ABTS solution were combined. At doses of 20, 40, and 80 µg/mL, Thymbra spicata solutions and Trolox solutions were produced, and 4 mL of ABTS •+ solution was added. Absorbances at 734 nm were determined utilizing a spectrophotometer following a 30-min incubation duration.

The cupric ion (Cu 2+ ) reduction capacities of the Thymbra spicata ethanol extract was detected by modifying the method of Apak et al. (2007). For this test, 0.01 M, 0.5 mL CuCl 2 solution, 0.5 mL 7.5 × 10 −3 M neocuprin solution, and 1 M ammonium acetate buffer were combined. Following the solution was mixed, various quantities of Trolox solutions and plant extracts were added. Their absorbance at 450 nm was detected following a half-hour incubation duration. A greater absorbance signifies a greater cupric ion (Cu 2+) reduction ability [ 36 – 38 ].


## Volatile Compounds (VC) analyses with GC–MS

Volatile compounds were analyzed using a Shimadzu QP2020 GC–MS system (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan), following the method described by Korkmaz et al. (2020, 2023). A 2 g sample was placed in a 20 mL SPME screw-cap vial (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) and immediately sealed with a polytetrafluoroethylene/silicone septum (Supelco). The sample was incubated at 40 °C for 30 min with continuous agitation at 50 rpm. A solid-phase microextraction (SPME) fiber (2.2 cm, DVB/CAR/PDMS coating, 50/30 μm; Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) was inserted into the vial and exposed to the headspace for 30 min under the same temperature and agitation conditions. After adsorption, the fiber was withdrawn and immediately introduced into the GC–MS injection port for thermal desorption at 250 °C for 5 min. Volatile compounds were separated using a DB-Heavy Wax column (60 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm film thickness; Agilent J&W Scientific, Folsom, CA, USA). The oven temperature was initially set at 40 °C, increased at a rate of 3 °C/min to 80 °C and held for 1 min, then increased by 5 °C/min to 240 °C, and held for 6 min. The injection temperature was 250 °C, and helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.05 mL/min. Mass spectrometry was performed in scan mode with an m/z range of 20–450. The ion source temperature was 201 °C, and electron ionization was conducted at 70 eV. Compound identification was based on comparison of mass spectra with the Wiley 9 mass spectral library. Furthermore, the identification of the compounds was confirmed by comparing the obtained Retention Indices (RI) values with those reported in the literature (NIST Chemistry WebBook database). The calculation of RIs, n-alkane (C10-C26), was used under the same chromatographic conditions [ 39 , 40 ].


## Phenolic compound analysis with liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) device

The phenolic compound content of the plant ethanol extract was analyzed with the LC-HRMS device at Bingöl University Application and Research Laboratory Center (Bingöl, Türkiye). LC-HRMS analyses were performed using the LC system, including the DIONEX UltiMate 3000 RS pump, DIONEX UltiMate 3000 RS autosampler and DIONEX UltiMate 3000 RS column oven, and Exactive Plus Orbitrap (ThermoFisherScientific) high-resolution MS composition with heated electrospray ionization interface. The Orbitrap-MS device was calibrated with positive (Pierce LTQ Velos ESI PositiveIon Calibration Solution) and negative calibration (Pierce™™ Negative Ion Calibration Solution) solutions using an auto-syringe injector (ThermoFisherScientific, USA). In the LC-HRMS analysis, the LC and MS parts were run simultaneously with the TraceFinder 3.2 (ThermoScientific) program installed on the system computer, and the data were collected and recorded with the Xcalibur software version 2.1.0.1140 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) program. A MerckPurosper® STAR RP-18 endcapped Hibar® HR model UHPLC (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 3 μm) column was used for the analyses. The column furnace temperature was operated at 30 °C. The column furnace temperature was operated at 30 °C. 0.5% (v/v) glacial acetic acid prepared in ultrapure water obtained by Ultrapure water system (GFL 2004/Human Power 1) in the mobile A phase and 99.9% pure LC–MS grade MeOH (Sigma) was used in the mobile B phase. Separation was performed according to the gradient elution conditions indicated in Figure at a sample injection volume of 20.0 μL and a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The analysis time was set to a total of 20 min. The analysis time was set to a total of 20 min. Equipped with a heated electrospray ionization interface, the Orbitrap HRMS was operated in both positive (Full MS/AIF) and negative (Full MS/AIF) mode. The ionization interface sheath gas (sheathgas) flow rate is 35; auxiliary gas (auxiliarygas) flow rate is 7; spray voltage is 3.5 kV; capillary temperature is 350 °C; auxiliary gas temperature is 350 °C; and The S-lens RF level is set to 50. Liquid samples were filtered through a PTFE syringe filter with a pore size of 0.22 µm and a diameter of 25 mm with the help of a 10 mL syringe and taken into a 15 mL falcon tube. 100 μL of 1.5 mL vial was taken from the filtrate, and a water mixture containing 900 µL 10% MeOH and 0.5% acetic acid was added to it, it was shaken well with the vial vortex and injected into the device. The concentration of the standards for phytochemical compounds belonging to the LC-Orbitrap HRMS analysis method was 10–20-40–60-80–100-200–400-600–800-1000 ppb (μg/kg) and each of them was injected in three repeated ways [ 41 , 42 ] (Figure S5).

## Molecular docking method

The (Schrödinger) Maestro program, which is well-known for its accuracy and ease of use and focuses on drug discovery, molecular dynamics, and quantum chemistry, was utilized for this procedure. The proteins were liberated from water molecules and molecules not present in the protein structure in order to prepare them for docking. Polar hydrogen atoms were charged to control the distribution of electric charges [ 48 – 50 ].


## DFT calculations

The Gaussian 09 software package and GaussView 5.0 software were used to carry out geometry optimization techniques using density functional theory-based quantum chemistry computations (B3LYP with a 6–311 + + G(d,p) basis set). The energy gap (ΔE) between the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) and the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) was calculated as part of the computations. In addition, evaluations were conducted for factors including nucleophilicity (Nu), softness (s), electrophilicity (ω), electronegativity (χ), and hardness (η). These quantum chemical techniques were also used to compute the dipole moment (μ) and Mulliken charges on the backbone atoms. The formulas used in previous studies were used in the calculations [ 35 , 51 – 55 ].


## Statistical analysis

All measurements were carried out in triplicate and the results were presented as mean values ± SD (standard deviations). For calculations, off-the-shelf statistical software (IBM SPSS Statistics 22, SPSS Inc., an IBM Co., Somers, NY) was used.


## Results and discussion

In this study, the anti-inflammatory properties of compounds such as rhoifolin, leucoside, naringenin, rosmarinic acid, ɣ -terpinene and carvacrol were investigated, and their potential effects on inflammation-related diseases were evaluated. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as diclofenac and ketoprofen were used for comparison due to their inflammation-reducing and analgesic effects. The pharmacological activities of Rhoifolin, leucoside, naringenin, rosmarinic acid, diclofenac, and ketoprofen were calculated, and their ADMET profiles were compared with DFT calculations, biological targets, and activities. Additionally, molecular interactions between these compounds and key inflammation-related structures such as TNF-α, IL-6, and NLRP3 were examined through molecular docking studies. These calculations and comparisons provide original data as they are the first of their kind in the field. Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted methods have been developed for drug discovery processes, particularly for identifying drug targets and pharmacokinetic analyses. This process is complex, consisting of multiple stages and having a generally low success rate. In recent years, information technology-focused disciplines such as bioinformatics, systems biology, cheminformatics, and molecular simulation have provided significant advantages in drug development processes, leading to notable advancements in these areas [ 56 , 57 ].

Gedikoğlu et al. (2019) investigated the antimicrobial activity of EtOH and MeOH extracts of T. spicata as well as the oils of T. spicata obtained by microwave-assisted extraction and hydrodistillation method on Bacillus cereus , Staphylococcus aureus , Staphylococcus epidermidis , Escherichia coli , Salmonella enteritidis , Salmonella typhimurium microorganisms. It has been stated that MeOH and EtOH extracts of this plant do not display antimicrobial activity, but its oils have antimicrobial efficacy on these microorganisms [ 58 ]. In another study by Sengun et al. (2020), aqueous extract of Thymbra spicata (88.75% carvacrol) harvested from Saricakaya/Eskisehir was found to have a stronger inhibition activity, particularly against Staphylococcus aureus . It was stated that the oil of Thymbra spicata displayed more effective antimicrobial activity than its extract on Bacillus subtilis , Escherichia coli , Enterococcus faecalis , Listeria monocytogenes , Staphylococcus aureus and Salmonella Typhimurium and that Thymbra spicata is a candidate for use in pharmaceutical applications and food products as a natural antioxidant and antimicrobial agent [ 59 ] It was found that the EO obtained from Thymbra spicata collected from Gaziantep has a lethal activity on Staphylococcus aureus , Enterococcus hirae , Enterococcus faecalis , Listeria monocytogenes , Escherichia coli , Klebsiella pneumoniae , Proteus hauseri , Pseudomonas aeruginosa , Salmonella typhimurium and Candida albicans pathogens even at low doses [ 60 ]. In this study, it was detected that Thymbra spicata ethanol extract displayed 58.33% activity on K. pneumoniae , 62.42% on E. coli , 67.19% on C. albicans , 86.67% on K. aerogenes , and 61.18% on S. aureus compared to Amikacin (30 µg) antibiotic. It demonstrated the most powerful activity on K. aerogenes bacteria. These studies'different levels of antimicrobial activity might be caused by a variety of factors, including the plant's growth environment, the solvent's polarity and purity, the sample's storage conditions, and the extraction procedures.

In this study, Thymbra spicata L. extracts prepared entirely with ethanol showed 10.75 mm inhibition on C. albicans and 10.25 mm on E.coli at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. It also exhibited the greatest inhibition effect on K. aerogenes, while it exhibited the lowest inhibition effect on S. aureus. The difference in the amount and content of phenolic and volatile compounds in the content of our plant sample may have been effective in these results (Table 3 and Fig. 1 ).

Since the hERG (human ether-à-go-go related gene) potassium channel produces the repolarizing current, which constitutes an important component of the cardiac action potential, blocking this current with small molecule drugs can lead to life-threatening cardiotoxicity. Prediction of hERG toxicity at an early stage is of great importance, especially since drug-induced arrhythmia poses a significant challenge in drug development [ 91 , 92 ]. Drug-Induced Liver Injury (DILI) represents hepatic damage attributed to the exposure to drugs (anti-tuberculosis drugs, antiepileptic drugs, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, anti-neoplastic drugs, immunomodulators, dietary supplements, and several traditional Chinese medicines) or to any naturally occurring chemical compound. DILI can be categorized as hepatocellular, cholestatic, or mixed, and may also include immune responses [ 93 – 95 ].

When the hERG blockers rhoifolin, leucoside, naringenin, rosmarinic acid, diclofenac and ketoprofen were examined, it was found that rhoifolin: 0.014, leucoside: 0.011, naringenin: 0.107, rosmarinic acid: 0.056, ɣ -terpinene: 0.037, carvacrol: 0.106, diclofenac: 0.069, ketoprofen: 0.142, respectively (Table 12 ). The hERG channel is an important ion channel in the cells of the clap and is involved in heart contractions and plays a critical role in regulating electrical activity in the heart. Blockade on the hERG channel, which means inhibition of this channel, can trigger heart arrhythmias and prolongation of the QT interval, which can lead to potentially life-threatening conditions. During drug development, hERG blockade values should be taken into account. Since all compounds were evaluated in the hERG- category (low inhibition), all compounds can be interpreted as having a low risk of blocking the hERG channel, with leucoside and rhoifolin in particular showing the lowest probabilities.

Molecular docking studies can also use DFT findings to estimate the binding affinity of molecules with free radicals. The resulting binding energy can be used as a measure of antioxidant activity. A high Eg value for a molecule indicates that the molecule is more stable and less reactive, while a low Eg indicates higher reactivity [ 106 , 107 ]. Compounds with the lowest IP values ​​are diclofenac, ɣ-terpinene, and rosmarinic acid. It is known that compounds with low IP have beneficial antioxidant activity because they can more easily donate electrons to neutralize free radicals [ 108 ].

Among the compounds, ɣ-terpinene has the largest Eg value, while the molecule with the lowest Eg value is rosmarinic acid. It indicates that rosmarinic acid is more reactive than the others, while ɣ-terpinene is more stable and less reactive. ɣ-terpinene has the highest Eg value despite having a low IP value, making it more stable. In addition, rosmarinic acid has a polar structure with a moderate dipole moment, suggesting that it may be effective in water-based biological systems. DFT data suggest that rosmarinic acid may have significant antioxidant properties among natural compounds.

## Conclusion

Thymbra spicata L. has significant therapeutic potential due to its rich phenolic and flavonoid components. Some of the most important compounds are rosmarinic acid, naringenin, carvacrol, and leucoside. All of them showed antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-microbial properties. These compounds show significant effects on inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α, IL-6, and NLRP3, and show potential efficacy in the treatment of inflammatory and autoinflammatory diseases. Their pharmacokinetic properties are generally acceptable; however, due to the risk of toxicity associated with certain compounds (e.g., ɣ-terpinene and carvacrol), meticulous dose modifications and special carrier systems are required. For compounds that do not work well with the body, new methods such as nanoformulation and enzymatic modification can be proposed. Molecular docking and DFT analyses suggest that these compounds may play an important role in drug design. However, extensive preclinical and clinical studies are required to translate this potential into concrete treatments. In summary, substances obtained from Thymbra spicata L. may serve as natural and effective candidates in modern drug development methodologies. Interdisciplinary methodologies and experimental validation are essential for the effectiveness of this process.
