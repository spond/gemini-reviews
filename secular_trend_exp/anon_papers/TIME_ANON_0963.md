## Abstract

Medicago is an economically important forage genus widely distributed across China, yet its mitochondrial genomes remain poorly understood. Comprehensive characterization of mitochondrial genome structure, function, and evolution is crucial for elucidating plant biological mechanisms, improving germplasm utilization, and advancing molecular breeding.

We assembled and compared the mitochondrial genomes of eight Medicago species, including six newly sequenced genomes. Five species contained typical single circular mitochondrial genomes, whereas M. falcata , M. platycarpos , and M. sativa displayed complex multipartite circular conformations. Genome sizes ranged from 281,240 to 356,577 bp, with 55–74 functional genes. Repeat analysis identified 141 simple sequence repeats (SSRs) and 76 tandem repeats (TSRs), largely composed of A/T-rich mononucleotide motifs, while dispersed repeats were mainly 30–49 bp in length. Codon usage showed a strong A/T bias and a preference for leucine, serine, and isoleucine. RNA editing events were predominantly C-to-U substitutions, primarily occurring at the first and second codon positions. Phylogenetic reconstruction based on 31 shared mitochondrial protein-coding genes (PCGs) strongly supported the monophyly of Medicago and recovered interspecific relationships consistent with previous chloroplast and nuclear genome studies. Most PCGs were under purifying selection, while a few, such as matR , exhibited signatures of positive selection, suggesting lineage-specific adaptive evolution.

This study expands the mitochondrial genomic resources of Medicago and provides new insights into its structural evolution and phylogenetic relationships, offering a valuable foundation for evolutionary and functional studies in Fabaceae.

## Background

Medicago , commonly known as alfalfa, belongs to the Fabaceae family and includes annual or perennial herbaceous plants. It originated in the Mediterranean region and Southwest Asia and is mainly distributed in northern, northwestern, and northeastern China. Alfalfa is rich in proteins, vitamins, minerals, polysaccharides, soy isoflavones, flavonoids, and multiple unidentified growth-promoting factors (UGF), earning it the title of “King of Forages.” It serves as a high-quality forage for ruminants such as dairy cattle and can enhance livestock and poultry production performance, immunity, and meat quality, while also modulating gut microbiota [ 1 , 2 ]. Currently, the total cultivation area of alfalfa in China exceeds 10 million mu, with an annual yield surpassing 2 million tons. Its hay and derived products are widely applied in feed, medicine, bioenergy, and ecological restoration due to their ease of storage and transportation. Alfalfa cultivation in China can be traced back to the Han Dynasty, as recorded in 《Records of the Grand Historian·Biography of Dayuan》, which notes that “horses favor alfalfa,” indicating its early integration into agricultural and pastoral systems. Owing to self-incompatibility and inter-subspecies cross-compatibility, alfalfa exhibits rich genetic diversity and strong ecological adaptability [ 3 ]. Overall, alfalfa holds a crucial role in agriculture, animal husbandry, and ecosystems, while systematic classification, germplasm exploration, and molecular breeding remain central to current Medicago research.

Plant mitochondrial genomes (mitogenomes) are independent of the nuclear genome and are typically maternally inherited, although paternal or biparental inheritance can also occur, giving them semi-autonomous genetic characteristics [ 4 ]. As essential energy organelles in eukaryotic plant cells, mitochondria play central roles in oxidative phosphorylation, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, fatty acid metabolism, iron-sulfur cluster assembly, and calcium homeostasis, providing the energy and metabolic regulation required for plant growth, development, reproduction, and stress responses [ 5 – 7 ]. In addition, mitochondria are deeply involved in programmed cell death, autophagy, signal transduction, and redox homeostasis, and they play critical roles in pathogen infection, stress adaptation, and cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS) [ 8 – 11 ]. Their origin can be traced back approximately 1.5 billion years to an endosymbiotic event involving an α-proteobacterium, followed by frequent gene transfer, integration, and loss during plant evolution [ 12 ]. Compared with chloroplasts and animal mitochondria, plant mitogenomes exhibit greater plasticity and complexity in structural organization, genome size, repeat sequences, and incorporation of foreign DNA [ 13 ]. Their gene content is relatively conserved, typically including 24 core genes and approximately 17 variable genes [ 14 ], with a total of about 19–41 protein-coding genes (PCGs), as well as 3 rRNA and a variable number of tRNA genes [ 15 – 17 ], making them important models for studying plant adaptive evolution and organelle interactions.

Although plant mitochondrial genomes exhibit extremely low nucleotide mutation rates [ 18 ], their structures are highly plastic, frequently undergoing rearrangements and commonly exhibiting C-to-U RNA editing. These events not only increase the diversity of protein translation but are also closely associated with cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS) and environmental adaptation [ 14 , 19 , 20 ]. Mitochondrial genomes display diverse structural types, including single circular, multi-circular, linear, branched, and even multi-chromosomal forms, with multiple conformations often coexisting within the same species [ 12 ]. Genome sizes vary widely, ranging from 66 kb in Viscum scurruloideum to over 18 Mb in Cathaya argyrophylla , a difference of more than 200-fold [ 21 , 22 ], primarily due to repeat amplification, foreign DNA integration, and frequent recombination [ 23 ], which increase heterogeneity and affect ATP production, CMS expression, and adaptive evolution [ 24 ]. In recent years, advances in long-read sequencing platforms such as PacBio HiFi and ONT, together with assembly tools including Mitofiner, GSAT, and PMAT, have significantly advanced plant mitogenome research [ 25 – 27 ]. Recent findings indicate that the true configurations of plant mitochondrial genomes are highly diverse, rather than the traditionally assumed single circular model [ 28 , 29 ]. Comparative analyses provide insights into plant phylogeny and evolution and offer valuable references for species identification, agronomic trait improvement, and molecular breeding [ 30 , 31 ]. Therefore, in-depth characterization of plant mitochondrial genome structure, function, and evolution is essential for understanding fundamental plant life processes, elucidating mechanisms of adaptive evolution, and promoting the utilization of superior germplasm resources.

Recently, although the mitochondrial genomes of several plant genera have been characterized [ 32 – 35 ], research on Medicago has largely been limited to individual species or specific populations, lacking systematic intra-genus comparative analyses [ 36 , 37 ]. In this study, we used the PacBio HiFi long-read sequencing platform to perform de novo assembly and annotation of the mitochondrial genomes of six Medicago species. Combined with two previously published genomes, we constructed a comparative dataset covering eight species to systematically analyze gene content, repeat sequences, relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU), RNA editing, collinearity, and selection pressure. Based on 31 shared protein-coding genes, we also reconstructed the phylogenetic relationships within Fabaceae. This study provides a new theoretical foundation for Medicago systematics, germplasm utilization, and organelle genome evolution research.


## Results


## Characteristics of the mitochondrial genomes of eight Medicago species

To comprehensively resolve the structural features and evolutionary divergence of Medicago mitogenomes, we performed a comparative analysis of eight Medicago species. Six of these mitogenomes were newly assembled de novo and functionally annotated (Fig. 1 A–F). Structurally, M. lupulina , M. minima , M. edgeworthii , M. truncatula , and M. arabica each exhibit a single circular molecule, whereas M. falcata , M. platycarpos , and M. sativa display complex, multichromosomal circular assemblies (Fig. 1 A–F). Genome sizes range from 281,240 to 356,577 bp, a ~ 1.27-fold difference (Table S3). GC content is similar across species (44.92% to 45.58%), consistent with typical angiosperm mitogenomes.

Functional annotation identified 55–74 genes per genome, including 33–41 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 18–24 rRNA genes, and 3–5 tRNA genes (Tables S3–S5; Figures S1–S6). The PCG complement was largely conserved, except in M. falcata , which showed a marked increase in gene copy number. For example, sdh4 occurs as a single copy in M. edgeworthii , M. platycarpos , and M. arabica , but is multicopy in the remaining species. Likewise, atp1 , atp4 , and nad4L are present in multiple copies in both M. falcata and M. arabica , while multicopy ccmB , ccmFN , and cox3 are unique to M. falcata . Notably, rps1 was detected only in M. truncatula and absent from the other seven species (Fig. 1 G).

We also observed variation in rRNA and tRNA gene copy number and presence/absence. For instance, rrn26 is multicopy in M. falcata . trnQ-UUG is present in multiple copies across all eight species. trnF-GAA occurs as a single copy only in M. arabica but as multiple copies in the other species. trnfM-CAU is multicopy in M. falcata , M. platycarpos , and M. sativa ; trnM-CAU is multicopy in M. lupulina and M. arabica ; and multicopy trnN-GUU and trnY-GUA were observed exclusively in M. falcata . These patterns likely reflect structural duplications and may indicate functional redundancy within certain Medicago mitogenomes (Fig. 1 G).

SSRs, composed of 1–6 nucleotide units, are short repeats commonly found in plant organelle genomes and can promote genome rearrangement and length expansion [ 38 ]. We identified 141 SSRs across the eight mitogenomes, with 11–23 SSRs per species (Fig. 2 B, Table S6). Mononucleotide repeats were the most frequent, accounting for 69.57%–95.2% of SSRs, followed by dinucleotide and trinucleotide repeats (4.8%–30.43%). No tetra-, penta- or hexanucleotide SSRs were detected. Among mononucleotide SSRs, A/T motifs predominated (~ 92.9%), while C/G motifs were scarce (~ 7.1%) and absent in M. falcata , M. edgeworthii , and M. sativa . For dinucleotide SSRs, (AG/CT or AT/TA) motifs were most common (4.76%–21.75%), although (AT/TA) repeats were absent in M. falcata and M. edgeworthii . Trinucleotide repeats occurred in all species except M. edgeworthii . Overall, SSR type frequencies differed among species but the genomic distribution patterns were broadly consistent. SSRs were generally evenly distributed, although M. minima and M. arabica showed regional clustering, suggesting localized enrichment or selective retention (Fig. 2 A).

TSRs are tandem repeats with unit lengths > 6 bp [ 39 ]. We detected 76 TSRs in total, with 4–18 TSRs per species. Unit lengths ranged from 6 to 33 bp, and all matches exceeded 77% similarity. M. arabica contained a notably higher TSR count than other species, which may reflect enhanced structural duplication or recombination activity (Fig. 2 C, Table S7).

DSRs (dispersed repeats) can mediate recombination and genome rearrangement and are widespread in plant mitochondria [ 40 ]. We observed forward (F) and palindromic (P) repeats in most species; reverse (R) repeats were detected only once in M. falcata , and complementary (C) repeats were absent across all species (Fig. 2 C, Table S7). In M. arabica , only 126 F-type repeats were found and no P-type repeats were detected. In the remaining species, F- and P-type counts ranged from 64 to 138 and 46–135, respectively (Fig. 2 C, Table S8). Although DSR counts varied among species, length distributions were similar: most repeats fell in the 30–49 bp class (~ 73.56%), followed by 50–99 bp (~ 17.26%). Notably, M. falcata harbored substantially more DSRs > 500 bp than the other species, which may partly explain its larger mitochondrial genome size (Figure S7).


## Analysis of codon usage in Medicago

To investigate codon usage patterns and preferences in mitochondrial PCGs of Medicago species, we conducted a comprehensive analysis using the mitochondrial genomes of eight species (Fig. 3 , Table S9). The total number of codons in PCGs ranged from 9,639 to 11,515 across species, covering all 64 codons corresponding to 20 standard amino acids and stop codons. Overall, leucine (Leu) was the most frequently used amino acid in most species, followed by serine (Ser) and isoleucine (Ile), whereas tryptophan (Trp) and cysteine (Cys) were the least frequently used. As expected, most amino acids were encoded by multiple synonymous codons; for example, arginine (Arg), leucine (Leu), and serine (Ser) each had six synonymous codons, while methionine (Met) and tryptophan (Trp) were each encoded by a single codon (ATG and TGG, respectively).

Analysis of relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values for all 64 codons revealed that approximately 75% had RSCU values greater than 1 in multiple species, indicating a marked usage preference. The remaining ~ 25% of codons showed RSCU values less than 1, suggesting that their usage frequencies were significantly lower than expected under equal codon usage. Certain codons, such as Asp (GAC) and Leu (CTC, CTG), had RSCU values close to 1, consistent with a “no preference” pattern. The overall RSCU profiles were highly consistent among Medicago species. In particular, Phe (TTT) consistently displayed the highest RSCU value across all species, suggesting that such codons may be influenced by similar selective pressures or mutational biases.

Furthermore, analysis of GC content showed that the third codon positions of all species had relatively low GC levels, averaging between 35.88% and 37.66%. This indicates a strong A/T bias in the mitochondrial genomes of Medicago . Collectively, these results demonstrate that codon usage patterns of mitochondrial PCGs in Medicago species are highly conserved and strongly biased toward A/T, implying that natural selection plays an important role in shaping codon usage evolution in this genus.


## Prediction of RNA editing sites in Medicago

To gain deeper insights into the post-transcriptional regulation of mitochondrial PCGs, we analyzed RNA editing sites and their characteristics in the mitochondrial genomes of eight Medicago species. Widespread C-to-U RNA editing events were identified across PCGs in all species, along with a few U-to-C events. The total number of editing sites per species ranged from 452 to 568, showing limited variation, which indicates that RNA editing is a conserved regulatory mechanism in higher plant mitochondria (Fig. 4 A, Table S10).

Among these sites, 92.53% to 95.35% were cytidine-to-uridine (C-to-U) substitutions. Analysis of codon position distribution showed that more than 50% of editing events occurred at the second codon position (59.40%–67.77%), followed by the first position (32.23%–40.60%). No editing events were detected at the third codon position (Fig. 4 B, Table S11).

The number and distribution of editing sites varied considerably among genes. Genes involved in the electron transport chain, cytochrome biosynthesis, and membrane transport—such as nad4 and ccmB —contained the highest numbers of editing sites (46–89). In contrast, ribosomal protein-coding genes such as rpsI ( rpsI16 , rpsI5 ) had relatively few editing sites (12–14). Frequent amino acid conversions included serine (Ser) to phenylalanine (Phe), leucine (Leu), or proline (Pro); arginine (Arg) to cysteine (Cys) or tryptophan (Trp); and proline (Pro) to leucine (Leu) or phenylalanine (Phe). Most of these changes involved transitions from hydrophilic to hydrophobic residues, which may enhance protein structural stability (Fig. 4 C, Table S12).

Additionally, editing events associated with stop codons were detected in genes such as atp6 , atp9 , and ccmFC , suggesting that RNA editing may play a regulatory role in translation initiation and termination.


## Phylogenetic relationship among the Medicago species

To clarify the mitochondrial genome–based phylogenetic relationships of Medicago species within Fabaceae, we performed phylogenetic analyses using the complete protein-coding gene (PCG) sequences from 25 Fabaceae species. A total of 31 shared PCGs (including atp , cox , cob , nad , and rps genes) were extracted, and phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using both the maximum likelihood (ML) method (Fig. 5 A) and Bayesian inference (BI) method (Fig. 5 B).

The two methods yielded highly consistent topologies, with most nodes showing strong support (Bootstrap ≥ 90%, Posterior Probability ≥ 0.95), confirming that the selected gene set carried robust phylogenetic signals. The trees revealed clear clustering patterns, with closely related taxa grouping into the same clades, reflecting strong phylogenetic concordance. Within Medicago , all species clustered into a single branch, indicating close evolutionary relationships.

At higher resolution, M. minima and M. lupulina were identified as sister taxa and, together with M. truncatula , formed a well-supported subclade. M. falcata and M. sativa also formed a sister group, whereas M. platycarpos , M. edgeworthii , and M. arabica occupied earlier-diverging positions, suggesting possible differences in evolutionary rates or selective pressures among these species.

These relationships were broadly consistent with those inferred from chloroplast genome analyses of Medicago and aligned with the overall framework of the current angiosperm classification system (e.g., APG-IV) [ 41 ]. This consistency underscores the reliability of phylogenetic reconstruction based on conserved mitochondrial PCGs. However, some species, such as M. truncatula , showed relatively low support values, indicating uncertain placement. This instability may be linked to the structural complexity of its mitochondrial genome or incomplete gene annotation and warrants further investigation. Overall, the well-supported phylogenetic framework established here provides new mitochondrial evidence for elucidating evolutionary relationships within Fabaceae and offers valuable resources for taxonomic studies and investigations of species origins and diversification in this family.


## Synteny analysis of mitochondrial sequences of Medicago species

To investigate the structural evolutionary features of mitochondrial genomes in Medicago , we performed a comprehensive collinearity analysis of eight representative species. The results showed that although these species are closely related phylogenetically, their mitochondrial genomes exhibit notable structural variation in the arrangement, length, and orientation of homologous blocks. Using BLASTn comparisons, we identified 23–44 homologous collinear segments longer than 400 bp between each genome pair (Fig. 6 ). These included both forward and reverse complementary blocks, which were broadly distributed across the circular mitochondrial chromosomes.

For example, between M. falcata and M. sativa , 24 collinear blocks were detected, with the longest segment spanning 74,236 bp and showing > 95% sequence similarity, reflecting strong structural conservation (Table S13). In summary, while Medicago mitochondrial genomes maintain high sequence homology, they also display substantial structural rearrangement and diversity. These differences are likely driven by recombination events mediated by repetitive sequences during species divergence. Our findings provide important insights into the structural evolution of Medicago mitochondrial genomes and their phylogenetic relationships.

Most PCGs showed Ka/Ks ratios well below 1, indicating pervasive purifying selection and strong functional conservation (Fig. 7 C). Genes such as atp1 , nad1 , nad4L , nad5 , nad6 , nad7 , and rps7 had Ka/Ks values approaching zero. These genes are primarily involved in energy metabolism, electron transport, and ribosomal function, underscoring their essential roles in core mitochondrial processes.

However, several genes exhibited Ka/Ks > 1 in specific pairwise comparisons, suggesting episodic positive selection (Table S14). Notably, cox2 , matR , and rpl16 showed elevated Ka/Ks ratios in comparisons involving M. arabica , M. edgeworthii , M. falcata , and M. truncatula . In one comparison, matR reached a Ka/Ks as high as 3.34, implying a possible role in lineage-specific adaptation.

To summarize, mitochondrial PCGs in Medicago are largely conserved under purifying selection, while a subset of genes displays signatures of positive selection in particular lineages. These results provide a theoretical basis for detecting candidate genes with accelerated evolution and guide subsequent functional validation.


## Discussion

Mitochondria, commonly known as the “powerhouses” of the cell, are essential for plant growth and development through their stable activity. Compared with those of animals and fungi, plant mitochondrial genomes are far more complex in both structure and regulatory mechanisms. They display pronounced variation in genome size, diverse gene content, abundant repetitive elements, frequent intron insertions, and highly dynamic genome conformations [ 42 , 43 ].

Specifically, we systematically characterized the mitochondrial genomes of eight representative Medicago species using high-fidelity (PacBio HiFi) long-read sequencing data, with six reported for the first time. Compared with other Fabaceae species such as Glycine soja (402,545 bp) [ 44 ] and Vicia faba (588,000 bp) [ 45 ], the mitochondrial genome size of Medicago ranged from 281,240 to 356,577 bp, indicating relatively limited expansion during evolution and representing a compact genome size within Fabaceae. In terms of gene content, several species (e.g., M. falcata ) exhibited increased copy numbers of several protein-coding genes (PCGs), including, atp4 , ccmB , ccmFN , cox3 , and n ad4L , whereas the rps1 gene was annotated only in M. truncatula and absent in all other species. This difference may result from (i) functional transfer to the nuclear genome followed by compensation, or (ii) the inherent plasticity and frequent recombination of plant mitochondrial genomes (Fig. 1 ). Similar patterns of gene duplication and loss have been reported in Camellia duntsa [ 46 ] and Mangifera [ 34 ], suggesting that dynamic changes in mitochondrial gene copy number are widespread across Fabaceae and other angiosperms, potentially contributing to gene regulation and adaptive evolution. Overall, the GC content of Medicago mitochondrial genomes remained stable (44.92%–45.58%), comparable to that of common bean ( Phaseolus vulgaris , 45.11%) [ 47 ] and bitter melon ( Momordica charantia , 45.60%) [ 5 ], reflecting conserved features associated with structural stability and ecological adaptation [ 48 ].

Comparative analyses across plant lineages have shown substantial differences in mitochondrial genome conformations: some species retain the typical single circular structure (e.g., Cinnamomum chekiangense , Salix truncatum ) [ 49 , 50 ], while others exhibit more complex structural diversity (e.g., okra Abelmoschus esculentus harbors two structural types; Populus simonii possesses three or more isoforms) [ 51 ]. Within this context, Medicago species also exhibit pronounced structural variation, with M. lupulina and M. minima showing a single circular form, whereas M. falcata and M. sativa display multiple circular configurations. Such isoform diversity parallels reports from Michelia [ 52 ], Punica [ 53 ], and Astragalus membranaceus within Fabaceae [ 54 ], highlighting the high plasticity of angiosperm mitochondrial genomes during evolution [ 55 ]. Notably, HiFi sequencing provides clear and robust evidence for resolving these complex conformations.

Repeat sequence analysis further indicates that Medicago mitochondrial genomes are enriched in diverse repetitive elements, including AT-rich SSRs, short repeats (< 200 bp), and large inverted repeats (> 500 bp). These elements likely promote genome rearrangements and structural evolution through homologous recombination (Fig. 2 ) [ 56 ]. Similar repeat features have been observed in Broussonetia [ 35 ] and Camellia [ 46 ], reinforcing the widespread role of repeat-mediated structural variation in plant mitochondrial lineages.

When placed in a broader Fabaceae context, Medicago mitogenomes are markedly smaller than those of Vicia faba (~ 588 kb) [ 45 ] and Cicer arietinum (~ 403 kb) [ 57 ], slightly smaller than Glycine soja (~ 402 kb) [ 44 ], and similar to Lotus japonicus (~ 380 kb) [ 58 ], suggesting relatively limited genome expansion during evolution. This compact genome size may be associated with their predominantly annual or short-lived life history, reflecting an energy-efficient and structurally stable evolutionary strategy. In contrast, Medicago shows highly dynamic local structural features, including copy number expansions of several PCGs in M. falcata and the presence of multiple isoforms in M. falcata and M. sativa , indicating strong structural plasticity and frequent recombination. By comparison, Vicia and Glycine possess larger mitogenomes but have fewer reported copy number variations or isoform types, suggesting more static structural organizations. Moreover, Medicago appears to have lower repeat content than Vicia and Cicer , which may reduce the frequency of large-scale recombination and foreign DNA insertions, thereby helping maintain genome compactness. Collectively, these observations suggest that Medicago follows a distinctive “small but structurally dynamic” evolutionary trajectory within Fabaceae, potentially balancing genome stability and adaptive flexibility.

Intron analysis shows that Medicago mitochondrial genomes retain numerous intron-containing PCGs (e.g., nad1 , nad2 , nad5 , nad7 ), with numbers and distribution patterns consistent with those reported in Cicer and Glycine within Fabaceae [ 44 , 59 ]. These introns are predominantly cis-spliced and structurally conserved, suggesting strong selective constraints and underscoring their value for plant systematics and population genetics [ 30 , 60 ]. Overall, Medicago mitochondrial genomes exhibit general conservation in size, composition, structure, and repeat features within Fabaceae, while showing moderate interspecific variation. These findings provide important insights into the evolutionary dynamics of legume mitochondrial genomes (Table S5).

In terms of codon usage, Medicago mitochondrial genomes exhibit a pronounced preference for synonymous codons ending with A or T. Among amino acids, leucine (Leu) showed the highest usage frequency, whereas tryptophan (Trp) and cysteine (Cys) were the least frequently used (Fig. 3 ). This codon bias closely parallels patterns observed in Mangifera [ 34 ] and Broussonetia [ 35 ], suggesting that codon usage is shaped by the combined effects of natural selection, mutational bias, and genetic drift [ 61 ]. Such preferences not only highlight potential adaptive mechanisms influencing transcriptional and translational efficiency in Medicago mitochondria but also align with codon usage trends widely conserved across angiosperm mitochondrial genomes.

RNA editing analysis revealed 452–568 potential C-to-U editing sites across mitochondrial PCGs in Medicago species, with more than 92% of edits occurring at the first or second codon positions (Fig. 4 ). These edits typically result in nonsynonymous substitutions that alter amino acid properties, most often involving a shift from hydrophilic to hydrophobic residues, which may enhance protein stability and functionality. Editing sites were particularly enriched in key metabolic genes such as nad4 and ccmB , suggesting important roles in maintaining mitochondrial respiratory function. Consistent with findings in Phaseolus vulgaris and Acer truncatum [ 47 , 62 ], the RNA editing sites in Medicago species show high conservation, underscoring the central role of this mechanism in the regulation of mitochondrial gene expression in angiosperms.

Collinearity analysis further showed that, despite the close phylogenetic relationships among Medicago species, their mitochondrial genomes retain 23–44 syntenic blocks longer than 400 bp, mainly distributed around core metabolic genes such as nad , cox , and ccm (Fig. 6 ). These conserved blocks suggest strong purifying selection and parallel the synteny patterns reported in Theaceae and Lauraceae [ 13 , 56 ], reflecting the evolutionary stability of key mitochondrial functional regions. Phylogenetic analysis based on 31 shared mitochondrial PCGs from 25 Fabaceae species produced both maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) trees, in which Medicago species formed a well-supported monophyletic clade, confirming their phylogenetic unity. Within the genus, M. minima and M. lupulina were most closely related, M. falcata and M. sativa formed a sister group, and M. platycarpos diverged earliest. This phylogenetic pattern is highly consistent with results from chloroplast genomes [ 63 , 64 ], nuclear transcriptomes [ 65 , 66 ], and the APG IV classification system, further validating the effectiveness and reliability of mitochondrial genomes in Fabaceae phylogenetic research(Fig. 5 ).

Ka/Ks ratio analysis showed that most PCGs in Medicago mitochondrial genomes had values far below 1, indicating strong purifying selection, with atp and nad genes involved in energy metabolism being particularly constrained (Fig. 7 ). This pattern aligns with the slow evolutionary rate and functional conservation observed in mitochondrial genes of Michelia and Theaceae [ 46 , 52 ]. However, several genes, including ccmB , ccmFc , ccmFn , nad3 , and nad9 , displayed Ka/Ks > 1 in certain species, suggesting potential roles in functional innovation during Medicago diversification and adaptive evolution. Notably, matR exhibited a Ka/Ks ratio as high as 3.34 in specific comparisons, indicating strong positive selection and pointing to possible lineage-specific rapid evolution linked to mitochondrial gene expression and respiratory regulation. Overall, patterns of codon usage, RNA editing, and selective pressure in Medicago are broadly consistent with those reported in other Fabaceae and angiosperms, while also revealing species-specific features that reflect adaptive evolutionary dynamics within a generally conserved framework.

While this study represents an initial comparative framework based on eight Medicago species, the currently available complete mitochondrial genomes of Fabaceae remain limited in both number and phylogenetic breadth. Given the inherent variability and structural complexity of plant mitochondrial genomes, it is still challenging to draw family-level conclusions from the relatively scarce genomic resources currently accessible for Medicago and other Fabaceae lineages. In particular, the lack of representative mitogenomes spanning diverse lineages and genome sizes constrains broader evolutionary inferences. Therefore, the findings presented here should be viewed as preliminary insights into the structural characteristics and evolutionary trends of Medicago mitochondrial genomes.

Despite these limitations, our results reveal that Medicago mitogenomes exhibit strong conservation in gene content, structural organization, codon usage, and RNA editing, while also displaying considerable diversity in gene rearrangements and signals of positive selection. Comparative analyses with Fabaceae and other angiosperms further highlight their distinct phylogenetic placement and dynamic adaptive evolution. These findings provide new perspectives on the evolutionary mechanisms shaping Medicago mitochondrial genomes and offer a valuable theoretical basis for functional gene studies, germplasm conservation, and molecular breeding. Looking forward, expanding the phylogenetic sampling across Fabaceae and integrating multi-omics data—including nuclear and transcriptomic resources—will be essential for unraveling the complex regulatory processes underlying their adaptive evolution. Such efforts will help test the generality of the patterns observed here across the family and clarify the evolutionary forces driving mitochondrial genome expansion.


## Conclusions

We assembled and compared the mitochondrial genomes of eight Medicago species, including six sequenced for the first time. Five species contained a single circular genome, whereas three exhibited multipartite configurations. Genome sizes ranged from 281,240 to 356,577 bp, encompassing 55 to 74 protein-coding genes (PCGs). Repetitive sequences were dominated by A/T-rich mononucleotide motifs. Codon usage showed a pronounced A/T bias, while RNA editing was characterized mainly by C-to-U conversions. Phylogenetic reconstruction strongly supported the monophyly of Medicago . Most genes were under purifying selection, whereas a few, such as matR , showed signatures of positive selection. These findings advance the understanding of mitochondrial genome structure and evolution in Medicago and provide a valuable foundation for future studies on Fabaceae mitochondrial genomes.


## Materials and methods


## Plant materials and cultivation

In this study, six representative species of the genus Medicago were selected, including Medicago lupulina ( M. lupulina ), Medicago falcata ( M. falcata ), Medicago minima ( M. minima ), Medicago edgeworthii ( M. edgeworthii ), Medicago platycarpos ( M. platycarpos ), and diploid Medicago sativa ( M. sativa ). Among them, seeds of M. minima were obtained from the Kunming Institute of Botany, Chinese Academy of Sciences. All seeds were thoroughly cleaned and surface-sterilized before being sown in pots filled with nutrient soil and cultivated under greenhouse conditions (14 h light/10 h dark photoperiod, temperature 25 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity of 60–70%). After 35 days of cultivation, young and healthy mature leaves were collected, immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen on-site, and then stored at − 80 °C in the laboratory for subsequent genomic DNA extraction. In addition, the mitochondrial genome sequences of Medicago truncatula ( M. truncatula ) ( NC_029641.1 ) and Medicago arabica ( M. arabica ) ( DQ662798.1 ) were downloaded from the public database ( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ ) for use in subsequent comparative genomic analyses.


## DNA extraction and sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from each sample using a modified CTAB method [ 67 ]. DNA quality was assessed by 0.75% agarose gel electrophoresis to evaluate fragment size and potential degradation. DNA purity was measured using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and DNA concentration was accurately quantified using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). For each sample, 5–10 µg of high-quality genomic DNA was selected for PacBio library construction. Library preparation was performed using the PCR-free SMRTbell method, followed by sequencing on the PacBio Revio platform. The library preparation process included DNA damage repair, end repair, adapter ligation, library purification, and quality control. Each sample yielded approximately 21~ 42.9 Gb of raw sequencing data (Table S1). The raw PacBio subreads.bam files were processed for quality control and data filtering using the official PacBio software SMRT Link (v12.0). The resulting high-quality data were used for subsequent mitochondrial genome assembly and analysis.


## Mitochondrial genome assembly and annotation

To enrich mitochondrial sequences, PacBio long reads were first aligned to the conserved mitochondrial genome sequence of the model Medicago species M. truncatula using Minimap2 (v2.28) [ 68 ]. Candidate reads with alignment lengths greater than 6,000 bp and covering core gene regions were retained. Based on these seed reads, iterative alignment and extension were performed to aggregate all highly overlapping reads (length ≥ 6 kb, identity ≥ 70%), thereby extracting the complete set of mitochondrial-associated reads for each sample.

The filtered mitochondrial reads were then subjected to de novo error correction and assembly using Flye (v2.9.1). Assembly graphs were visualized using Bandage (v0.8.1) to determine whether the mitochondrial genome exhibited a typical circular structure or a multi-branched conFigureuration.

Mitochondrial genome annotation was conducted using a combination of tools. General gene annotation was performed using the online platforms PMGA [ 69 ] and CPGview [ 70 ]. tRNA genes were annotated with tRNAscan-SE [ 71 ], and open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using the Open Reading Frame Finder ( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/ ). ORFs shorter than 102 bp or overlapping with known genes were excluded. ORFs longer than 300 bp were annotated against the NR database based on alignment results. All annotation results were manually inspected and curated to ensure accuracy. Circular genome maps were automatically generated using the IPMGA platform.

To ensure consistency in annotation formats for downstream comparative genomic analyses, the two Medicago mitochondrial genomes downloaded from the public database and the six newly assembled genomes from this study were all re-annotated using the same pipeline and software.


## Analysis of repeated sequences

To systematically characterize the types and distribution patterns of repetitive sequences in Medicago mitochondrial genomes, we identified and analyzed three major categories of repeats—simple sequence repeats (SSRs), tandem repeats (TSRs), and dispersed repeats (DSRs)—based on the final assembled circular genomes. SSRs were detected using the MISA tool (Misa-web-IPK Gatersleben, ( https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/ ) [ 38 ]. TSRs, defined as repeat units longer than 6 bp, were identified with Tandem Repeats Finder under default parameters (( https://tandem.bu.edu/trf/basic_submit ) [ 39 ]. DSRs were predicted using the REPuter program on the BiBiserv2 platform ( https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer?id=reputer_view_submission ), with parameters set to a Hamming distance of 3, a minimum repeat length of 30 bp, and a maximum repeat number of 5000 [ 40 ]. Finally, the distribution patterns of all identified repeats were visualized using the Circos module implemented in TBtools [ 72 ].


## Analysis of codon usage and prediction of RNA editing sites

Differences in codon usage frequency among organisms are considered the result of an evolutionary equilibrium shaped by long-term natural selection and mutational biases. In this study, relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) analysis of mitochondrial genomes from eight Medicago species was performed using Perl scripts. Representative and unique CDS sequences were selected, and the codon usage counts for each gene were tabulated [ 73 ]. To predict RNA editing sites in the mitochondrial genomes of these eight Medicago species, the plant RNA editing prediction tool PREPACT ( http://www.prepact.de/prepact-main.php ) was employed for analysis [ 74 ].


## Phylogenetic analysis

To clarify the phylogenetic position of Medicago species within the Fabaceae family, a phylogenetic analysis based on mitochondrial genomes was conducted in this study. Mitochondrial genome data of 17 Fabaceae species related to Medicago were downloaded from the NCBI database and re-annotated using the same annotation software, PMGA [ 69 ], to ensure consistent annotation formats for subsequent analyses. Suriana maritima was selected as the outgroup in the phylogenetic analysis. All analyzed species and their corresponding GenBank accession numbers are listed in Table S2.

Using PhyloSuite (v1.2.3) [ 75 ], conserved mitochondrial protein-coding gene (CDS) sequences shared among the 25 Fabaceae species were extracted. After manual alignment and curation, 31 shared coding genes were retained for further analysis. Multiple sequence alignment of these nucleotide sequences was performed using MAFFT [ 76 ] with default parameters. The aligned sequences were then concatenated into a single matrix using the “Concatenate Sequence” function in PhyloSuite for phylogenetic tree construction.

Phylogenetic trees were constructed using both maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods. ML analysis was conducted with IQ-TREE [ 77 ] using the TVM + F + I + G4 nucleotide substitution model, and node support was evaluated with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. BI analysis was performed using MrBayes (v3.2) [ 78 ]. The resulting phylogenetic trees were visualized using the ITOL web platform [ 79 ].


## Colinear analysis

To investigate structural conservation and rearrangement characteristics among the mitochondrial genomes of Medicago species, a comprehensive synteny analysis was performed on eight Medicago mitochondrial genomes. First, pairwise genome alignments were conducted using BLASTn (v2.14.0+) [ 80 ] to identify conserved homologous sequence fragments. The BLASTn parameters were set as follows: -evalue 1e-10, with low-quality alignments filtered out. Only homologous regions longer than 400 bp and with sequence identity greater than 90% were retained as syntenic units for subsequent comparison. To further illustrate the structural similarities and variations of mitochondrial genomes among different Medicago species, the extracted syntenic blocks were visualized using LINKVIEW2 (v1.0.5) software.


## Analysis of Ka/Ks values

To investigate the natural selection pressures during the evolutionary process of Medicago species, eight representative species were selected for analysis. Shared PCGs were aligned using MAFFT (v11) [ 81 ], and the ratio of nonsynonymous (Ka) to synonymous (Ks) substitutions (Ka/Ks) was calculated using KaKs_Calculator (v2.0) [ 82 ].
