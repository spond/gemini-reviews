## Abstract

The prostate gland is among the last internal organs to deteriorate during human decomposition. However, the effect of postmortem interval (PMI) on the mRNA and lncRNA expression and splicing is yet to be investigated in detail. The current study aims to identify the functional role of postmortem gene induction and pathway activation in prostate tissues with respect to the PMI gradient. Cadaver samples that were used in this study were collected during forensic autopsies and preserved at -20 °C in the morgue at the University of Pavia (Pavia, Italy). After RNA extraction, total RNA sequencing was performed on Illumina’s NovaSeq 6000 using paired-ended sequencing approach. StringTie was used to perform expression level for mRNAs and lncRNAs by calculating FPKM. Additionally, mRNAs and lncRNAs differential expression analyses were performed by DESeq2. rMATS was used to identify alternative splicing events and analyze differential alternative splicing events between samples having high and low PMI. Pathway analysis of the differentially expressed genes demonstrated the enrichment of FoxO signaling, aldosterone-regulated sodium reabsorption and adipocytokine signaling pathways in prostate tissue with high PMI. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) indicated the positive enrichment of genes belonging from protein export, proteasome, ferroptosis, and citric acid cycle in high PMI group. A comprehensive detection of alternative splicing events (ASEs) at the cellular level in postmortem prostate tissue was performed to reveal skipped exon events to be most prominent ASE in high PMI group followed by retained intron events. Our study implies that the transcription machinery remains active in prostate tissue even after five days postmortem. The results will add profound knowledge about postmortem changes at a molecular level and can add useful information for the determination of postmortem interval, which remains a challenge for forensic pathologists.

## Introduction

Currently, the determination of postmortem interval is based on quite dated and error-prone methods 1 . The most accurate of these methods, such as the Hessge’s nomogram or the mechanical excitability of skeletal muscle, remain applicable only during the first few hours after death 2 . While multiple PMI determination methods exist, challenges persist due to limited understanding of human decomposition processes and methodological susceptibility to errors and biases 3 . Developing alternative approaches becomes crucial in cases requiring robustness beyond 24 h of decomposition 4 .

The prostate gland demonstrates exceptional resistance to decomposition among internal organs 5 , 6 . Continued gene expression in male reproductive organs still happens during decomposition, which might decay significant parts of the organ after human death 7 . Elucidating the fundamental variables governing postmortem gene activation could reveal novel mechanisms of cellular death processes 8 – 11 , potentially advancing PMI assessment systems 6 . Despite this potential, the relationship between PMI and prostate-specific mRNA/lncRNA expression patterns remains uncharacterized.

Analysis of postmortem gene expression dynamics could establish temporal profiles of specific gene presence, absence, and abundance 9 , 12 , 13 . Such data would enable the development of mathematical models predicting PMI based on tissue-specific gene expression patterns 13 – 16 . This study investigates the functional role of PMI-dependent gene induction and pathway activation in prostate tissues.


## Results and discussion

The present study investigates gene expression of five post-mortem prostate samples (Table S1). The prostate samples were taken from individuals died from both violent and natural causes, without any prostate disease, with a mean age of 46.2 ± 15.7 years (mean ± SD) (Table S1). Each tissue sample had at least 56.3% of exons in their mapped regions (Figure S1, Tables S2,3,4). This indicates that even though the tissue was acquired postmortem, the cells were still viable. The highest mapped exon level was 66.57% and the lowest mapped exon level was 56.31% (Figure S1). The total number of genes and gene density were also comparable between tissue with high PMI (> 3 days) versus low PMI (≤ 3 days) (Fig. 1 A, B , Figure S2A-C). Next mRNA differential expression analysis was performed by DESeq2 pipeline. A total of 92 genes were upregulated while 113 genes were downregulated in prostate tissue with high PMI as compared to low PMI (Fig. 1 C, D ). Figure 1 D shows a heatmap of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in prostate tissue between high PMI and low PMI. A few interesting clusters of DEGs were observed between the conditions based on the hierarchical clustering analysis (Fig. 1 D). The cluster 1 and cluster 2 of the DEGs represented 13 genes that were consistently upregulated in high PMI as compared to low PMI (Fig. 1 D). Interestingly, eight ( ADAMTS4 , FURIN , SERPINE1 , IRS1 , CRY1 , RANBP9 , ERRFI1 , SERPINB3 ) out of these thirteen genes displayed enzyme binding molecular function when queried using STRING pathway analysis (Figure S3A). Further analysis revealed the presence of additional protease binding molecular function in four of these eight genes ( ADAMTS4 , FURIN , SERPINE1, SERPINB3 ) (Figure S3A). Among other significant upregulated genes involved in post-translational protein modification was MUC3A which participates in protein ubiquitination process. Interestingly transcription factors and regulators CEBPD , NFIL3 , SOCS3 and KRT17 were also increased in prostate tissue with high PMI indicative of active gene transcription process mainly towards cellular proliferation, migration and apoptosis (Figure S3A). Cluster 3, which represented 9 transcripts that were downregulated in prostate tissue with high PMI as compared to low PMI, did not exhibit any functional relationship as determined by STRING pathway analysis (Figure S3B). However, there were certain genes such as GALNT9 and ZNF177 that participate in transcriptional and translational regulation of genes (Figure S3B). In addition, lysine-specific demethylase hairless ( HR ) gene that participate in transcriptional regulation of cell cycle was also downregulated in prostate tissue with high PMI as compared to low PMI (Figure S3B). As genome-wide experiments do not allow the evaluation of RNA degradation, therefore the marked changes in the gene expression may be attributed to the combination of a reduced RNA synthesis and/or to the RNA degradation as reported previously 17 . A follow-up unbiased analysis was performed to observe the cellular component, biological process and molecular functions associated with all significant DEGs in prostate tissue with high PMI as compared to low PMI using Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis. The cellular component analysis demonstrated that the number of DEGs was highest in the cytoplasm followed by membrane, nucleus and plasma membrane (Fig. 2 A). As observed by STRING analysis (Figure S3A), the protein binding molecular function was the pathway comprised of most DEGs followed by metal ion binding and DNA binding activities in prostate tissue with high PMI as compared to low PMI (Fig. 2 A). Signal transduction, regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase and regulation of DNA-templated transcription were few biological processes that were most enriched in high PMI prostate tissues (Fig. 2 A). Enrichment of biological processes such as cell differentiation, positive regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, positive regulation of cell proliferation, and negative regulation of apoptotic process further strengthens our observation that cellular machinery was still active in prostate tissue with high PMI as compared to low PMI. Figure 2 B demonstrates the scatterplot of enriched GO terms with the associated rich factor which is a ratio of DEGs in GO term to the total number of genes in that GO term. Positive regulation of muscle development and muscle cell differentiation were top enriched GO terms which supported the observation of cells being alive in high PMI prostate tissue. Furthermore, enrichment of GO terms such as adenosine metabolic process, protease binding, positive regulation of angiogenesis, 5’-nucleotidase activity, positive regulation of glycogen biosynthetic process and protein kinase C signaling established the fact that the cells were metabolically active even with high PMI. Follow up analysis then investigated the signaling pathways that were enriched in prostate tissue with high PMI as compared to low PMI using KEGG pathway and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). Among the KEGG pathways that were enriched in prostate tissue with high PMI were FoxO signaling and aldosterone-regulated sodium reabsorption pathways (Fig. 3 ). Physiologically, the members of the FoxO family are involved in cell cycle progression, proliferation, apoptosis and response to oxidative stress in an androgen-dependent manner 18 – 22 . The aldosterone-regulated sodium reabsorption pathway regulates the activity of the epithelial sodium channel and sodium reabsorption from the luminal fluid within the prostate gland. This pathway is mediated through the activation of mineralocorticoid receptors within prostate epithelial cells and potentially impact sperm motility and viability by influencing prostate fluid composition. These findings from KEGG pathway analysis indicated the functional integrity of prostate tissue even after high PMI. Follow-up GSEA analysis further consolidated this observation by the positive enrichment of biological functions (i) protein export, (ii) proteasome, (iii) ferroptosis, (iv) tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and (v) aldosterone-regulated sodium reabsorption in prostate tissue with high PMI (Fig. 4 A-F, Table S5, Figure S4A,B). Activation of ferroptosis and TCA cycle also points towards cellular proliferation as in benign prostate tissue, ferroptosis is generally considered to be minimal or absent due to well-regulated iron metabolism. Among the genes that were negatively enriched in the prostate tissue with high PMI was glycosaminoglycan (GAG) biosynthesis heparan sulfate pathway (Fig. 4 G, Table S5). GAGs are large, negatively charged polymers involved in signaling of extracellular matrix organization during prostatic growth 23 . Diminished levels of GAG biosynthesis associated with prostate tissue with high PMI correlate with the cessation of androgen production by the testicles in postmortem prostate tissue 24 . Collectively, these total RNA sequencing data suggest ongoing transcriptional activity persists in prostate tissue at extended postmortem intervals. We subsequently quantified PMI-dependent temporal expression patterns of long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) in prostate tissue. Circos plot presented in Fig. 5 A shows lncRNA density distribution with expression levels for each sample studied. To investigate the novel expressed novel lncRNAs, transcripts that overlapped with known mRNAs, known lncRNAs and transcripts shorter than 200 bp were discarded. Then Coding Potential Calculator (CPC) version 2 25 and Coding-Non-Coding Index (CNCI) 26 were utilized to predict transcripts with coding potential. All transcripts with CPC score < 0.5 and CNCI score < 0 were retained and considered as novel lncRNAs. Five types of transcripts assembled by StringTie were retained as following class codes: (j) Potentially novel isoform (fragment): at least one splice junction is shared with a reference transcript, (i) A transfrag falling entirely within a reference intron, (o) Generic exonic overlap with a reference transcript, (u) Unknown, intergenic transcript and (x) Exonic overlap with reference on the opposite strand (Figure S5). A similar expression pattern between high and low PMI prostate tissue was observed in either of these five lncRNAs transcript types (Figure S5). Next, differential expression analysis was performed to observe the changes in the lncRNA expression with respect to the PMI. A total of 91 lncRNAs were upregulated while 262 genes were downregulated in prostate tissue with high PMI as compared to low PMI (Fig. 5 B, C ). Figure 5 D shows heatmap of differentially expressed lncRNAs in prostate tissue between high PMI and low PMI. Two interesting clusters of DEGs were observed between the conditions based on the hierarchical clustering analysis (Fig. 5 D). The cluster 1 of the differentially expressed lncRNAs represented 6 lncRNAs that were constantly upregulated in high PMI as compared to low PMI (Fig. 5 D). This comprises three known lncRNAs ENST00000479777, ENST00000561775 and ENST00000503185 which are associated with genes SLC44A4 , RPL4 and AC139887.2 . The other three significant differentially expressed novel identified lncRNAs were MSTRG.13012 , MSTRG.9221 and MSTRG.4682.11, which are associated with genes SNTG2 , PDE4D and ENSG00000282885 (Fig. 5 D). Unbiased pathway analysis of the differentially expressed lncRNAs was performed to observe the pathways enriched in high PMI as compared to low PMI group. GO enrichment analysis revealed the enrichment of development and morphogenesis pathways such as embryonic skeletal system morphogenesis and development patterns (Fig. 6 A). Additionally, a number of lipid catabolic processes were also enriched (Fig. 6 B). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated several metabolic processes such as arachidonic acid metabolism, estrogen signaling, phospholipase signaling were among top enriched pathways contributed by differentially expressed lncRNA. Interestingly, although borderline significant, prostate cancer and excision related lncRNAs were also enriched in prostate tissue with high PMI. Altogether, the lncRNA expression analysis also supported the findings of presence of cellular metabolism and proliferation events. However, additional in-depth complementary orthogonal analyses (e.g., metabolomics), are required to establish the significance of specific post-mortem metabolic pathways. Finally, to investigate cellular viability and cellular stress, alternative splicing (AS) events were compared between prostate tissue with high PMI and low PMI. Following events were compared between the two conditions: (i) skipped exon (SE), (ii) alternative 5’splice site (A5SS), (iii) alternative 3’splice site (A3SS), (iv) mutually exclusive exons (MXE) and (v) retained intron (RI) using junctions only (JC) and junctions and exons (JCEC) approaches (Fig. 7 A-C). Both approaches indicated the presence of SE as the predominant differentially expressed AS event in prostate tissue with high PMI as compared to low PMI (Fig. 7 B,C, Table S6). The next predominant differentially expressed splicing pattern was RI followed by A3SS, A5SS and MXE in prostate tissue with high PMI (Fig. 7 B,C, Table S6). Presence of exon skipping events in prostate tissue with high PMI pointed towards the viability of the cells and possible presence of protein diversity in the tissue. In the present study, we investigated total RNA expression in postmortem prostate tissue and observed persistent transcriptional activity after PMI that did not significantly decrease with increasing PMI duration. Multiple genes involved in diverse cellular processes, along with lncRNAs and ASEs, exhibited significant upregulation in high-PMI samples compared to low-PMI controls, while others were downregulated. These findings suggest that postmortem transcriptional changes are not merely consequences of tissue degradation but reflect active regulatory mechanisms. Postmortem human tissue samples are a significant asset for investigating gene expression patterns. Postmortem genetic analysis of the RNA transcriptome from a putative body fluids and tissues fragment collected at a crime scene should permit assignment to a specific organ source given that each tissue exhibits allele-specific expression. Whole RNA sequencing technologies generate data that should distinguish RNA biomarkers that will determine the source of organs at crime scenes 27 . These data are important, from a forensic point of view, for reconstructing sequences of events that may have occurred at the crime scene and have the potential for the interpretation of evidence and provide essential contextual timetables and anatomical information. We hypothesize that changes in gene expression in different organ systems that occur because of the death process and cellular stress can be correlated to the PMI using specific biomarkers. The biomarkers were chosen after iterative specificity testing of numerous candidate genes in prostate tissues. In the present study, human postmortem prostate tissue transcriptome was studied as potential biomarker for PMI correlation. Although there are conflicting reports on what the postmortem stretch means for RNA integrity; however, a few examinations in various vertebrates have demonstrated that RNA can generally remain intact for extensive periods. Likewise, various antemortem factors, including environmental parameters and death conditions, may impact the nature of the collected tissues and their RNA. RNA quality affects the proportions of the genes measured in expression 15 . Understanding the relationship between these changes and postmortem interval is crucial for the reliable use of postmortem gene expression profiles as proxies of physiological state 15 . Results obtained from this study indicate that both mRNA and lncRNA were present up to 5 days postmortem and could be used for PMI estimation. Previous studies have also correlated mRNA degradation pattern to develop mathematical model for estimating PMI in animal model 28 – 30 . Certain housekeeping genes such as β-actin, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), and metabolic genes such as Hypoxanthine Phosphoribosyltransferase (HPRT), cytochrome P4502E1 (Cyp2E1), and Peptidylprolyl Isomerase A (Ppia) were selected for their stability in multiple studies 16 , 30 , 31 . The multi-parametric mathematical models using these target biomarkers were constructed to describe the characteristic relationship between △Ct values (normalized to reference biomarkers) and the observed PMI. Following validation, the reliability of the mathematical model was demonstrated by presence of low error rates in human samples (12.5%) 31 . In addition to coding RNAs, noncoding RNA such as microRNA (e.g. miR-195, miR-200c, miR-1, miR-206 etc.) and ribosomal RNAs (e.g. 5S, 28S, 18S) are also being selected as reliable marker for PMI estimation owing to their stability 31 . In addition, Ferreira et. al. has demonstrated that functional analysis of genes with recurrent exons (i.e., with association with PMI in more than two tissues) shows a noteworthy enrichment on RNA binding and RNA splicing genes 15 . They concluded that death led to some splicing deregulation in blood 15 . Our observation of lncRNAs presence and occurrence of significant splicing events, advocate for their inclusion in the future mathematical models to develop a practical tool for PMI estimation. Future human cross-organ post-mortem studies will establish if such post-mortem stability of lncRNA and splicing events are limited to only prostate tissue, or it is universal for other internal organs such as brain, skin, liver or bone. These cross-organ studies are also important to establish the PMI estimation tool that can be used for both sexes as the lessons learned from the prostate tissue in this study applies only to the male decedents.

The main limitation of this study is that we analyzed only five samples. However, this is a subset of a larger series which was previously analyzed providing results in which we wanted to go deeper with this study. Moreover, as the samples were retrieved from real forensic cases, not all the variables were possible to control, such as different environments in which death occurred, different causes and manners of death and different agonic periods, that might have possibly influenced the results. External factors such as temperature, humidity, sunlight exposure and microbiota interaction in the post-mortem period can influence the RNA degradation rate, mostly through epigenetic regulators 17 . However, due to the smaller number of samples, the effect of external environment on PMI gene expression changes was not investigated in this study. To address the low sample limitation, we included additional prostate tissue RNA seq data from (n = 42) GTEX database for which ischemic time was available (Table S7, S8). The samples from the GTEX cohort were divided into two groups i.e. early (n = 23, ischemic time < 150 min) and late (n = 19, ischemic time > 1000 min) based on the tissue ischemia time provided in the database (Table S7, S8). Next mRNA differential expression analysis was performed by DESeq2 pipeline. A total of 137 genes were upregulated while 84 genes were downregulated in prostate tissue with late as compared to early ischemic time (Fig. 8 A, B ). The pathway analysis of the DEGs was performed using the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) tool. The enriched network as predicted by IPA was then overlaid with biofunctions found to be significantly associated with high PMI the current study (Fig. 2 ). The network contained 16 focus molecules (Table S9), among which upregulated genes were enriched in biological processes such as DNA binding, cell proliferation and differentiation, energy production, and signal transduction (Fig. 8 C). Most interestingly, the positive regulation of muscle development and muscle cell differentiation in high PMI in the current study as observed in Fig. 2 B was also enriched in this new cohort with upregulation of classical muscle-related genes such as MYOG , MYL4 , MYH8 among others (Figure S6). This finding of ours is in line with the previous observation of muscle-related genes such as tropomysin, actin and actinin to be differentially expressed in prostate tissue along with genes related to ribosomal proteins and metabolic enzymes 32 . Furthermore, genes involved in hepran sulfate biosynthesis (late stage) were downregulated [SULT1C3 (log FC = −2.5) and SULT2A1 (log FC = −2.4) in the GTEX cohort like our cohort (Fig. 4 G). Taken together, the results obtained from GTEX cohort corroborated with the results obtained in our study. ## Methods


## Cases

Tissue samples were collected from the prostate of five males with different causes of death (Table S1). Autopsies were performed by the University of Pavia, Department of Public Health, Experimental and Forensic Medicine in Pavia, Italy. After the pelvic organs were removed as a group, the prostate was isolated. Then, a sample was taken from the inner part of the gland, always from the left half, using a sterile, disposable surgical scalpel. The samples were placed into labeled polyethylene bags and stored at − 20 °C until shipment to the Thanatos Laboratory at Alabama State University in Montgomery, Alabama, USA. Upon arrival, the samples were examined and stored at − 80 °C until further study. This research was approved by Alabama State University Institutional Review Board (IRB) number 2023106. Protocols were conducted following valid regulations and guidelines for working with human corpses. Due to unavailability to obtain informed consent from relatives, informed consent was waived by Alabama State University for this study (IRB number 2020164). Moreover, these can be considered archive samples as for this study we used part of the tissues already collected for forensic and diagnostic purposes.


## RNA extraction

RNA was extracted using QIAGEN RNeasy Mini Kit (cat. no. 74104, Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) from the prostate tissues (~ 30 mg) of three control samples with a PMI of ≤ 3 days as per manufacturer’s instructions. Experimental samples were extracted from two cases with PMIs of > 3 days. RNA extracts were quantitated using the Nanodrop [YEAR] (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).


## Library construction and sequencing

For total RNA sequencing service (LC Sciences, Huston, TX, USA), RNA integrity was checked with Agilent Technologies 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Information provided for library sequencing, transcript assembly, and related bioinformatics analysis by LC Sciences 33 . Ribosomal RNA was removed, followed by fragmentation with divalent cation buffers in elevated temperature. The sequencing library was prepared following Illumina’s TruSeq-stranded-total-RNA-sample preparation protocol. Quality control analysis and quantification of the sequencing library were performed using 2100 Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA Chip (cat. no. 5067–4626, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Paired-ended sequencing was performed on NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with two reads of 150 base pairs each.


## Bioinformatics analysis


## Transcripts assembly

Firstly, Cutadapt 1.0 34 was used to remove the reads that contained adaptor contamination, low quality bases and undetermined bases. Then sequence quality was verified using FastQC 0.10.1 ( http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/ ). We used HISAT2 2.0 35 to map reads to the genome of Homo Sapiens (GRCh38.p14 with Ensembl 110). The mapped reads of each sample were assembled using StringTie 1.3.4 36 . Then, all transcriptomes from five samples were merged to reconstruct a comprehensive transcriptome using gffcompare ( [GITHUB_REPO_REDACTED]/ ). After the final transcriptome was generated, StringTie 36 and ballgown ( http://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/-html/ballgown.html ) was used to estimate the expression levels of all transcripts.


## Different expression analysis of transcripts

StringTie 1.3.4 36 was used to perform expression level for mRNAs and lncRNAs by calculating FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads). mRNAs and lncRNAs differential expression analysis was performed by DESeq2 37 software between two different groups. The genes/mRNAs and lncRNAs with the parameter of false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 and absolute fold change ≥ 2 were considered differentially expressed genes or mRNAs or lncRNAs.


## GO enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

Gene ontology (GO) ( http://www.geneontology.org ) is a major bioinformatics initiative to unify the representation of gene and gene product attributes across all species 33 , 38 , 39 . More specifically, the project aims to: 1) maintain and develop its controlled vocabulary of gene and gene product attributes; 2) annotate genes and gene products, and assimilate and disseminate annotation data; and 3) provide tools for easy access to all aspects of the data provided by the project, and to enable functional interpretation of experimental data using the GO, for example via enrichment analysis 33 , 38 , 39 .

Significant GO terms were calculated by hypergeometric equation as shown below. TB gene number = number of total genes; TS gene number = number of differentially expressed genes in total genes; B gene number = total number of genes in all GO terms; S gene number = number of differentially expressed genes in this GO term. Those GO terms with p value < 0.05 were defined as significant GO terms. Number of differentially expressed genes enriched in GO terms, p value and rich factor are shown in scatterplot. Rich factor = (number of differentially expressed genes in GO term)/(total number of genes in GO term). The larger rich factor is, the higher the enrichment is.

## Novel lncRNA identification

First, transcripts that overlapped with known mRNAs, known lncRNAs and transcripts shorter than 200 bp were filtered. Then we utilized CPC0.9-r2 25 and CNCI2.0 26 to predict transcripts with coding potential. All transcripts with CPC score < 0.5 and CNCI score < 0 were retained and considered as novel lncRNAs. The remaining transcripts with class code (i, j, o, u, x) were considered as lncRNAs.


## Target gene prediction and functional analysis of lncRNAs

To explore the function of lncRNAs, we predicted the cis -target genes of lncRNAs. lncRNAs may play a cis role acting on neighboring target genes. In this study, coding genes in 100,000 upstream and downstream were selected. Enrichment analysis of GO functions and KEGG pathways were performed for the target genes of the lncRNAs. Significance was expressed as a p value < 0.05.


## Alternative splicing analysis

rMATS 40 (version 4.1.1) was used to identify alternative splicing events and analyze differential alternative splicing events between samples. We identified AS events with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 in a comparison as significant AS events.


## Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

We performed gene set enrichment analysis using software GSEA 41 (v4.1.0) and MSigDB to identify whether a set of genes in specific GO terms, KEGG pathways 42 – 44 shows significant differences in two groups. Briefly, we input gene expression matrix and rank genes by Signal2Noise normalization method. Enrichment scores and p value was calculated in default parameters. GO terms, KEGG pathways meeting this condition with |NES|> 1, NOM p-val < 0.05, FDR q-val < 0.25 were considered to be different in two groups.


## Localization of lncRNAs on genome

Two diagrams were generated to show the localization and abundance of lncRNAs in genome using the program Circos 45 . One diagram showed lncRNA expression level in different samples. The lncRNAs in the other diagram were subdivided into five categories according to their class code generated by StringTie: (i) a transfrag falling entirely within a reference intron (intronic); (j) potentially novel isoform or fragment at least one splice junction is shared with a reference transcript; (o) generic exonic overlap with a reference transcript; (u) unknown, intergenic transcript (intergenic); (x) Exonic overlap with reference on the opposite strand (antisense).


## STRING pathway analysis

Protein–protein interaction network and function enrichment analysis was conducted using STRING database version 12.0 as reported by us previously 46 , 47 . Briefly, 13 genes that were constantly upregulated in high PMI as compared to low PMI in cluster 1 and cluster 2 of the DEGs were subjected to pathway analysis using default parameters [network type = full STRING network; minimum required interaction score = medium confidence (0.4)].


## Ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA)

After the identification of DEGs, enriched pathways were identified using the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) database (QIAGEN Inc., Redwood City, CA, USA www.qiagen.com/ingenuity ) that predicts biological networks enriched in the dataset based on the existing literature. Significant DEGs were subjected to canonical pathway analysis using IPA as reported by us previously 48 , 49 . For DEGs, manually selected pathways were visualized for network enrichment.


## Data download from GTEx

Gene expression (transcripts per million) TPM values and sample annotations, including the tissue ischemic time, age, RNA Integrity Number, tissue autolysis score, were downloaded from GTEx database 50 , 51 . Out of the 282 prostate samples available in GTEx portal, 42 samples were analyzed after dividing into two groups i.e. early (n = 23, ischemic time < 150 min) and late (n = 19, ischemic time > 1000 min) based on the tissue ischemia time provided in the database.
