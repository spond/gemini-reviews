## Abstract

Tumor-associated macrophages have been implicated in the progression and treatment resistance of glioblastoma (GBM). This study aimed to identify macrophage-related genes associated with prognosis and therapeutic response in GBM.

Bulk RNA-seq data from 533 patients with GBM were downloaded from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) databases. Bioinformatic tools were used to detect the co-expression gene modules associated with the infiltration of immune cells, identify a prognostic macrophage-related gene signature, and explore their association with sensitivity to chemotherapeutic drugs and immune checkpoint blockade. Single-cell RNA-seq data and multiplexed immunofluorescence were used to validate ISG20 expression (a member of the identified gene signature) in macrophages.

We detected gene modules associated with macrophages and identified a signature consisting of three macrophage-related genes (ISG20, PARP12 and IFIT5) in the discovery set (TCGA–GBM, n = 159), and validated its prognostic value in the validation set (CGGA–GBM, n = 374). This gene signature demonstrated favorable accuracy in predicting prognosis and resistance of immuno- and chemo-therapy. The co-expression of ISG20 and PD-1 in macrophages was verified by single-cell RNA-seq data and multiplex immunofluorescence.

This study presents a macrophage-related gene signature to predict prognosis and therapeutic response in GBM. ISG20, PARP12 and IFIT5 are interferon-stimulated genes, and further investigations may provide new insights into the interplay between macrophages and interferon signaling in GBM.


## Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) represents the most malignant form of primary brain tumor, possessing a poor prognosis characterized by high mortality and recurrence rates [ 1 ]. The conventional treatments of GBM includes surgical resection, temozolomide (TMZ) chemotherapy and radiotherapy [ 2 ]. The development of novel therapies, such as immunotherapy, molecular targeting therapy, and electric field therapy, is of great clinical significance. Over the past decades, the tumor microenvironment (TME) has been implicated in influencing the behavior of malignant cells [ 3 ]. Louveau et al. discovered intracranial lymphatic vessels [ 4 ], leading to a review of brain tolerance and a revisit of immune cells’ roles in brain tumors. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are the major immune cells in the TME of GBM, accounting for approximately one third of the total cells [ 5 ]. Nearly 15% of TAMs are from intrinsic microglia, while the others are from circulating monocytes [ 6 ]. TAMs can be classified into heterogeneous subgroups, possess either pro-tumor or antitumor activity, and contribute to treatment sensitivity according to the originations, surface markers, and characteristics of transcriptome [ 7 ]. Although TAMs are proposed to be involved in GBM progression and therapeutic resistance, treatment strategies targeting TAMs are in early development. Further exploration of TAMs is needed to develop more precise therapeutic approaches [ 8 ].

Recently, omics approaches embracing genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics have made great progress due to advances in omics technologies and computational tools. Omics, especially transcriptomics, linked with bioinformatics tools have opened new avenues to identify and characterize biomolecules involved in cancer biology and therapy. Although the research based on omics approaches in the field of neuro-oncology is sharply increasing, the diversity of research is not high and the field is still in its infancy [ 9 ]. Xiong et al. use an integrative approach to infer tumor purity based on transcriptomics data and comprehensively evaluate the impact of tumor purity on GBM prognosis in the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort [ 10 ]. Shan et al. applied the transcriptomics datasets (Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas [CGGA] and TCGA) to comprehensively explore the interaction between macrophage-related genes and survival in gliomas, and identified a 9-genes signature that efficiently classify glioma patients into risky subgroups with differing prognosis [ 11 ]. Although bulk RNA-seq has guided our initial understanding of glioma genetics, this method fails to accurately define the expression profiles of the diverse cell subpopulations in glioma, leading to an underappreciation of heterogeneity and a misclassification of tumors [ 12 ]. Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) provides an opportunity to access information about cellular biology at unprecedented resolution [ 12 ]. Therefore, it has important applications in cancer research, as various cell types and the TME contribute to tumor initiation, progress and therapeutic resistance. However, it should be noted that small sample size due to high cost and difficult protocols is the common limitation of studies relying solely on scRNA-seq, and integration of bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq data has potential to be a valid solution.

In the present study, we performed an integrated analysis of bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq data to explore macrophage-related genes in GBM. We identified and validated a three-gene signature (ISG20, PARP12 and IFIT5) that had favorable accuracy in predicting prognosis and sensitivity of immuno- and chemo-therapy. The mRNA expression levels do not always match the protein level. Therefore, we confirmed the expression of ISG20 and its co-localization with PD-1 in macrophages using multiplex immunofluorescence assay in GBM samples. This study presented a novel macrophage-related gene signature to predict prognosis and therapeutic response in GBM. ISG20, PARP12, and IFIT5 are interferon-stimulated genes, and further investigations may provide new insights into the interplay between macrophages and interferon signaling in GBM.


## Materials and methods


## Bulk RNA-seq datasets and samples

The bulk RNA-seq expression data and clinical information for GBM cohorts from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) were collected from the (TCGA: https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/ and CGGA: http://www.cgga.org.cn/ ). We obtained 159 TCGA–GBM samples with available overall survival (OS) data, which were designated as the training cohort. For validation, 374 CGGA–GBM samples with available OS data were obtained from mRNAseq-693 and mRNAseq-325 datasets. Additionally, five formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded samples from GBM patients were used for multiplexed immunofluorescence validation. These samples were collected from the First Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital from January 2022 to February 2023. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chinese PLA General Hospital with documented approval (No. S2018-089-02). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All the procedures involving human participants complied with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.


## Immune infiltration analysis

The CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to estimate immune cell infiltration within the TME of GBM based on bulk RNA-seq. The immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores were calculated according to the ESTIMATE algorithm to infer the abundance of immune and stromal cells and tumor purity of the GBM TME.


## WGCNA

WGCNA was used to identify co-expression modules and explore the relationships between these modules and the infiltrated immune cells as previously described [ 13 ].


## Construction and validation of the risk score model

Genes in the module with the highest positive correlation coefficient in the WGCNA were chosen to develop the risk score model. The univariate Cox regression analysis was used to screen genes of prognostic value with p value < 0.05. LASSO estimation and the multivariate Cox regression were further applied to develop a best-fitting risk score model using the glmnet and survminer R packages, respectively. The risk score was created as follows: risk score = ∑ i = 1 n β i ∗ i . βi represented the multivariate Cox regression coefficient of a gene, and i was the expression level of the corresponding gene. Patients with GBM were stratified into low- and high-risk subgroups according to the median risk score of the training cohort (TCGA–GBM). The prognostic performance of the risk score model was externally validated in the CGGA–GBM cohort.


## Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was performed as previously described [ 14 ]. The h.all.v7.4. symbols.gmt gene set collection was used as the annotation reference. Risk score (high or low) was designated as the phenotype. Significantly enriched pathways (FDR <0.05) were ranked using the normalized enrichment score.


## Prediction of therapeutic response

The pRRophetic R package was used to explore potential drugs sensitivities [ 15 ]. The immune phenotyping score (IPS) is a scoring scheme for quantifying tumor immunogenicity and predicting immune checkpoint blockade effectiveness [ 16 ]. The IPS of patients with TCGA–GBM was retrieved from the TCIA database ( https://tcia.at/ ).


## scRNA-seq data analysis

The IMMUcan scDB database ( https://immucanscdb.vital-it.ch/ ) is a fully integrated scRNA-seq database exclusively dedicated to human cancer and accessible to non-specialists [ 17 ]. GBM datasets were searched and selected from the dataset list. Cell type compositions of each sample were explored by selecting various levels of annotation using the ‘UAMP Plot’ panel. The ‘Gene X vs. Gene Y expression’ panel was used to evaluate gene co-expression at the cell type resolution.


## Multiplexed immunofluorescence assay

The multiplex immunofluorescence assay was performed to validate the co-expression of ISG20 and PD-1 in macrophages using the OPALTM Polaris 7-Color Manual IHC kit (catalog# NEL861001KT; Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA) as previously described [ 18 ]. Four-micrometer sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval. After blocking non-specific binding with goat serum (SAP-9110, ZSGQ-BIO, Beijing, China), sections were sequentially incubated with primary antibodies: anti-ISG20 (1:100, catalog# ab157477, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-CD68 (1:600, catalog# GM081429 , Gene Tech, Shanghai, China), anti-PD-1 (1:200, catalog# 43248, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), and anti-CD163 (1:800, catalog# GT207729 , Gene Tech, Shanghai, China). Corresponding horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies, tyramide signal amplification, and OPAL fluorescence dyes (520 [ISG20], 570 [CD68], 620 [PD-1] and 690 [CD163]) were applied. Finally, the sections were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence signals were scanned and analyzed using a Vectra Polaris multispectral imaging system (Akoya Biosciences) and InForm software (version 2.5.0, Akoya Biosciences).


## Statistical analysis

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the differences between the two groups. Correlations were determined using Spearman’s correlation analysis. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 3.6.3) or GraphPad Prism (version 9.0.0). All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.


## Results


## Identification of co-expression modules associated with infiltrated immune cells in GBM

To investigate the association between immune cell infiltration and GBM progression, we first conducted a quantitative analysis of immune cell abundance in GBM. The infiltration of 22 immune cells in TCGA–GBM samples dissected using the CIBERSORT algorithm is shown in Figure S1A . The correlation between the relative abundances of the 22 immune cells is shown in Figure S1B . We explored the co-expression modules related to immune cells. Based on the expression matrix of TCGA–GBM, a signed network was constructed using the R package ‘WGCNA’. The sample clustering to detect outliers is shown in Figure S2A . A soft threshold β = 8 with R 2 = 0.854 was calculated as the best fit for the network, depending on the optimum scale dependence and mean connectivity ( Figure S2B ). After merging similar modules with a height of less than 0.25 ( Figure S2C ), a total of 21 modules were obtained ( Figure S2D ). The light-yellow module showed the strongest positive relationship with M1 macrophages (correlation coefficient = 0.48, p = 6e–11; Figure 1 ), implying that this module may play a critical role in macrophage infiltration in GBM. A total of 129 genes were identified in the light-yellow module.

## Construction and validation of a macrophage-related gene signature for OS prediction in GBM

Macrophages make up most of the GBM microenvironment, and their presence is reflective of tumor aggressiveness, serving as a predictor of OS in patients with GBM [ 19 ]. Therefore, genes in the light-yellow module that are closely related to M1 macrophages were selected to build a risk model for survival prediction. Univariate Cox regression screened out nine genes with prognostic value ( Figure 2A ), which were used to construct the risk model. The LASSO method was employed to penalize the effect of multicollinearity, and three genes were excluded ( Figure 2B ). Finally, the risk model consisting of three genes (ISG20, PARP12 and IFIT5) was developed using multivariate Cox regression (global p value = 0.0001; Akaike information criterion = 1027.57; concordance index = 0.63). Detailed information on these three genes is presented in Table 1 . The risk score of each patient in the training (TCGA–GBM) and validation (CGGA–GBM) cohorts was calculated using the risk score formula. The patients were classified into high- or low-risk subgroups using the median value of the risk scores in the training cohort as the threshold ( Figure 2C ). Kaplan–Meier survival analyses showed that a high-risk score was related to a lower OS rate in both the training (log-rank p < 0.001) and validation (log-rank p < 0.01) cohorts ( Figure 2D ). To assess the sensitivity and specificity of the risk score in predicting the 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival rates of patients with GBM, we conducted a time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis in the training and validation cohorts. The areas under the ROC curves (AUROCs) for 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival were 0.694, 0.686, and 0.834, respectively, in the training cohort and 0.615, 0.607, and 0.633, respectively, in the validation cohorts, indicating that the predictive accuracy of the risk model was favorable ( Figure 2E ).

## The macrophage-related gene signature is an independent risk factor of OS for patients with GBM

The association between clinical characteristics and risk scores were further investigated. GBM patients in the high-risk group tended to be older ( p < 0.05), with mesenchymal subtype ( p < 0.01), non-G-CIMP ( p < 0.01), and IDH1 wild-type ( p < 0.01) ( Figure 3A ). We them conducted a Cox regression analysis with age, sex, subtype, G-CIMP, IDH1 mutation, and risk score as covariates. The univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that age ( p < 0.001), G-CIMP ( p < 0.001), IDH1 mutation ( p < 0.05), and risk score ( p < 0.001) were significantly related to OS ( Figure 3B , upper panel). The risk score was an independent risk factor of GBM as shown in the multivariate Cox regression analysis ( p = 0.01; Figure 3B , lower panel). Moreover, stratification analysis suggested that the risk score could distinguish individuals with different prognoses in stratified age (age <60 years, log-rank p < 0.01; age >60 years, log-rank p < 0.05; Figure 3C ) and subtypes (classic plus mesenchymal, log-rank p = 0.0997; neural plus proneural, log-rank p < 0.001; Figure 3D ), although the p value was not significant in patients with classic or mesenchymal subtypes. Finally, we developed a survival nomogram prediction model on the basis of the clinicopathological features of patients with GBM ( Figure 3E ). The calibration curve displayed favorable coincidence between the observed and predicted 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival rates ( Figure 3F ).

## The macrophage-related gene signature predicts therapeutic response for GBM patients

We used GSEA to explore signaling pathways associated with the macrophage-related gene signature. The results showed that the high-risk score was significantly related to a range of immune and cancer-related signaling pathways, including TNF-α signaling via interferon response, IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling, apoptosis, NF-κB, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (all FDR < 0.05; Figure 4A ). For instance, the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway has been implicated in multiple cancers, promoting the proliferation, metastasis, and invasion of malignant cells, while possessing a strong suppressive role in antitumor immune response [ 20 ]. Furthermore, we investigated the role of macrophage-related gene signatures in TME remodeling. ESTIMATE analysis revealed a positively association between the risk score and the stromal ( p < 0.001) and immune ( p < 0.001) scores ( Figure 4B ), and GBM patients in the high-risk subgroup showed increased immune ( p < 0.001) and stromal ( p < 0.01) scores ( Figure 4C ). Immune checkpoints can act as the molecular ‘brakes’ to suppress the antitumor immune response [ 21 ]. Next, we investigated the association between risk score and immune regulators. The risk score was most positively associated with TNFRSF14 ( p < 0.001) and most negatively correlated with CD200 ( p < 0.001) ( Figure 4D ). The risk score was significantly associated with a range of immune checkpoint regulators, such as PDCD1 ( p < 0.001), CD274 ( p < 0.001), CTLA4 ( p < 0.001), and PDCD1LG2 ( p < 0.001) ( Figure 4D ). We further compared the expression of these genes in two subgroups. The results showed that CD200 ( p < 0.001) was significantly downregulated in the high-risk subgroup, whereas CD274 ( p < 0.001), CTLA4 ( p < 0.05), PDCD1 ( p < 0.001), PDCD1LG2 ( p < 0.01), and TNFRSF14 ( p < 0.001) were significantly upregulated ( Figure 4E ). Moreover, we investigated the sensitivity to immunotherapy in different risk subgroups. The results showed that PD-1 blockade ( p < 0.001) and PD-1+ CTLA4 dual blockade ( p < 0.001) showed significantly better treatment efficacy in the high-risk subgroup ( Figure 4F ). Additionally, 18 potential small molecule compounds (such as AZ628, PAC-1, refametinib, and rapamycin) with different drug action mechanisms (such as Raf kinase inhibitor, procaspase-3 activator, MEK inhibitor, and mTOR inhibitor) showed significant variation in sensitivity between the two subgroups ( Figure 4G ). These findings provide a potential reference for GBM treatment. Taken together, these data imply that the macrophage-related gene signature indicates the therapeutic efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade and a range of small molecules in GBM.

## scRNA-seq analysis and multiplexed immunofluorescence verify co-expression of ISG20 and PD-1 in macrophages

The GSE131928 dataset, containing 10 cases of GBM in the IMMUcan scDB database, was selected for scRNA-seq analysis. UMAP analysis identified nine cell clusters (dendritic cell [DC], cycling macrophages (macro cycling), macrophages, mast cells, malignant cells, monocytes, T cells, oligodendrocytes, and unknown cells) based on cell annotation of ‘immune’ ( Figure 5A ). Figure 5B showed the relative cellular counts in GBM samples. ISG20 expression across different cell types is shown in Figure 5C , and ISG20 was mainly expressed in T cells (68.9%), DCs (38.5%), macrophages (16.1%), and oligodendrocytes (12.0%) ( Figure 5D ). Co-expression analysis revealed that ISG20 significantly overlapped with CD68 ( p < 0.001, pan-macrophage marker), CD86 ( p < 0.001, M1 macrophage marker), IRF5 ( p = 0.013, M1 macrophage marker), CD163 ( p < 0.001, M2 macrophage marker), and STAT6 ( p = 0.005, M2 macrophage marker) in macrophages ( Figure 5E ). Multiplexed immunofluorescence analysis further validated the co-localization of ISG20, PD-1, CD163, and CD68 in GBM samples ( Figure 5F ). Collectively, these results confirmed ISG20 and its co-localization with PD-1 in macrophages, suggesting an inhibitory TME with the presence of ISG20.

## Discussion

GBM represents the most malignant type of adult brain tumor. Patients with GBM receiving standard care therapy gain limited clinical benefits, with most of them experiencing tumor recurrence [ 22 ]. Over the past decades, immunotherapy has become a promising therapeutic option for several types of advanced cancers. However, the treatment efficacy of immunotherapy for GBM is unsatisfactory because of the unique immune characteristics of the brain, heterogeneity of GBM cells, and diversity of the TME [ 23 ]. The identification of novel biomarkers capable of indicating immune state and therapeutic sensitivity may facilitate patient stratification for more effective treatments.

Based on the bulk RNA-seq data of GBM patients from TCGA and CGGA cohorts, the present study set to explore macrophage-related genetic markers for GBM. Firstly, the CIBERSORT algorithm was applied to the bulk RNA-seq data to estimate immune cell infiltration within the TME. Then, a weighted free-scale network was constructed to identify a various of gene modules (represented by different colors), and to evaluate their associations with the immune cells. The light-yellow module was most positively associated with M1 macrophages, and a total of 129 genes within this module were designated as macrophage-related genes. Finally, based on these 129 candidate genes, a prognostic macrophage-related genes signature consisting of ISG20, PARP12 and IFIT5 was developed by employing Cox regression and LASSO estimation in the TCGA–GBM cohort ( n = 159) and validated in the CGGA–GBM cohort ( n = 374). Sun et al. developed a multicellular gene network-based approach to investigating intercellular gene associations between tumor cells and TAMs and to identify biomarkers of prognosis and drug resistance in gliomas [ 24 ]. They used RNA-seq data from mice bearing gliomas treated with BLZ945 (a CSF1R inhibitor) to construct drug-sensitive and drug-resistant multicellular gene networks (sensitive network, perturbation network and differential network) [ 24 ]. The genes in the differential network were mapped to those of genes in homo sapiens, resulting in a list of 29 candidate genes [ 24 ]. Based on these 29 candidate genes, a macrophage-related gene signature consisting of 12 genes (ANPEP, DPP4, PRRG1, GPNMB, TMEM26, PXDN, CDH6, SCN3A, SEMA6B, CCDC37, FANCA and NETO2) was developed in a learning set of CGGA–glioma cohort ( n = 310) using the Cox regression model and LASSO regression method, and validated in an independent validation set of TCGA–glioma cohort ( n = 690) [ 24 ]. Shan et al. attempted to identify the correlation between expression of macrophage-related genes and survival of patients with gliomas, and to develop a signature to predict the survival status [ 11 ]. After reviewing the studies on macrophages phenotype, they initially extracted 127 macrophages-related genes from three reviews and 125 genes could ultimately matched to the RNA-seq data [ 11 ]. Based on these 125 candidate genes, they developed a prognostic signature consisting of 9 genes (SOCS1, MSR1, TGFB1, CTSC, SOCS3, PTX3, FN1, PSME2 and CXCL9) in a training set of 216 glioma samples from CGGA dataset using the Cox regression method, and validated in an independent validation set of 580 glioma samples from TCGA dataset [ 11 ]. Above three studies identified distinct prognostic signatures due to differences in macrophage-related genes collection (WGCNA vs. multicellular gene network vs. literature review), transcriptional data from participants (glioma vs. GBM), and model construction method (Cox regression + LASSO estimation vs. Cox regression). The sort of studies mutually complements to expand our understanding of prognostic biomarkers for glioma. Moreover, PD-1 blockade and PD-1 plus CTLA4 dual blockade showed significantly better treatment efficacy in the high-risk subgroup stratified by the macrophage-related gene signature in the present study. The expression of ISG20 and its co-localization with PD-1 in macrophages was verified by scRNA-seq data analysis, and was further confirmed by the multiplex immunofluorescence assay. These findings suggest an inhibitory TME might exist with the presence of ISG20, providing valuable insights in therapeutic strategy for GBM.

ISG20 was first identified as a promyelocytic leukemia nuclear body-associated protein induced by interferons [ 25 ]. ISG20 has been reported to be involved in regulation of RNA stability through governing the maturation of small nucleolar RNAs and ribosome biogenesis [ 26–28 ]. Interferons trigger expression of ISG20 [ 29 ], and enhanced ISG20 expression can be found in cases of infection [ 30 ] and some types of cancers [ 31 ]. In glioma, ISG20 was observed to elevate along with increasing tumor grade. Upregulated ISG20 was also positively correlated with monocyte-derived macrophage and neutrophil infiltration, immune checkpoint molecules (such as PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA4) expression, and chemokine levels in the TME of glioma [ 32 ]. Du et al. demonstrated that hypomethylation of FOSL2 activates ISG20 transcription, promoting M2 macrophage polarization in GBM [ 33 ]. In our study, we also revealed a close association between ISG20 and macrophages, which are consistent with those of previous research, suggesting that ISG20 may serve as a promising biomarker and potential therapeutic target for GBM.

ADP-ribosyltransferases of the PARP family participate in various cellular functions, such as DNA damage repair and immune responses [ 34 ]. The induction of synthetic lethality has been proposed as a novel anti-cancer option based on the pharmaceutical inhibition of PARP1/2 in cancers with defective DNA repair mechanisms [ 35 ]. PARP12 can be induced by interferons and participating in the regulation of intracellular membrane transport, stress granules, and antiviral effects [ 36–38 ]. Although PARP12’s precise role in cancer biology remains unclear, it has been implicated in suppressing hepatocellular carcinoma metastasis by interacting with the transcription factor FHL2, stabilizing it, and repressing TGF β1 transcription [ 39 ]. The gene family of interferon-induced protein with tetratricopeptide repeat (IFIT) was originally found to be involved in antiviral immune responses [ 40 ]. IFIT1, IFIT2, IFIT3, and IFIT5 have been identified in humans [ 41 ]. Recent studies have revealed the roles of IFITs in human cancer. Zhang et al. found that IFIT1 was significantly increased in newly diagnosed GBM tissue and was negatively associated with the expression of MGMT; patients with higher IFIT1 and lower MGMT had more favorable prognosis [ 42 ]. Lipatova et al. used transcriptomic and proteomic analyses to show that IFIT3 in GBM cells could negatively affect sensitivity to oncolytic viruses [ 43 ]. Lo et al. demonstrated that epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition could be initiated by interferons-induced IFIT5 in prostate cancer [ 44 ]. However, the roles of IFIT5 and PARP12 in GBM remain largely unexplored. Our study unveils the prognostic significance of IFIT5 and PARP12 and their relationship with macrophages in GBM, which deserves further evaluation in future studies.

Interestingly, three individual genes (ISG20, PARP12 and IFIT5) from the macrophage-related gene signature are all interferon-stimulated genes. Interferon signaling in tumor cells induces a series of interferon-stimulated genes, including STAT1, IFI27, MX1, OAS1, IFIT1, and BST2 [ 45 ]. Interferon-stimulated gene expression profiles have been linked to increased tumor stemness in GBM [ 46 ], immune checkpoint blockade resistance in melanoma [ 47 ], and radiation and chemotherapy resistance in breast cancer [ 48 ]. Recently, Sørensen et al. revealed that microglia can induce a STAT1 signaling-mediated interferon-stimulated gene profile in GBM cells and increase GBM cells resistance to TMZ [ 49 ]. More in-depth research on the functions and relevant mechanisms of these genes may uncover the interplay between macrophages and interferon signaling in GBM.

The present study has several limitations. Firstly, with the advances in omics technologies and computational tools, bioinformatics has offered valuable insights into cancer research. However, introduction of multiple datasets inevitably brings about biases due to variation of data sources. Consequently, bioinformatics results should be carefully considered and firmly validated before translated into practical use. Secondly, it is noteworthy that prognosis can be affected by various factors, such as age, preexisting conditions, genetic disorders, and treatment modalities. Therefore, other potential prognostic variables should be incorporated into the gene signature. Thirdly, the sensitivity of immuno- and chemo-therapy was initially determined by computational prediction. To this end, subsequent experimental validation and clinical trials are needed to confirm the effectiveness of these pharmaceuticals. Finally, there is few experimental data regarding the role of ISG20, PARP12 and IFIT5 in macrophages. Further investigation into the functions and mechanisms of these genes in GBM is needed.


## Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents a novel macrophage-related gene signature consisting of ISG20, PARP12 and IFIT5 in GBM. This three-gene signature had favorable accuracy in predicting prognosis and sensitivity of immuno- and chemo-therapy. The three individual genes of this signature are interferon-stimulated genes. Further investigation into the functions and mechanisms of these genes may unveil the interplay of macrophages and interferon signaling in GBM.
