## Abstract

The literature suggests that alterations in functional connectivity (FC) of the Salience Network (SN) may contribute to the manifestation of some clinical features of Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). The SN plays a key role in integrating external sensory information with internal emotional and bodily information. An atypical FC of this network could explain some symptomatic features of ASD such as difficulties in self-awareness and emotion processing and provide new insights into the neurobiological basis of autism. Using the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange II we investigated the resting-state FC of core regions of SN and its association with autism symptomatology in 29 individuals with ASD compared with 29 typically developing (TD) individuals. In ASD compared to TD individuals, seed-based connectivity analysis showed a reduced FC between the rostral prefrontal cortex and left cerebellum and an increased FC between the right supramarginal gyrus and the regions of the middle temporal gyrus and angular gyrus. Finally, we found that the clinical features of ASD are mainly associated with an atypical FC of the anterior insula and the involvement of dysfunctional mechanisms for emotional and social information processing. These findings expand the knowledge about the differences in the FC of SN between ASD and TD, highlighting atypical FC between structures that play key roles in social cognition and complex cognitive processes. Such anomalies could explain difficulties in processing salient stimuli, especially those of a socio-affective nature, with an impact on emotional and behavioral regulation.

Keywords: Autism Spectrum Disorder, Resting-state fMRI, Salience Network, Functional connectivity


## Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by deficits in communication and social interaction, repetitive behaviors, and restricted interests which become apparent at an early age [ 1 , 2 ]. In recent years, using modern non-invasive functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) techniques, a growing number of studies have shown that particular symptom manifestations of ASD are associated with alterations in the functioning and connectivity of brain networks [ 3 – 5 ]. Promising results seem to come from studies of resting state fMRI (rs-fMRI), a technique based on low-frequency fluctuations of the blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal in the absence of external stimuli [ 6 ]. Specifically, it is a task-free technique that allows the examination of spontaneous functional activity across brain areas [ 7 ]. Previous rs-fMRI studies have demonstrated abnormalities in functional connectivity (FC) in ASD, identifying both hyper- and hypo-connectivity in several networks, especially those involved in social-cognitive and affective processes [ 8 , 9 ], such as the Salience Network (SN) [ 10 , 11 ]. The main cortical nodes of the SN include the anterior insula (AI) [ 10 ], anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), rostral prefrontal cortex (RPFC), and supramarginal gyrus (SMG) [ 12 ]. This network is involved in multiple functions including attention, interoception, and subjective awareness [ 13 ], playing a key role in detecting and selecting salient stimuli, including those of a social nature. The SN is also involved in integrating external sensory information with internal emotional, cognitive, sensory, and bodily information relevant to guide behavior [ 14 , 15 ] and plays a switching role between two other fundamental networks, namely the Default Mode Network (DMN) and the Central Executive Network (CEN). According to some authors, a dysfunction of the SN within the DMN-SN-CEN architecture and interactions may be responsible for some of the clinical features of autism, including the presence of internalizing symptoms and reduced emotional awareness [ 16 – 18 ]. In the literature, the SN dysfunction hypothesis has been proposed as a neurocognitive model able to explain the interaction between sensory problems and social difficulties in autism [ 19 , 20 ]. The role of the SN in autistic symptomatology seems to be evident from childhood. Tsang and collaborators [ 21 ] suggested that early disruption of SN connectivity could impair processes that guide the perception of salient social stimuli, such as the human face, by conferring greater salience to lower-level non-social stimuli [ 21 ]. These potential aberrations in the FC of the SN could contribute to the appearance of typical features of the autism spectrum, including social communicative deficits and sensory abnormalities [ 21 ], impairing adaptive and flexible behavior [ 13 , 22 ]. Some studies report hypoconnectivity in the SN in children, adolescents, and adults with autism, especially between AI, prefrontal sensory regions, and emotion processing areas, such as the amygdala [ 23 – 25 ]. On the other hand, in ASD children hyperconnectivity was found within the SN and between the insula and striatal/retrosplenial regions [ 13 , 26 ]. The literature, therefore, points to an effect of age on SN connectivity in ASD [ 24 , 27 ]. A study conducted by Abbott and collaborators [ 23 ] on children and adolescents with autism found that the baseline symptomatology of ASD, namely sensory symptoms and socio-communicative impairments, was predominantly related to connectivity within the SN. Furthermore, SN connectivity patterns have been used to discriminate ASD children from typically developing (TD) children, demonstrating 83% accuracy [ 13 ]. Overall, one of the most supported hypotheses is that an atypical FC within the SN in individuals with ASD could underlie the differences, compared to TD individuals, in the detection of “salience” towards the social world and external environment [ 11 , 15 , 28 – 30 ]. This could therefore explain the atypical focus of attention on extraneous sensory stimuli rather than relevant social cues [ 31 ], altered social information processing [ 29 ], difficulties in self-awareness and emotion processing [ 32 ], and hypo- or hypersensitivity in autism [ 24 ]. Although numerous studies have highlighted the role of the SN in autistic symptomatology, most have primarily focused on developmental age and the specific function of the insula. This is compounded by a lack of consensus regarding the specificity of FC abnormalities in ASD [ 23 ]. Therefore, further studies seem to be needed to clarify the differences between ASD and TD in the FC of the SN and how this is related to autism symptoms. Based on these premises, using rs-fMRI data from the Autism Brain Imaging Data Exchange II (ABIDE II), the aim of our study was twofold: (1) to analyze differences in FC between ASD and TD male adults by focusing on specific regions of the SN, i.e. ACC, AI, RPFC, and SMG; (2) to explore how differences in FC between ASD and TD are related to social and behavioral difficulties as measured by the Social Responsiveness Scale-2nd edition (SRS-2) [ 33 ] and how functional connectivity of the group with ASD is related to symptom severity as measured by the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule-2nd edition (ADOS-2) [ 34 ].


## Methods


### Participants

The study was conducted using rs-fMRI data from the ABIDE II (http://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/abide/abide_II.html), an open-access repository aggregating behavioral, rs-fMRI and structural magnetic resonance imaging data from 19 different laboratories. We selected the Barrow Neurological Institute (BNI) database comprising 58 male individuals (age range 18–64 years) of whom 29 with ASD [mean age = 37.4; SD = 16.1, n = 13 (18–25 years), n = 16 (40–62 years)] and 29 age, and gender-matched TD individuals [mean age = 39.6; SD = 15.1, n = 10 (18–25 years), n = 19 (40–64 years)]. ASD diagnosis was based on the cut-off of the ADOS-2 [ 34 ] and all ASD participants have general intellectual abilities one standard deviation above or below the mean, as measured by the Kaufman Brief Intelligences Test-2 (KBIT-2) [ 35 ]. All participants were right-handed. Exclusion criteria for both ASD and TD groups were incompatibility with MRI scanning and intelligence quotient (IQ) score > 1 standard deviation below the mean at KBIT-2. In addition, TD individuals were also assessed for their past or current neurological and psychiatric history, close family members with ASD, or other medical illnesses that could alter brain functioning. ### Behavioral and clinical assessment

Participants underwent several neurocognitive, behavioral, and clinical measures. In our study, we focused on autism severity scores and social difficulties measured respectively through the ADOS-2– Module 4 [ 34 ] and SRS-2 [ 33 ].

The ADOS-2 [ 34 ] represents the gold standard diagnostic tool and consists of the semi-structured assessment of aspects of communication, social interaction, and restricted and repetitive behavior, considered the three fundamental symptom areas of autism. Higher scores indicate greater symptom severity and for each area a cut-off is indicated, beyond which an autism diagnosis is suggested. In Module 4, the cut-off values for the diagnosis of ASD are a score of two or more in the communication component, a score of four or more in the social interaction component, and a score of seven or more in the communication + social component (Total Score).

The SRS-2 [ 33 ] is a 65-item rating scale that measures the severity of deficits in social behavior associated with ASD. The SRS-2 is composed of 5 sub-scales based on diagnostic criteria for ASD: Social Awareness (e.g., ability to recognize social cues), Social Cognition (e.g., ability to interpret social behaviors), Social Communication (e.g., reciprocal communication in social situations), Social Motivation (e.g., desire to participate in social interactions), and Restricted Interests and Repetitive Behaviors (e.g., circumscribed interests and stereotypy). Items are scored on a 4-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 (not true) to 4 (almost always true). Items are summed together and converted to T-score (Total T-Score) with a maximum sum of 195 points. T-scores less than or equal to 59 are considered within the normal range and generally not associated with clinically significant ASD; T-scores of 60–65 are considered in the mild range, suggesting mild-moderate deficits in social interaction; T-scores of 66–75 are considered clinically significant and indicate moderate social impairment. A Total T-Score greater than or equal to 75 is considered severe and strongly associated with a clinical diagnosis of ASD.


### Descriptive analysis

Two-sample independent t-test was used to compare the ASD and TD groups for age, IQ, and SRS-2 scores. The analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS 27.0). All results were considered significant at p ≤ 0.05.


### Imaging data Acquisition

The scans were acquired with a 3.0 Tesla Philips Ingenia with a 15-channel head coil (http://fcon_1000.projects.nitrc.org/indi/abide/scan_params/ABIDEII-BNI_1_scantable.pdf). MRI scan included 3D-T1 weighted (matrix size = 244 × 227; field of view [FOV] = 270 × 252 mm; slice thickness = 1.2 mm; flip angle = 9°; number of slices = 170; slice orientation = axial) and resting-state images (echo planar imaging [EPI]: echo time = 25ms; repetition time = 3000ms; flip angle = 80°; matrix size = 64 × 64; FOV = 240 × 240 mm; slice thickness = 4 mm; number of slices = 50; number of volumes = 120, slice orientation = axial). During the resting-state scan, participants were instructed to remain with their eyes closed, avoid falling asleep, and without thinking about anything in particular.


### Imaging data analysis


#### Preprocessing

DICOM images were converted to NIfTI with MRIConverter, (release 2.1.0; https://lcni.uoregon.edu ). MRI data were preprocessed using CONN Toolbox (release 21.b) [ 36 ], SPM12 (release 12.6) [ 37 ] and MATLAB (release R2022b) [ 38 ]. Functional and anatomical data were preprocessed using a flexible preprocessing pipeline [ 39 ] including realignment with correction of susceptibility distortion interactions, slice timing correction, outlier detection, direct segmentation, MNI-space normalization, and smoothing. Functional data were realigned using SPM realign & unwarp procedure [ 40 ], where all scans were coregistered to a reference image (first scan of the first session) using a least squares approach and a 6-parameter (rigid body) transformation [ 41 ], and resampled using b-spline interpolation. Temporal misalignment between different slices of the functional data (acquired in ascending order) was corrected following SPM slice-timing correction procedure [ 42 , 43 ], using sinc temporal interpolation to resample each slice BOLD time-series to a common mid-acquisition time. Potential outlier scans were identified using ART [ 44 ] as acquisitions with framewise displacement above 0.9 mm or global BOLD signal changes above 5 standard deviations [ 45 ], and for each subject, a reference BOLD image was computed by averaging all scans, excluding outliers. Functional and anatomical data were normalized into standard MNI space, segmented into grey matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tissue classes, and resampled to 2 mm isotropic voxels following a direct normalization procedure [ 36 , 46 ] using SPM unified segmentation and normalization algorithm [ 47 , 48 ]. Finally, spatial convolution with a Gaussian kernel of 8 mm full-width half maximum (FWHM) was used to smooth the functional data.

Furthermore, a standard denoising pipeline [ 49 ] was used to denoise the functional data that includes regression of potential confounding effects characterized by physiological noise components (10 CompCor noise components for white matter and5 CompCor noise components for CSF) estimated by CompCor [ 50 , 51 ], motion parameters and their first order derivatives (12 factors), and ART for scrubbing, and effect of rest [ 52 ] within each functional run. A bandpass frequency filtering of the BOLD timeseries [ 53 ] was set between 0.008 Hz or above 0.09 Hz to minimize possible signal noise interference. Then, we performed two sets of analyses: a Region of Interest (ROI)-to-ROI connectivity (RRC) analysis and a Seed-based connectivity (SBC) analysis.


#### RRC and SBC analysis

To explore functional connectivity among SN nodes (Fig. 1 a), RRC metrics were estimated across the predetermined SN ROIs in CONN - obtained through independent component analysis on the Human Connectome Project dataset [ 36 ], namely the ACC [ 12 , 54 , 55 ], right and left AI [ 12 , 54 , 55 ], the right and left RPFC [ 12 ], and the right and left SMG [ 12 ].

For each subject, the time series of voxels in each ROI were averaged and the strength of functional connectivity for each pair of ROIs was obtained based on Fisher-transformed bivariate correlation coefficients of a general half-weighted linear model [ 56 ].

To examine the whole-brain connectivity of SN, SBC analyses were performed for each subject. Specifically, 7 separate SBC analyses were performed, one for each SN ROIs that was considered a separate seed. For each subject and each SBC analysis, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated between the seed averaged time series and all other voxels of the brain.


#### Group-level analyses

At the second level of analysis, participants’ RRC matrices and SBC maps were entered into two separate General Linear Models (GLM) [ 57 ], respectively. In both RRC and SBC analyses, we performed a between-group contrast (ASD > TD) to explore possible differences between ASD and TD in the FC of each region of SN. To examine the relationship between FC changes in the SN and severity of social deficits, the scores obtained by the participants of the two groups at each sub-scale of the SRS-2 were entered as covariates through a one-way ANCOVA covariate interaction. Specifically, we performed a between-group analysis considering ASD > TD contrast. In addition, through regression analysis, we examined the effect of the scores of each ADOS-2 subscale on the FC of ASDs. This analysis was limited to the ASD group only, as the TD group was not administered ADOS-2.

For the RRC analyses, the results considered statistically significant are: (1) cluster formation threshold p < 0.05 at the voxel level (p-uncorrected, two-tailed); (2) familywise corrected cluster size threshold p-FDR < 0.05. For all the analyses of SBC, the results considered statistically significant are: (1) cluster-forming p < 0.005 voxel-level threshold (p-uncorrected, two-tailed); (2) familywise corrected p-FDR < 0.05 cluster-size threshold [ 58 – 60 ]. For the results of SBC analyses, we considered the pick region in the cluster.


## Results


### Descriptive analysis

Groups did not differ in age ( t (56) = -0.52; p = 0.60) or IQ ( t (56) = -1.37; p = 0.18). As expected, the total and subscale scores of the SRS-2 (i.e., Awareness, Cognition, Communication, Motivation, and Restricted Interests and Repetitive Behaviors) were significantly higher ( p < 0.001 for all comparisons) in the ASD group compared to the TD group (see Table 1 ). For the ASD group, the mean ADOS-2 total score was 11.39 (SD = 3.48) while the TD group did not fill out the diagnostic measure.


### Resting state functional connectivity


#### Group differences in functional connectivity

RRC analysis revealed no significant differences in FC between ASD and TD. On the contrary, SBC analyses showed significant differences between the two groups (Table 2 ). Left and right RPFC showed a reduced FC to the left cerebellum ( p = 0.002 and p = 0.001 respectively), specifically to Lobule VI and Crus I in ASD individuals compared with TD individuals. Moreover, the right SMG presented an increase in FC ( p = 0.002) to the left anterior Middle Temporal Gyrus (MTG) and the right Angular Gyrus (AG) in the ASD individuals relative to the TD individuals (see Fig. 1 b).

#### Salience network connectivity and autistic symptoms

RRC analyses showed no significant differences in the FC between the ASD and TD groups for the SRS-2 subscales. In the SBC analysis, we found group differences in the association between the FC of the insula and some autistic traits measured by the SRS-2 (Table 3 ; Fig. 2 ). Specifically, compared to the TD group, the ASD group showed higher FC from the right AI to the left posterior division of the MTG associated with higher scores on Social Awareness subscales ( p = 0.001). Moreover, the ASD group showed greater FC from the right AI to the left temporal pole (TP) associated with higher scores on Social Motivation subscales compared to the TD group ( p = 0.001). Finally, greater FC was also found between the left AI and left posterior temporal fusiform cortex, associated with higher scores on Restricted Interests and Repetitive Behaviors subscales in the ASD group compared with the TD group ( p = 0.001).

## Discussion

Our study aimed to investigate the FC within key regions of the SN in ASD and to understand how differences in connectivity are associated with autism clinical features. We analyzed the rs-fMRI data from the ABIDE II dataset, comparing age– and sex-matched groups of ASD and TD individuals. Overall, the results of our SBC analysis indicate alterations in the FC of SN nodes in the ASD group, showing both an increase and decrease in connectivity, as reported in previous studies [ 16 , 61 , 62 ]. Furthermore, this result seems to align with what has been suggested in the literature [ 63 , 64 ] concerning an aberrant and idiosyncratic FC in autism rather than a condition related to an exclusive hyper- or hypoconnectivity. As suggested by Hull and collaborators [ 62 ] the heterogeneity of results could be related to the nature of ASD itself and the uniqueness of the clinical condition for each individual.

In our study, the ASD group showed reduced FC relative to the TD group between RPFC and an area usually associated with several cognitive deficits, namely the posterior cerebellum [ 65 ], in particular with Lobule VI and Crus I. Our results provide further support to the literature regarding abnormalities in cerebro-cerebellar connectivity in autism [ 66 – 68 ]. Notably, our findings align with recent studies that have highlighted the cerebellum’s involvement in various ASD symptoms, identifying this region as one of the areas hypoactivated during social processing, action observation, and social language [ 69 – 71 ]. RPFC, Crus I e Lobule VI are involved in different higher cognitive functions such as working memory, planning, decision-making, attention, mentalizing, and emotion regulation [ 66 , 67 , 72 , 73 ]. Reduced FC between these regions could explain some typical features of ASD, including difficulties in processing and responding appropriately to social and emotional stimuli, and consequently the impairment of social interaction abilities. Some studies have shown that both Crus I and Lobule VI have connections to the non-motor executive network and the SN [ 74 ]. Since both structures are involved in emotion processing and attention [ 75 ], these regions could contribute to the function of the SN by modulating attention towards emotionally salient stimuli or by helping to regulate emotional responses to such stimuli - processes that are impaired in autism. Lobule VI plays a key role in motor control, contributing to the correct planning and execution of movements, as well as monitoring sensory information [ 65 , 75 ]. Reduced FC between this specific area and the RPFC can impair the ability to plan and coordinate movements, also contributing to repetitive and stereotyped behavior [ 66 , 68 ].

We found an increased FC between the SMG and the MTG and AG regions, supporting the hypothesis of altered coordination among brain networks involved in attention regulation, emotions and internal state processing in autism [ 11 ]. This result was further confirmed when considering autistic symptomatology as measured by ADOS-2 in both the total score and the social interaction component. The SMG, MTG, and AG play a key role within the DMN [ 76 , 77 ] suggesting a possible alteration in the switching between the SN and DMN. This disruption may interfere with the appropriate shift from internally to externally oriented cognition in ASD [ 11 ]. Hyperconnectivity between these regions could explain an increased sensitivity to environmental signals and the subsequent difficulty in filtering out distractions and directing attention to relevant stimuli, including social stimuli [ 11 ]. These functions are indeed mediated by both the SMG and AG [ 78 , 79 ]. For instance, the AG operates as an intermodal center for combining and integrating multisensory information, directing attention to key information, understanding environmental events, manipulating mental representations, and solving problems [ 79 , 80 ]. Moreover, it is involved in the mentalizing process [ 81 ]. The MTG is a key component of the social brain and several studies have shown an association with ASD symptomatology, although the literature reports conflicting results regarding the hyper- or hypoconnectivity of this region [ 82 ]. Previous studies [ 82 – 84 ] have demonstrated that the bilateral posterior MTG (pMTG) is particularly vulnerable in both children and adults with ASD compared to TD individuals, highlighting its role in communicative, social, and mentalization deficits. However, differently from previous studies that identified hypoconnectivity of the pMTG with various brain regions [ 82 ], we observed increased connectivity. Specifically, our results showed that hyperconnectivity between the left pMGT and the right AI was associated with higher scores on the SRS-2 social awareness subscale, which refers to the ability to detect social cues. The AI is involved in the conscious representation of emotions in self and others and is therefore essential for interpersonal behavior [ 85 ]. Along with the MTG, the AI contributes to the empathic encoding of others’ emotional experiences [ 86 ], as well as in recognizing and understanding their mental states and decoding facial expressions [ 87 ]. As noted by Haghighat and collaborators [ 61 ], much of the literature emphasizes hypoconnectivity of the insula in ASD as a main cause of social and emotional deficits [ 88 ]. However, recently this assumption has been challenged by evidence of overconnectivity [ 13 , 89 – 91 ]. We hypothesize that hyperconnectivity between the regions that regulate key aspects of social interaction, as suggested by our results, could compromise the proper decoding of social contexts, including difficulty in discriminating between external and internal information, resulting in information overload. This finding also seems to be closely linked to the aspects of social motivation, defined as the drive to engage in social and interpersonal behavior. In this context, we observed that individuals with autism who experienced greater difficulty in engaging in social behavior exhibited increased FC between the right AI and the left temporal pole.

An interesting result involves the Restricted Interests and Repetitive Behaviors subscale score of SRS-2, where greater severity seems to be associated with increased FC between the left AI and left fusiform cortex in the ASD group. Several studies have shown that the fusiform area, also known as the fusiform gyrus, exhibits atypical responses in individuals with ASD [ 92 , 93 ]. However, while most researchers have focused on the role of the fusiform cortex in social impairment [ 69 , 94 ], its potential involvement in repetitive and stereotyped behavior and interests has been less explored. The fusiform cortex is primarily implicated in visual perception, particularly in the processing and recognition of faces [ 95 ]. Nevertheless, studies have shown that this area also responds selectively to stimuli for which individuals exhibit high levels of visual identification expertise [ 96 , 97 ]. Indeed, some studies suggest that in individuals with autism, the fusiform face area may respond to non-social topics and stimuli that arouse intense interest [ 96 , 98 , 99 ]. On one hand, our results appear to support this line of research, leading to the hypothesis that a hyper-connectivity between the fusiform cortex and the AI could explain the intense focus on specific objects or topics of interest in autism, often associated with an emotional and reward value, as demonstrated in the literature [ 100 , 101 ]. On the other hand, our results could also explain the enactment of repetitive behaviors, hypersensoriality, and attention to detail as compensatory mechanisms in response to visual information overload [ 102 ].

It is interesting to note that the results of our study, in line with previous research [ 31 , 103 , 104 ], demonstrate atypical connectivity between specific regions of the SN and predominantly temporal structures which overall play a key role in social cognition skills [ 73 , 105 ] and complex cognitive processes such as attention and working memory [ 78 ]. These abnormalities could explain difficulties in attention and processing of salient stimuli, especially those of an affective and social nature, with an impact on emotional and behavioral regulation and integration [ 106 , 107 ]. This hypothesis is further supported by the results obtained considering the autistic symptoms measured through the ADOS-2. Indeed, we found decreased FC between the right AI and the anterior division of the right ITG which correlated with greater impairments in social interaction and higher total scores on the ADOS-2. Reduced FC between these two regions could negatively affect the ability to understand and communicate social and emotional information during interpersonal conversation, including recognizing and expressing emotions through facial expressions and appropriately modulating gaze, all functions regulated by the AI and the ITG [ 88 , 108 , 109 ]. The ITG is also implicated in fluent language [ 109 ], which is a pre-requisite for administering Module 4 of the ADOS-2 [ 110 ]. This aspect may be impaired even in high-functioning autism, particularly when engaging in conversations that involve abstract content, such as describing or understanding emotions [ 111 ]. While we observed reduced FC between brain structures associated with socio-emotional processing in relation to increased autistic symptoms, our results suggest greater engagement of areas typically implicated in visual recognition, spatial cognition, and motor control. Specifically, we found increased FC between the ACC and the lateral occipital cortex, which was associated with higher ADOS-2 scores, including both total score and social interaction. This result could reflect an alternative pathway for processing social and emotional information in autism, relying on a more “favorite mode” that involves visual memory and heightened focus on non-social details rather than the social aspects of interaction [ 112 , 113 ]. Finally, an interesting result concerns the higher FC between the SMG and the preCG in association with a higher score in the communication subscale of the ADOS-2. This result aligns with existing literature on alterations of brain structures involved in gesture production and comprehension [ 114 , 115 ], an aspect assessed in the communication subscale of the ADOS-2. More broadly, it reflects difficulties in motor and imitative abilities which seem to play a role in the social and communication impairments [ 115 ].

In conclusion, our preliminary study identified atypical FC between core regions of SN and structures that play a key role in social cognition (e.g., Theory of Mind skills, empathy, emotion recognition) and in complex cognitive processes (e.g., attention, working memory, and executive functions) in ASD adults male. Additionally, our results suggest the presence of alternative pathways for encoding social information and increased engagement of structures typically associated with visuospatial rather than social processes. We propose that such anomalies may explain both the socio-affective and behavioral impairments in autism, including the manifestation of repetitive and stereotyped behaviors, as well as sensory difficulties. Finally, although we mainly observe a trend toward hyperconnectivity in ASDs compared to TDs, we also identify patterns of hypoconnectivity, contributing to the hypothesis of greater variability and idiosyncratic FC in adulthood with ASD [ 61 – 64 ].

Understanding the functioning of brain networks and structures in ASD, along with how their interactions contribute to symptomatology, could have important implications for both diagnosis and treatment. For example, our findings support the need to ensure environments where potentially distracting inputs are minimized, aiming to capture attention through strategies and stimuli that are more attractive and interesting for the ASD individuals. From a clinical-therapeutic perspective, virtual reality and robotic technologies hold significant potential, acting as tools that are often well-received by individuals with ASD due to their ability to provide customizable, safe, and predictable environments [ 116 ]. It has been suggested [ 99], that social robots act as simplified versions of people, enabling ASD individuals to more effectively filter meaningful perceptual stimuli and process social information than more complex human stimuli, which may be perceived as confusing or overwhelming. For example, integrating these devices into therapeutic practice could facilitate a gradual transition of attention from an inanimate, non-social object to a more complex social stimulus, allowing to avoid or reduce emotional and cognitive overload. In addition, the variability of FC observed in ASDs supports the importance of an individualized clinical approach to ASD treatment, which should be modulated according to the specific profiles of the person. It would be useful to monitor changes in FC following specific treatments aimed at emotional, social, and behavioral aspects.

We are aware that our study has limitations. The main limitation is the small sample size, which reduces the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, our sample includes only male participants. The choice to perform the analysis on a male-only sample is primarily related to their overrepresentation in autism [ 117 , 118 ]. In addition, this approach eliminates the gender effect on the results, allowing for a more accurate study of FC in the selected sample. However, future investigations should also include female participants to explore possible gender differences in a larger sample.
