## Abstract

In comparative evolutionary genomics, faster or slower evolution of a particular gene, site, or branch in a phylogenetic tree, when compared to the appropriate average, has been interpreted as evidence of conservation, functional importance, or adaptation. With large consortia generating hundreds of genomes, there is an opportunity to interrogate these datasets for evidence of accelerated or reduced evolutionary rates in protein-coding genes associated with the presence or absence of a given phenotype (e.g. marine vs terrestrial, nocturnal vs diurnal). Such rate shifts can reflect the molecular basis of convergent phenotypic adaptation when they occur repeatedly across independent lineages. Here we introduce an explicit phylogenetic rate test, MoleRate , for acceleration or reduction of nucleotide or protein evolutionary rates in focal lineages vs the rest of the phylogeny. Compared to existing methods, MoleRate offers execution, explicit likelihood-based hypothesis testing, and the ability to detect and filter out potentially aberrant signal from single lineages. We demonstrate MoleRate ’s performance on simulated and empirical data, and apply it to several mammalian phenotypes. We also highlight its visualization capabilities, which enable exploration and communication of results. These analyses show that MoleRate detects biologically significant enrichments in selective pressure on specific functions related to the given phenotype, and that enrichments in selective pressure related to the given phenotype, absent when random lineages are tested.


## Introduction.

In adaptation studies, convergently evolving phenotypes serve as natural replicates for evolutionary change across the tree of life. Phenotypic convergence occurs when different species or populations independently evolve similar traits, often due to shared selective pressures ( Stern 2013 ). At the molecular level, phenotypic convergence can arise through identical amino acid changes, different substitutions within the same gene, or distinct genes providing similar adaptive functions. While convergence at a single amino acid is assumed to be rare ( Zhang and Kumar 1997 ), gene-level convergence provides increased statistical power for detecting adaptive evolution. One powerful way to detect such patterns is by examining lineage-specific shifts in molecular evolutionary rates, which can signal changes in selective constraint or adaptive evolution.

Methods for detecting convergence focus either on identifying directional selection at specific amino acid sites ( Parker et al. 2013 ; Parto and Lartillot 2018 ; Rey et al. 2018 ) or detecting selection acting on the same gene across species, even if different sites are involved ( Martin et al. 2021 ; Wirthlin et al. 2024 ). Codon-based tools, such as HyPhy ( Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2020 ; Wisotsky et al. 2020 ) and codeml ( Yang 2007 ), are widely used to detect gene-level positive selection and convergent evolution ( Shultz and Sackton 2019 ; Barkdull and Moreau 2023 ; Zehr et al. 2023 ). However, genome-wide applications of these approaches require substantial computational resources, often necessitating parallelized analyses on computing clusters. Evolutionary rate-based methods, such as RERconverge, PhyloAcc, TRACCER and TraitRate ( Mayrose and Otto 2011 ; Hu et al. 2019 ; Kowalczyk et al. 2019 ; Treaster et al. 2021 ), offer a less computationally demanding alternative by detecting shifts in molecular rates across genes. Relative rate approaches have been used since before the beginning of the modern genomic era ( Muse and Gaut 1997 ), and are computationally relatively cheap ( Kowalczyk et al. 2019 ). Such methods assume that adaptive evolution is typically followed by purifying selection, resulting in lineage-specific rate shifts ( Chikina et al. 2016 ).

While evolutionary rate-based methods are a computationally efficient alternative to codon models, they rely on assumptions that can be improved upon. In particular, RERconverge, one of the most widely used approaches, is best suited for large genome-wide datasets and performs rate testing in a two-step framework: estimating rates and then testing them post hoc. This separation can limit power and fails to account for parameter uncertainty.

Here, we introduce MoleRate , a likelihood model-based method designed to detect shifts in evolutionary rates on subsets of phylogenetic tree branches compared to a genome average tree. Implemented in HyPhy, MoleRate is similar in concept to RERconverge , with the key difference that RERconverge performs “standard” regression-type testing on branch length estimates, whereas MoleRate performs “phylogenetic” regression directly as a part of the fitting process. MoleRate tests accommodate several models of rate variation, and implements a procedure to filter out cases when rate variation is attributable to too few outlier branches. We demonstrate the method on several previously studied cases of convergent evolution and simulated data. MoleRate includes intuitive, adaptable JavaScript -based visualization notebooks for interactive result interpretation. Users can efficiently explore rate shifts across genes and lineages and customize the framework for specific datasets and research questions.


## Methods.

Given a protein or a nucleotide sequence alignment MoleRate estimates relative evolutionary rates on a designated (focal) part of a phylogenetic tree and performs a series of nested hypothesis tests to determine if rates vary between the focal part of the tree and the rest of the tree, or within the focal branches. If an overall test for rate differences is significant, additional tests can be performed at the level of individual branches to screen for cases where only a single (or a few) branches contribute to the overall difference signal, as an outlier detection test.

## Hypothesis testing and interpretation.

MoleRate conducts pairwise likelihood ratio hypothesis tests between nested models ( Table 2 ). A Holm-Bonferroni multiple testing correction is applied to raw test p-values to control the Type I family-wise error rate.

Hypothesis testing in MoleRate . L :number of labeled branches; N :total number of branches; (*) when fitten.

| Null model | Alternative model | D.F. | Interpretation |
| Proportional | Proportional Partitioned | 1 | Evolution is accelerated or slowed on B compared to the background. Gene-wide rate shift on test branches. |
| Proportional | Unconstrained Test | L | Evolutionary rates are different from the overall gene relative rate on B ; Rate heterogeneity across test branches. |
| Proportional | Unconstrained* | N | Branch lengths are not proportional to reference. General rate heterogeneity across the tree. |
| Proportional Partitioned | Unconstrained Test | L-1 | Branch lengths on B are not proportional to reference. Rate heterogeneity within test branches. |
| Proportional Partitioned | Unconstrained* | N-2 | Group relative rates are constant within the groups. Rate heterogeneity within test or background branches (or both). |
| Unconstrained Test | Unconstrained* | N-L-1 | Branch lengths are not proportional to reference on background. Rate heterogeneity within background branches. |
The key interpretive hypothesis is Proportional (null) vs Proportional Partitioned (alternative). Given two branch groups, labeled and background, the test determines whether the group level rates (slopes in Figure 2B ) are different or not. In evolutionary terms, this test determines if the labeled group evolves at a rate that is slower or faster than the background group. For example, if the branches in the labeled group have a unique phenotype, accelerated protein evolution could indicate positive selection (or relaxation of constraint), and slowed evolution could indicate greater conservation.


## Outlier detection.

When testing Proportional (null) vs Proportional Partitioned (alternative), a significant test result that the relative evolutionary rates between labeled and background branches are different may arise due to the influence of a single or a few branches; much like an outlier effect in linear regression (see an example in Results ). MoleRate implements a simple branch-by-branch testing procedure for such outlier detection. For each branch from the labeled set, b k ⊂ B , we fit the Proportional + 1 model ( Table 1 ). Two LR tests (each with a single degree of freedom) are then performed; p-values are corrected using the Holm-Bonferroni procedure.

Proportional (null) vs Proportional + 1 (alternative). A significant result from this test implies that the relative rate on the single branch b k , is different from the background relative rate of the tree. This identifies the branch as having a significant individual rate shift.

Proportional + 1 (null) vs Unrestricted Test (alternative). A significant result from this test implies that labeled branches in B , except b k , have sufficiently different relative compared to the background relative rate. This tests for a signal of convergence across the labeled set even after the potential outlier status of b k is accounted for.

We classify individual branches as follows

Uninformative. Neither test is significant, indicating that b k does not have a significant individual rate shift, nor does its inclusion/exclusion significantly impact the overall convergence result.

Informative. Both tests are significant, indicating that b k and the remaining labeled branches differ from the background.

Outlier. Only the Single Branch test is significant, suggesting that b k alone drives the signal of rate difference.


## Implementation.

MoleRate is implemented in HyPhy versions 2.5.71 or later ( Kosakovsky Pond et al. 2020 ). MoleRate is executed using the hyphy molerate command. MoleRate requires a phylogenetic tree, an input gene alignment, and phenotype annotations to specify foreground and background branches. More details for specific analysis options can be found in the HyPhy Analyses GitHub repository ( https://github.com/veg/hyphy-analyses/tree/master/molerate ). Alignments are analyzed one at a time, and each analysis produces a machine readable JSON file, documented in the linked repository. Each analysis is configured with the following options, also documented in the repository.

A protein or nucleotide alignment to analyze.

A species tree with branch lengths to be used as reference. MoleRate will compute the intersection of species between the tree and the alignment (case insensitive) and reduce both accordingly. If internal branches are deleted from the tree, their lengths are added to the adjacent branch to maintain relative levels of divergence.

A list of focal species. At least one of these species must be in the intersection computed in step 2.

Internal branch labeling strategy

Model options: substitution matrix, site-to-site rate variation.

Include the Unrestricted model? Yes/No

Perform branch level outlier/informative testing? Yes/No

Individual JSON files may be viewed and explored using the following ObservableHQ notebook https://observablehq.com/@hyphy/molerate . A Python 3 script included in the repository can be used to process directories of MoleRate JSON files and output summary CSV files for subsequent processing or visualization using https://observablehq.com/@hyphy/molerate-summary .

## Outlier detection.

To evaluate whether significant rate shifts were driven by single lineages or shared among multiple branches, we applied MoleRate ’s outlier detection procedure ( Figure S5 ). Among the significantly accelerated genes, LIM2 (lens intrinsic membrane protein 2) represents a well supported case: all three tested (subterranean) branches provide strong evidence for rate acceleration. The hypotheses that each branch has the same rate as the background ( One Branch test) can be rejected, and the hypotheses that the remaining branches ( All but this branch test) has the same rate as the background can be similarly rejected. In contrast, SEPT7 (Filament-forming cytoskeletal GTPase), a single branch ( Star-nosed mole ) provides the entire rate difference signal ( R L ∕ R θ > 4 ) ; notably there is no evidence that the remaining two branches evolve at rates different from background. A manual inspection of the corresponding gene alignment shows an obvious sequence assembly/alignment error in this species towards the 3’ end of the gene. Masking this misalignment completely removes the rate difference signal (results not shown).


## Subterranean phenotype.

To further characterize the functional relationships among accelerated and decelerated genes in subterranean lineages, we performed protein–protein interaction network analyses using STRING v12.0 ( Szklarczyk et al. 2023 ). We uploaded the top accelerated ( Table S1 ) and decelerated ( Table S2 ) genes separately and examined enrichment for functional categories, biological processes, and pathways using default parameters (minimum required interaction score = 0.4, medium confidence; evidence from experiments, databases, and text mining included).

The top accelerated genes in the three subterranean branches are significantly enriched for eye formation, vision, and olfaction; most decelerated genes are also enriched for olfactory receptors and sensory perception ( Table 5 ). Loss of vision in subterranean mammals coupled with enhanced olfaction are well-documented in previous studies ( Nevo 1979 ; Partha et al. 2017 ; Davies et al. 2018 ); these and other studies also reported immune, skin, morphological, and hypoxia-related adaptation. Genes involved in lens/eye development and olfaction are predominant at the top of the list for most accelerated genes ( Table S1 ). Acceleration of protein substitution rates would be consistent with two distinct processes: loss of function ( e.g. , due to loss of vision), leading to relaxation of selection, or gain of function/adaptation, leading to positive selection. Protein evolutionary rates alone cannot differentiate between these two scenarios, and other tests, e.g. , based on dN/dS or other adaptive evolution measures are traditionally used for this purpose. Deceleration of protein evolutionary rates in subterranean species could be interpreted as intensified purifying selection, possibly following a burst of adaptation ( Table S2 ).

## Marine phenotype.

The top accelerated genes in the five marine branches are significantly enriched for olfactory receptor activity and sensory perception ( Table 5 ). The top decelerated genes are related to heterochromatin assembly and regulation, sperm motility and fertilization. Relaxation of constraint on genes related to olfaction is a well-characterized evolutionary process for marine mammals ( Kishida and Thewissen 2012 ; Chikina et al. 2016 ; Farina et al. 2024 ). Gene ontology and pathway enrichment were analyzed using STRING-DB (minimum required interaction score = 0.4, medium confidence; evidence from experiments, databases, and text mining included), following the same procedure as for the marine phenotype.

For marine accelerated genes, 82/120 genes were experiencing significantly relaxed selection (RELAX K<1) compared to the rest of the lineages at p≤0.05 (45/120 at p≤0.00001), consistent with loss of constraint being a major factor in rate acceleration ( Table S3 ). Five genes, however, showed evidence for intensified selection pressure (K>1 and p≤0.05): NT5DC4, ZNF835, SBSN, ZNF416, OR4S1 .

On the other hand, for marine decelerated genes, 25/148 genes were experiencing significantly intensified selection (RELAX K>1) compared to the rest of the lineages at p≤0.05 (1/148 at p≤0.00001, Table S4 ). Two of the genes showed evidence for relaxed selection pressure (K<1 and p≤0.05): SHISA8 and CNST .


## Hairless phenotype.

The top accelerated genes in the ten hairless branches are significantly enriched for terms and pathways related to epidermis and hair formation, olfactory receptor activity, an infection pathway, and an estrogen signaling pathway ( Table 5 ). The top decelerated genes in the same branches are enriched for the Herpes simplex virus 1 infection KEGG pathway. Gene ontology and pathway enrichment were analyzed using STRING-DB (minimum required interaction score = 0.4, medium confidence; evidence from experiments, databases, and text mining included), following the same procedure as for the marine and subterranean phenotypes.

For hairless accelerated genes, 30/39 genes were experiencing significantly relaxed selection (RELAX K<1) compared to the rest of the lineages at p≤0.05 (24/39 at p≤0.00001), consistent with loss of constraint being a major factor ( Table S5 ). On the other hand, for marine decelerated genes, 20/37 genes were experiencing significantly intensified selection (RELAX K>1) compared to the rest of the lineages at p≤0.05 (6/37 at p≤0.00001), consistent with a significant role for constraint for slower rates ( Table S6 ). One gene ( CNST ) in this group showed significant evidence of relaxation (p≤0.05). Relaxation of constraint on genes and pathways involved with hair formation and development in hairless mammals is an obvious and well-supported result ( Nery et al. 2014 ; Sun et al. 2017 ; Kowalczyk et al. 2022 ). Loss of constraint on olfactory genes is most likely a confounder with the marine mammal phenotype (also hairless). Because we required that ≥5 branches independently support acceleration/deceleration, the set of genes meeting selection criteria was much smaller than for the other two phenotypes.


## Random subset.

While many individual genes met the selection criteria, no functional or pathways categories were sufficiently enriched for an analysis using 6 randomly chosen subspecies, even though a substantial number of individual genes passed selection criteria ( Table 4 ).


## The effect of changing filtering criteria.

To assess how different post-analysis filtering choices influence downstream biological interpretation, we examined how the number and composition of genes identified as accelerated depend on the criteria used to define them. Specifically, we compared results obtained under progressively stricter definitions of convergence, requiring multiple informative branches and excluding outlier-driven cases. As a simple sensitivity analysis, we considered the size and composition of accelerated gene sets for the subterranean and marine phenotypes as a function of different selective criteria ( Table 6 ). Encouragingly, enrichment analyses are semantically congruent. Requiring more than one informative branch is similar, but not identical to requiring 0 outlier branches. More than 50% of genes with significantly accelerated rates are fully explained by the influence of a single branch (compare FDR ≤ 0.01 to other categories). Existing literature and the overall logic of convergent evolution analyses emphasizes events shared or repeated across different lineages/branches. How to balance enlarging the set of candidate genes (recall), and more targeted selection of candidate genes (precision) is a decision that needs to be made individually, study by study.

## Nucleotide vs amino-acid rates.

For comparison, we ran MoleRate on the subterranean phenotype but treating the alignments as nucleotide data. Because of lower levels of conservation in nucleotide space and the large influence of synonymous substitutions on branch lengths, it is not clear a priori what level of agreement one would expect between the two rate analyses. While the raw number of significantly accelerated/decelerated genes found by a nucleotide analysis is higher ( Table 4 ), filtering by multiple branches and outliers yields much smaller (~50%) gene sets compared to a protein analysis. Specifically, of the top 60 accelerated genes identified by the nucleotide analysis, 44 genes were also top accelerated genes in the amino acid subterranean test. Similarly, of the top 44 decelerated genes from the nucleotide analysis, 26 genes were top decelerated genes in the amino acid subterranean test. The smaller nucleotide gene sets are enriched for essentially the same categories as the corresponding protein gene sets.


## Computation performance.

Because MoleRate fits relatively simple models, and in many cases does not even need to estimate individual branch lengths, meaning its run times are modest. For example, the UCSC subterranean phenotype analysis required a median of 12 seconds / alignment, including outlier tests on an Apple M4 processor (2 threads). Given the embarrassing parallelism of the analysis, the entire genomic analysis would require <1 hour on a modern compute node with 256 total cores, for instance.

We compared the performance of MoleRate to RERconverge . Because RERconverge depends on background branches to estimate the mean and variance of evolutionary rates across the tree, we hypothesized that its performance may be reduced when the number of background species is small. Since MoleRate models branch-specific rates directly within a likelihood framework, we further hypothesized that it may be less sensitive to background size and could perform better under these conditions. To test these hypotheses, we analyzed the subterranean and hairless trait using both methods, comparing results on the full UCSC species set and on pruned species trees. Figure 4 shows precision-recall curves comparing the performance of RERConverge and MoleRate using the subterranean trait and a tree with 20 background species. These enrichment tests include four candidate gene sets, including: Lens Morphology Genes, Retina Morphology Genes, Eye Physiology Genes, and Expressed Ocular Genes. The phenotype-based sets were obtained from the MGI Mammalian Phenotype database via the Enrichr interface (cataract [MP:0001304] and abnormal lens morphology [MP:0001303] for lens; abnormal retina morphology [MP:0001325] for retina; abnormal eye physiology [MP:0005253], abnormal cone electrophysiology [MP:0004022], abnormal eye electrophysiology, and abnormal rod electrophysiology for physiology). The expressed ocular genes set were those with protein expression in the human eye confirmed by LC-MS/MS (Ahmad et al. 2018) and no expression outside ocular tissues in Human Protein Atlas (Yu et al. 2015; Karlsson et al. 2021), resulting in 65 eye-specific genes.

Precision–recall curves comparing the performance of RERConverge (black curve) and MoleRate (blue, green curves, representing results after FDR correction) on the subterranean phenotype using all background species, across four candidate gene sets (Lens Morphology, Retina Morphology, Eye Physiology, and Expressed Ocular). The x-axis shows recall, defined as the proportion of genes in each candidate set recovered as significant by the evolutionary analysis. The y-axis shows precision fold-change, calculated as the log-transformed enrichment of candidate genes among the significant results relative to expectation.

With only 20 background species, MoleRate recovered greater enrichment of candidate genes at similar levels of recall compared to RERconverge, particularly for the lens morphology and expressed ocular gene sets ( Figure 4 ). This advantage was even more pronounced when using MoleRate’s outlier testing, which ensures that signals are not driven by a single lineage but instead reflect convergence across multiple labeled branches. By contrast, when analyses were performed using the full species set, both methods showed more comparable performance (Figure S6).

When comparing the performance of two methods, interpretation is challenging because the “true” set of convergently evolving genes is not known in advance. In the subterranean case, it might seem encouraging if one method detects stronger signal among ocular genes, but more signal does not necessarily imply greater accuracy. What we can say with confidence is that both RERConverge and MoleRate produce similar overall performance curves, and both detect enough vision-related genes for enrichment analyses to consistently recover eye-related functions, with MoleRate being more precise when a small set of high-confidence genes is sought.


## Discussion.

We developed and benchmarked a phylogenetic tool, MoleRate , to identify genes or other alignable elements in multi-species genome-scale alignments, where nucleotide or amino acid (coding data only) substitution rates are significantly different between pre-designated sets of branches in the species tree. Using a reference tree with genome-averaged branch lengths makes this analysis fast and applicable to rapid screening of genomic datasets with modern scales. Finding genes where specific lineages/branches are evolving much faster or much slower than the rest of the tree is relevant for many biological applications, the key one being: which genes are associated with the development or a specific phenotype or trait in multiple independent branches or lineages?


## Comparison to existing relative rates methods

There are several existing tools and approaches for performing these types of analyses, either based on rate estimation, dN/dS analysis, or more complex phylogenetic regression. MoleRate approach is not conceptually new, but it offers several unique features that motivate its development. Compared to RERConverge , which takes estimated rates and performs post hoc analyses on them, MoleRate performs rate estimation and testing within the phylogenetic inference framework, thereby automatically accounting for parameter estimation uncertainty. Compared to dN/dS and Bayesian methods used in this context, MoleRate is several orders of magnitude faster (seconds per gene), and less sensitive to errors in alignment and model misspecification. MoleRate includes tests for outlier signal, so that reported rate acceleration or deceleration is robustly supported by multiple tree branches. It also directly estimates the effect size, i.e., the extent of acceleration or deceleration, and allows for more fine grained testing of within-group variation in rates. In addition, MoleRate is accompanied by extensive visualization tools that enable exploration of both individual gene analysis results and large-scale genome-wide analyses results. Our visualization tools extend well beyond what is typically offered by other similar tools.


## Performance on Simulated and Empirical Data

MoleRate performs well on simulated data, with power to detect acceleration and deceleration growing with effect size and alignment size, and this performance is minimally affected by substitution model misspecification, or some errors in how branches are labeled. Using an empirical benchmark dataset, a UCSC alignment of 62 mammal species, MoleRate reliably recovers many accelerated and decelerated genes found by previous studies using different approaches. It recovers very clean signals for enrichment of expected functional and molecular categories: vision (loss) and olfaction in subterranean species, olfaction (loss) in marine species, and hair (loss) in hairless species. Acceleration and deceleration in protein space is largely attributable to relaxation or intensification of selective constraint, validated here with a codon-based RELAX test. Top enriched categories are robust to changes in how genes are filtered, and even the use of nucleotide data for testing. An important observation following from MoleRate analyses is that as much as 50% of the statistically significant signals for rate changes are driven by a single branch (species), which could be indicative of alignment, assembly, or annotation errors or other aberrations.

The simplicity and speed of MoleRate coupled with its robust recovery of expected genes and features from extensively analyzed phenotypes position the tool as a useful addition for screening and evolutionary annotation of large genomic datasets. Production of candidate genes and pathways for further analyses and annotation in the context of phenotypic or convergent evolution is a useful hypothesis generation tool.
