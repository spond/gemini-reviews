## Abstract

Functional neuroimaging studies have described changes in local activity or in distributed connectivity in Major Depressive Disorder (MDD). We studied the interactions between these levels of neural organization to reveal novel pathobiological signatures of MDD. We scanned 21 unmedicated MDD subjects and 21 matched non-depressed controls at rest with simultaneous 18 fluorodeoxyglucose PET (FDG-PET, to measure local metabolic activity), and resting-state functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (rsfMRI, to measure hemodynamic functional connectivity, FC). We first identified regions of interest (ROIs) with increased (hypermetabolic) or decreased (hypometabolic) glucose metabolism in depressed compared to non-depressed subjects. Next, we used these hypermetabolic and hypometabolic regions as seed-based ROIs to derive FC maps, and compared these maps across depressed and non-depressed subjects. Depressed subjects demonstrated hypermetabolism in regions partially overlapping with the cingulo-opercular network, and hypometabolism in regions partially overlapping with the default network. In depressed subjects, hypermetabolic ROIs displayed stronger FC to portions of the frontostriatal salience network. Interestingly, hypometabolic ROIs displayed weaker FC with almost identical frontostriatal salience network regions. This work further emphasizes network-level metabolic and hemodynamic functional connectivity changes in MDD. More specifically, our findings suggest that local metabolic activity is up- or down-regulated in a distributed fashion in depression and that these metabolic changes are associated with comparable increases and decreases in functional coupling to the frontostriatal salience network—further bolstering the putative pathogenic importance of this network in MDD. ## Introduction

Several studies have employed Positron Emission Tomography (PET) and/or resting-state functional neuroimaging to contrast individuals with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) and non-depressed cohorts, with the goal of revealing neurobiological signatures of depression. For example, PET studies, such as those employing 18 fluorodeoxyglucose PET (FDG-PET), have revealed regions of increased and decreased resting metabolism in depressed subjects when compared to non-depressed controls. Common findings emerging from such studies include increased metabolism in the subgenual anterior cingulate cortex (sgACC) [ 1 – 7 ] and in the hippocampal formation [ 8 , 9 ], and decreased resting metabolism in dorsolateral prefrontal cortex [ 7 , 8 , 10 – 12 ] and in the anterior insula [ 13 ]. However, such findings are considerably variable, with other studies implicating abnormal metabolism in disparate regions [ 10 , 12 , 14 ], and still other studies implicating the same regions, but with opposing changes in resting metabolism observed (e.g., decreased sgACC resting metabolism) [ 10 , 15 ]. Similarly, network-level alterations in depression have been investigated using resting-state functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (rsfMRI) to measure differences in resting-state functional connectivity (FC) in depressed individuals. Such work has uncovered changes in the FC of canonical large-scale networks, most notably the default, salience, cingulo-opercular and frontoparietal control networks [ 16 – 21 ]. However, like the studies assessing resting metabolism in MDD, studies assessing abnormal resting-state FC in depression have yielded variable results.

The heterogeneity of findings in resting-state functional neuroimaging studies has impeded the development of systems-level models of MDD. It is possible that studying MDD across levels of neural organization (e.g., between the local and distributed levels) could better inform the pathobiology of depression [ 22 ]. For instance, abnormalities at the level of regional glucose metabolism could inform abnormalities at the level of distributed functional connectivity, and vice versa. Notably, local glucose consumption has been found to correlate with patterns of functional connectivity in healthy individuals [ 23 , 24 ]. However, to our knowledge, only one study has examined neural differences in MDD using both fMRI and FDG-PET on the same group of patients with depression, and this study focused on spatial similarities between local BOLD signal fluctuations (regional homogeneity) and local metabolic activity [ 10 ]. As such, we sought to explore the relationship between local metabolic activity and distributed hemodynamic connectivity in MDD. To do so, we imaged a group of 21 depressed individuals and 21 matched non-depressed controls with simultaneous FDG-PET and rsfMRI using a combined MR-PET scanner. We first asked what regions showed increased (hypermetabolism) or decreased (hypometabolism) metabolism in depressed versus non-depressed subjects. Next, we investigated how the FC of these hyper- and hypometabolic regions differed between the two groups. To accomplish this, we defined hyper- and hypometabolic regions-of-interest (ROIs) in depressed > non-depressed subjects. We then used these ROIs as seeds to derive FC maps and compared the resultant maps between the same two subject cohorts.

We hypothesized that metabolic and FC changes in depressed subjects would follow a topographical network pattern. Based on extant studies, we posited that resting metabolic activity would be higher in limbic and paralimbic regions of the default and salience networks, such as the frontal insula and sgACC [ 25 ]; and lower in prefrontal portions of the frontoparietal control network [ 1 , 8 ]. We also hypothesized that FC changes would mirror metabolic changes [ 23 ]—i.e., that hypermetabolic salience and default network nodes would increase their functional correlations with one another, while hypometabolic frontoparietal control network nodes would become less functionally inter-correlated.


## Materials and methods


## Subjects

42 subjects participated in this study: 21 depressed subjects (15 F, 6 M, mean age: 21.8 yrs., SD ± 3.1) and 21 age-matched non-depressed subjects (14 F, 7 M, mean age: 23.4 yrs., SD ± 2.6) (age group comparison on an unpaired t-test, p = 0.07). The diagnosis of MDD in depressed subjects was confirmed through the Structured Clinical Interview (SCID) for DSM-IV-TR for Axis I disorders [ 26 ]. Co-morbid personality disorders were not assessed. The SCID was administered by author MCE (a board-certified psychiatrist). MDD subjects were allowed to participate if they met criteria for co-morbid anxiety disorders, including Generalized Anxiety Disorder, Panic disorder with or without Agoraphobia, and Specific Phobias (e.g., Social Phobia)—or if they met criteria for co-morbid Obsessive Compulsive Disorder. Potential MDD subjects were excluded if they met criteria for a current hypomanic, manic or psychotic disorder. They were also thoroughly asked about any lifetime history of hypomanic or manic symptoms to rule out a possible bipolar diathesis. Inclusion criteria for depressed subjects included a score of ≥ 17 on the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (mean score for depressed subjects = 18.7, SD ± 2.8). Of the 21 MDD subjects, 18 were medication naïve. These 18 individuals were also in their first Major Depressive Episode (MDE). The three remaining MDD subjects had had prior episodes of depression, and during those episodes they were treated with antidepressants. None of the MDD subjects were taking psychotropic medications at the time of their study participation and all MDD subjects were medication free for a period of at least 6 months prior to their study participation. Inclusion criteria for non-depressed subjects included failing to meet criteria for any Axis I psychiatric disorder on the SCID, as well as scoring ≤ 3 on the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (mean score for non-depressed subjects = 0.3, SD ± 0.8). Moreover, non-depressed subjects were thoroughly screened by MCE to ensure that they had no lifetime history of undiagnosed depressive symptoms (i.e., a period in which they may have met criteria for an MDE but during which they did not seek professional help). For instance, these subjects were excluded if they i) had ever seen a therapist, ii) had ever taken any psychotropic medications (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, mood stabilizers, antipsychotics or benzodiazepines), or iii) had ever gone through a sustained period (≥ 2 weeks) in which they felt extremely sad, had significant anhedonia, and/or in which their social or occupational function was significantly compromised by mood symptoms. Subjects provided written informed consent in accordance with the guidelines provided by the Human Research Committee of the Mass General Brigham Hospital.

This study includes data from the Baseline FDG-PET/rsfMRI imaging session described in our previous work [ 27 ]— although that study did not include depressed individuals. ## ROI selection

We used pre-defined, whole-network ROIs derived from the 17-network parcellation in Yeo et al. 2011 [ 28 ]. Each of the 17 ROIs used corresponded to one of the 17 networks: cingulo-opercular (CO), salience, default networks “A”, “B” and “C,” frontoparietal control networks “A”, “B” and “C,” dorsal attention networks “A” and “B,” somatomotor networks “A” and “B,” the limbic orbitofrontal network, the limbic temporal network, the central visual network, the peripheral visual network, and the temporoparietal network. Each ROI consisted of a composite mask encompassing all network nodes across both hemispheres. Notably, since Yeo et al. 2011 [ 28 ], several studies have clarified the topography of the CO and salience networks [ 29 , 30 ]. Consistent with those reports, here, we classify what was depicted as “salience network A” in the Yeo 17-network parcellation as the CO network. In contrast, we classify what was depicted as “salience network B” as the salience network proper—which most prominently includes posterior midline regions [ 31 ], ventral anterior insula, rostrodorsal anterior cingulate (approximately corresponding to Brodmann areas (BAs) 24a’, 24b’, 24c’ and 32’) [ 32 , 33 ], and more dorsal and lateral portions of dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (approximately corresponding to BAs 9 and 9/46) [ 34 , 35 ]. We also created a 10 mm spherical ROI in the right sgACC (MNI coordinates: 6, 16, -10, derived from Fox et al. 2013 [ 36 ]). To visualize network-level changes in metabolism and FC at the voxel-wise level, we superimposed boundaries of the 17-network parcellation over FDG-PET and FC voxel-wise maps.


## FDG-PET image acquisition

FDG-PET images were acquired on a BrainPET prototype (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Participants in both groups were injected with 18 Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) prior to PET imaging and the amount of this did not differ between groups, depressed: mean MBq = 185 (range 154-200), mean mCi = 5.0 (range 4.2-5.4); non-depressed: mean MBq = 189 (range 164-212), mean mCi =5.1 (range 4.4-5.7); unpaired t-test, p = 0.20 for both MBq and mCi values. The duration between FDG injection and the beginning of scanning also did not differ between depressed (mean = 34.2 min) and non-depressed subjects (mean = 33.0 min) (unpaired t-test, p = 0.35). PET data was collected with the BrainPET prototype up to a timepoint of 70 min post-injection. Data was stored in listmode format.


## FDG-PET preprocessing

PET images were reconstructed using an ordered-subsets expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm using 6 iterations and 16 subsets, and correcting for random coincidences, dead time, isotope decay, detector sensitivity, photon attenuation and scatter. Attenuation correction was provided via the Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM)-based method [ 27 , 37 ]. Static images were reconstructed 45 to 65 min post injection. The reconstructed PET volume consisted of a 256×256×153 matrix of 1.25 mm isotropic voxels. Motion correction was enabled into the PET reconstruction using a dual-pass image reconstruction method. Finally, the PET images were co-registered back into the MRI MPRAGE images to allow ideal alignment. Images were spatially normalized into MNI space using SPM (version 12; Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology) Dartel toolbox [ 38 ].


## FDG-PET data analysis

To derive quantitative values of regional glucose metabolism, we normalized ROI-based PET values using whole-brain as a normalization factor, yielding whole-brain normalized standardized uptake values (WBn-suv). We quantified differences in resting metabolism between the two groups by comparing differences in WBn-suv values in each of the 17 whole-network ROIs, as well as in the sgACC ROI, using permutation tests (N = 5000) given the small sample size in each group. To visualize the topography of differences in resting metabolism between depressed and non-depressed cohorts, we used the 3dttest + + function in AFNI (version 16.3.13; National Institutes of Health) to perform unpaired two-sample t-tests and derived voxel-wise statistical maps thresholded at a p value of < 0.01, uncorrected (Fig. 1 ). Of note, as no clusters initially survived at FWE-corrected p < 0.05, we chose the more liberal threshold of p < 0.01, uncorrected to capture as many regions with aberrant metabolism as possible for subsequent exploratory FC analyses.


## MRI image acquisition

Concurrent to FDG-PET acquisitions, MRI data were acquired using a 3.0-T whole-body scanner (Siemens™) equipped for echo planar imaging with a 12-channel 3-axis gradient head coil. Head movements were restricted using foam cushions. For each MR scanning session, one structural scan (~8 min) and three consecutive fMRI blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) resting-state runs (6 min each) were performed. Structural images were acquired via a T1-weighted 3D magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) image, acquired with the following parameters: TE = 1.64 ms, TR = 2530 ms, TI = 1200 ms, voxel size = 1 ×1 × 1mm. BOLD images were acquired using T2*-weighted sequences (TR = 3000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle = 90°, voxel size = 3.375 × 3.375 × 3.000 mm), FOV 448 × 448, with no acceleration factor. A fixation dot (a small white dot centered on a black background) was displayed to subjects via a rear projection system during BOLD imaging. Participants were instructed to stay awake, remain extremely still and to stare at the fixation dot.


## fMRI preprocessing

Images were first preprocessed using spatial normalization to a standard MNI 152 template brain via linear regression, as well as motion and slice timing corrected using a combination of software packages: SPM, FSL (version 4.0.3; the FMRIB Software Library), and MATLAB (version 7.4.0; Mathworks). The following nuisance variables and their temporal derivatives were regressed during preprocessing: whole brain global signal, motion parameters, mean white matter and mean CSF signal (as described in Yeo et al. 2011 [ 28 ]). Additionally, data was low-pass filtered to exclude signals < 0.08 Hz. Smoothing was performed with a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel.


## FC data analysis

Regions reflecting differences in resting metabolism between depressed and non-depressed cohorts on FDG-PET imaging (i.e., clusters larger than 10 voxels and surviving a statistical threshold of p < 0.01, uncorrected) were used as ROIs for a seed-based FC analysis. Two clusters located in the left and right lateral orbitofrontal cortex were excluded from calculations of the FC seeds because they appeared to reflect susceptibility artifacts (left cluster: x = −40.5, y = 30, z = −21; right cluster: x = 49.5, y = 30, z = −16.5). The resulting ROIs were divided into those exhibiting increased metabolism (i.e., hyper metabolic) in depressed relative to non-depressed subjects and those exhibiting decreased metabolism (i.e., hypo metabolic). Masks were then created for these two respective sets of hypermetabolic and hypometabolic regions. Each mask (hypermetabolic and hypometabolic) consisted of a composite of all regions surviving the statistical threshold. These two masks were then used as seeds to calculate FC differences between the two groups. In other words, we statistically compared FC maps between depressed and non-depressed subjects derived from the hypermetabolic and hypometabolic ROIs (Fig. 1 ).

Following pre-processing, volumetric seed-based FC analyses were conducted by extracting the BOLD timecourse from the seed masks/ROIs as defined above and calculating the z -transformed Pearson correlation coefficient between this mask and all other brain voxels. FC comparisons between depressed and non-depressed groups were performed using unpaired two-sample t-tests in AFNI (National Institutes of Health) using the 3dttest + + function [ 39 ]. Voxel-wise statistical FC maps were generated from these comparisons at a p value of <0.01 uncorrected and subsequently thresholded at an FWE cluster corrected p value of <0.01 (using SPM software). FC maps were also projected to the surface for visualization using the Connectome Workbench (version 1.3.2) toolbox ( www.humanconnectome.org/software/connectome-workbench ), and the location of the significant clusters was qualitatively compared to the topography of networks from the Yeo et al., 2011 17-network parcellation [ 28 ] and the Du et al., 2024 15-network parcellation [ 31 ].

In addition, because prior FC studies implicated the sgACC and the default, salience, CO and frontoparietal control networks in MDD, voxel-wise FC comparisons were performed with whole-network ROIs from these regions/networks using the same statistical approach as the group comparison on FC maps derived from FDG-PET hyper- and hypometabolic seeds described above (i.e., unpaired two-sample t-tests using the 3dttest + + function in AFNI).


## Quality control for head motion in fMRI BOLD data

As head motion has a significant effect on FC [ 40 ], we employed a stringent quality control analysis [ 41 ]. Each BOLD run was evaluated for slice-based temporal SNR (sSNR; slice-based tSNR calculated as the mean signal over time divided by the standard deviation of the signal over time for each slice), mean and maximum relative framewise motion, mean and maximum absolute motion, and number of framewise movements greater than 0.1 mm and 0.5 mm. A given run was excluded from the analysis if the sSNR for that run was lower than 2 standard deviations below the group mean of all runs or if there were more than 5 movements greater than 0.5 mm in that run.


## Results


## Differences in resting metabolism between depressed and non-depressed controls

We first assessed topographic differences in resting metabolism, based on FDG-PET imaging, between depressed and non-depressed subjects. These results are displayed as voxel-wise FDG-PET maps (statistically thresholded at an uncorrected p value of < 0.01) in Fig. 2 , with Fig. 2A showing regions of increased metabolism (hypermetabolism) in depressed compared to non-depressed subjects (see Supplemental Table S1 for full cluster list) and Fig. 2B showing regions of decreased metabolism (hypometabolism) in depressed compared to non-depressed subjects (see Supplemental Table S2 for full cluster list). Network-level changes are shown in Fig. 2C . Depressed subjects exhibited hypermetabolism in portions of the CO network (purple outlines, Fig. 2C )—particularly in the right frontal insula and right supramarginal gyrus. In contrast, depressed subjects demonstrated hypometabolism in portions of the default network—including the right posterior cingulate (default network A, yellow outlines, Fig. 2C ), right retrosplenial cortex (default network C, white outlines, Fig. 2C ), and the left lateral temporal cortex (default network B, maroon outlines, Fig. 2C ). Of note, overlap between regions of hypermetabolism and the CO network; and hypometabolism and the default network were not exact.

With respect to quantitative differences in WBn-suv values, consistent with the voxel-wise maps, depressed subjects showed statistically higher resting metabolism in the CO network than non-depressed subjects (Δ mean WBn-suv CO network: depressed > non-depressed = 0.016, p = 0.016). Depressed subjects showed lower resting metabolism in each of the three default networks, with this approaching statistical significance in default network C (Δ mean WBn-suv default network C: depressed > non-depressed = -0.022, p = 0.055).

Contradicting some previous reports [ 39 ], we did not find differences in sgACC resting metabolism between depressed and non-depressed subjects (Δ WBn-suv right sgACC depressed > non-depressed = -0.002, p = 0.92). Similarly, there were no differences in this region on voxel-wise maps (Fig. 2 ).

## Differences in the resting FC of hypermetabolic and hypometabolic regions between depressed and non-depressed subjects

We subsequently asked how the FC of hyper- and hypometabolic regions differed between groups. In depressed compared to non-depressed subjects, hypermetabolic regions showed increased FC with the salience network, particularly with prefrontal portions of this system (Fig. 3 ). After applying FWE cluster correction, one cluster (also overlapping with the prefrontal portion of the salience network) survived (Fig. 3 , inset ). In contrast, hypermetabolic regions showed decreased FC with portions of the default network, particularly the left angular gyrus and the bilateral lateral temporal cortices (Fig. S 2 ). No region exhibited significant FC changes between groups after applying an FWE cluster correction with this contrast.

## Discussion

Our results add to the growing body of literature invoking network-level dysfunction in MDD. Furthermore, our results suggest links between changes in resting metabolism and resting FC in MDD. This echoes other studies demonstrating relationships between resting metabolism and resting FC in healthy cohorts [ 23 , 24 , 45 ] and in MDD patients specifically [ 10 ]. We discovered changes in resting metabolism in depressed subjects that roughly mirrored the topography of large-scale networks implicated in this disorder. We also found that in depressed subjects, dorsomedial and dorsolateral prefrontal portions of the salience network served as a point of topographical convergence for FC increases and decreases of hypermetabolic and hypometabolic regions, respectively. These results further bolster evidence that prefrontal salience network regions play important roles in affective symptomatology. For example, critically, Lynch and colleagues recently showed that almost identical regions, which they classified as parts of the “frontostriatal salience network,” were significantly expanded in densely sampled depressed individuals using precision FC methods [ 35 ].


## Depressed individuals exhibit network-level changes in metabolism

Notably, we did not find increased resting metabolism in the sgACC in depressed subjects. Although this contradicts seminal results from some other studies [ 1 , 3 ], evidence that resting metabolism is higher in depressed individuals is conflicting [ 14 ] and meta-analyses have not shown this [ 13 ]. Reasons for these inconsistencies could include differences in methodology (e.g., image resolution, the reference region used for image normalization), clinical heterogeneity across patients in different studies (e.g., depression biotype heterogeneity, treatment-resistance status) and whether studies were done in the resting-state or through task provocation.

We found overlap between the demarcations of the CO and default networks and regions of hyper- and hypometabolism, respectively, in depressed subjects. Other studies have discovered metabolic abnormalities in select portions of the CO and default networks in MDD. For example, consistent with our results, Parkar et al. found decreased glucose metabolism on FDG-PET imaging in default network regions in suicidal individuals [ 46 ]. Also, McGrath and colleagues found that right frontoinsular glucose hypermetabolism predicted responses to escitalopram treatment in depressed patients [ 47 ]. Still other reports have implicated abnormal metabolism in comparable network regions, but with the directionality of metabolism changes opposite to those found here. For instance, a voxel-based meta-analysis of 10 FDG-PET studies in MDD revealed decreased insular glucose metabolism in depression [ 13 ]. Similarly, other studies have uncovered increased resting metabolism in the hippocampus in MDD [ 9 , 10 ], whereas our results suggested mildly decreased left hippocampal metabolism in depressed subjects. While other studies have identified metabolic derangements in regions corresponding to network nodes, this is among the first investigations to explicitly implicate glucose metabolism abnormalities across distributed canonical large-scale networks in MDD.


## Specific relevance of the salience, CO and default networks to the pathogenesis of MDD

Several investigations have implicated network-level dysfunction in depression based on resting-state FC and/or graph theoretical analyses. The salience, CO and default networks are consistently cited as demonstrating aberrant functional correlations in MDD based on group averaged data [ 16 , 17 , 20 , 48 – 52 ]. For example, relatively consistent findings are those of increased resting-state FC within the default network in depression [ 49 , 53 , 54 ] and altered FC between the sgACC and this network [ 55 – 57 ]. Increases in FC have also been described in the salience network, and aberrant FC has been identified between the default and salience networks [ 58 ], and between these networks and the frontoparietal control network [ 59 – 61 ]. For example, studies have suggested that the salience network acts as a functional “switch” between default and cognitive control systems and that depressed individuals maladaptively adjudicate resources between these networks [ 60 , 62 ].

The salience, CO and default networks are uniquely anatomically and functionally positioned to process affective, self-relevant, autonomic and interoceptive information—making their contributions to the pathogenesis of depression likely. For example, the anterior insula (which encompasses nodes of the CO and salience networks) receives the majority of ascending autonomic and interoceptive input from the viscera in humans and in non-human primates [ 63 , 64 ]; and the ventral anterior insula specifically (a node of the salience network) is theorized to process the emotional valence of these inputs. Similarly, default network nodes are involved in self-referential thought (furthering its putative role in depressive rumination [ 49 , 50 , 54 ]), as well as in socioemotional processing [ 43 , 65 ].

Notably, our results did not directly implicate the frontoparietal control network. However, the regions of altered metabolism and FC we observed did not perfectly conform to the topography of the salience, CO or default networks, and some of the regions we reference could conceivably fall within the borders of the frontoparietal control network, depending on the cortical parcellation used.


## Portions of the frontostriatal salience network acted as a point of functional convergence

We found that frontostriatal salience regions served as a point of functional convergence with distributed nodes in depressed subjects. In addition to Lynch et al. [ 35 ], other studies have identified comparable frontal salience regions as playing pivotal roles in affective processing. First, the rostrodorsal anterior cingulate is a structural hub which receives extensive limbic and paralimbic inputs. For example, Tang et al. used retrograde tracing in the macaque and diffusion MRI tractography in humans and in non-human primates to demonstrate that this region represented a convergence point of cross-module integration and high degree-centrality in prefrontal cortex [ 66 ]. Second, rostral ACC activity is predictive of antidepressant treatment responses [ 32 ]. Third, comparable frontostriatal salience regions have been designated as a “dorsal nexus” in depression based on their hyperconnectivity with canonical large-scale networks in MDD [ 67 ]. Fourth, Li and colleagues identified a similar region that demonstrated increased FC in depressed subjects [ 53 ]. Fifth, the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations in the BOLD MRI signal in the dorsal anterior cingulate decreases following sgACC deep brain stimulation in MDD patients, and this correlates with clinical improvement [ 68 , 69 ]. Sixth, the expression of 5-HT 1A receptors (which play a central role in the pathogenesis of MDD [ 70 ]) peaks in the dorsal and rostral anterior cingulate [ 71 ]. Lastly, prefrontal nodes of the salience network are emerging targets for Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (TMS) in the treatment of depression [ 72 ]. Indeed, it may be no accident that prefrontal regions most anti-correlated with the sgACC, which are more efficacious TMS targets in depression [ 73 ], fall within the borders of the prefrontal salience network [ 27 , 72 ].

Our results are also broadly consistent with models of depression in which corticolimbic regions are functionally hyper- or hypo-connected to distributed systems. The sgACC is a seminal example, as it exhibits increased or decreased FC with large-scale networks in depression [ 55 , 74 , 75 ]. We bolster such depression network models by showing that connectivity changes preferentially occur with regions/networks whose resting metabolism is altered. More specifically, our results support a model in which frontostriatal salience coupling is biased away from hypometabolic default network and towards hypermetabolic CO nodes in MDD.


## Limitations

This study is primarily limited by its small sample size (21 subjects per group) which restricts the ability to draw conclusions generalizable across depressed patients. Compounding this is the fact that depression is highly phenotypically variable [ 18 ]. Thus, it is possible that a more granular examination of different clinical biotypes would yield different metabolic and hemodynamic FC results [ 18 ]. Moreover, the FDG-PET differences between groups did not survive correction for multiple comparisons and the seeds for FC analyses were thus derived from uncorrected statistical maps. Thus, the FDG-PET results of this study need to be interpreted with caution as there is a likelihood of false positive errors. In addition, the topographical overlap between regions of altered metabolism/FC and network boundaries were far from perfect. Furthermore, while our fMRI preprocessing decisions, including regression of nuisance variables, were similar to that of other recent studies (e.g., compare to reference [ 31 ]), future studies should explore the use of expanded motion parameters (e.g., see reference [ 72 ]), alternative CSF/WM denoising techniques, and bandpass filtering. Finally, our methods were not capable of establishing causation with respect to the associations between metabolic and FC changes in depression—i.e., whether increased or decreased metabolism “caused,” or were the result of, increased or decreased FC.


## Conclusion

We used combined MR-PET imaging to simultaneously acquire resting FDG-PET and FC data in a group of 21 unmedicated depressed individuals and compared them to a group of 21 non-depressed controls. Our results suggest that depression is characterized by changes in metabolic activity across large-scale networks, and that these networks alter their functional coupling with frontostriatal portions of the salience network (whose cortical expansion has recently been shown to be a trait-based biomarker of depression [ 35 ]). These observations suggest a possible framework for network re-organization in depression, in which broadly distributed cortical systems exhibit changes in their metabolic activity and concurrently become more and less functionally coupled to common prefrontal salience regions. These results could have implications for more refined neuromodulation targets for depression, e.g., through more precise and more robust targeting of these regions in MDD.
