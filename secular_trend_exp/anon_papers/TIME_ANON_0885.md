## Abstract

A novel strain of influenza A(H1N1) was first detected in April [YEAR], resulting in a global pandemic. Alterations in its hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) proteins can impact the virus's properties, pathogenicity, and response to treatment. Thus, ongoing epidemiological and molecular studies are crucial for effective vaccine development and management. We aimed to monitor the evolution of influenza A(H1N1) pdm09 virus (as defined by WHO) in deceased individuals during a specific period in Shiraz, Iran.

A total of 200 post‐mortem samples were collected from deceased individuals. Subsequently, the HA and NA gene segments of these viral isolates were subjected to targeted amplification and sequencing. Phylogenetic analysis was then conducted on the HA and NA gene sequences.

A total of 21 samples between [YEAR] and [YEAR] were confirmed positive for influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 viruses. All strains isolated from deceased patients belonged to subclade 6B.1, with I216T and S162N mutations. Our strains differed from the A/California/07/[YEAR] vaccine strain, with the substitutions S203T, S185T, K163Q, and D222G observed in the HA antigenic epitope. Additionally, E224G, R223Q, and D222G mutations were detected in the RBS of HA. The NA gene analysis of influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 viruses showed sporadic detections of oseltamivir‐resistance mutation, H275Y, in 5% of reported sequences. Nevertheless, no other NAI resistance‐associated mutations were detected in the NA of these viruses.

Our research strains exhibited genetic variations compared to the vaccine strains. The results of this study demonstrate the genetic diversity of influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 viruses and emphasize the critical importance of continued molecular investigations to inform effective influenza control strategies.

Keywords: antigenic site, hemagglutinin, influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus, neuraminidase, vaccine


## Introduction

Influenza is a major global public health concern [ 1 ]. Globally, an estimated 3−5 million individuals grapple with severe influenza infections each year, resulting in 500,000 annual fatalities [ 2 , 3 ]. Influenza viruses belong to the Orthomyxoviridae family and contain a negative‐sense RNA genome [ 4 ]. Influenza viruses are categorized into three distinct types (A, B, and C) based on disparities observed in their structural genes (M, NP). Furthermore, subtypes are classified based on the antigenic attributes of their surface glycoproteins, namely hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) [ 5 , 6 , 7 ]. Influenza viruses undergo frequent point mutations, known as antigenic drift, and the rearrangement of genome segments, referred to as antigenic shift. These processes result in antigenic variations that can lead to either epidemics or pandemics [ 8 ]. A novel influenza A(H1N1) virus pandemic emerged in March [YEAR] in Mexico and the United States [ 9 ]. The virus spread worldwide and remains one of the prevalent strains among humans [ 10 , 11 ]. The influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus has a new reassortment, incorporating genetic segments from human, avian, and swine lineages. Phylogenetic investigation has substantiated that the HA, NS, and NP genes derived from the classical swine virus lineage; in contrast, the M and NA genes originated from the avian‐like Eurasian swine H1N1 lineage. Moreover, the PA and PB2 genes trace back to the North American avian virus lineage, and the PB1 gene originates in the human H1N1 virus lineage [ 8 , 12 , 13 ]. Due to the lack of pre‐establishment immunity, the influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus showed pronounced human‐to‐human transmission [ 14 , 15 ]. It's important to acknowledge that the influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus exhibits variations in transmission, replication, pathogenesis, and tropism when compared to seasonal H1 and H3 strains [ 16 ]. According to recent data, over 109,000 cases of influenza‐like illness (ILI) were reported in Iran between [YEAR] and [YEAR], with 22% testing positive for influenza viruses, mostly type A (H1N1 and H3N2), and a case fatality rate of up to 8.06% for A(H1N1)pdm09 virus in severe cases [ 17 ]. Mutations in the HA affect pathogenicity and receptor binding specificity in circulating strains [ 8 , 11 ]. As a result, continued surveillance is vital for tracking viral genome changes that influence the detection of emerging variants and their pathogenicity. Also, the World Health Organization (WHO) monitors influenza viruses for their susceptibility to NA inhibitors as an essential requirement [ 18 ]. Oseltamivir resistance can be determined by genotyping and phenotyping assays [ 19 ], and continuing epidemiological and molecular investigations are crucial for tracking alterations in the virus genome that impact pathogenicity and identifying emerging variants. These surveillance efforts contribute to proficient pandemic flu management and the design of improved vaccines for combating influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus infections.

We hypothesized that the influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus strains circulating among deceased patients in Shiraz during the [YEAR]–[YEAR] influenza season harbor significant mutations in the HA and NA genes, which may contribute to altered antigenicity, increased virulence, or potential antiviral resistance. This study aimed to: (1) Detect and genetically characterize the influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 strains isolated from deceased individuals to identify viral genetic variations potentially associated with increased virulence or immune escape, and to explore strains that may be linked to severe disease or fatal outcomes. (2) Analyze phylogenetic relationships and identify molecular variations in the HA and NA segments. (3) Compare the mutations found with current vaccine strains (A/California/07/[YEAR] and A/Brisbane/02/[YEAR]). (4) Assess potential drug resistance mutations, particularly in the NA segment.

The time frame of October [YEAR] to December [YEAR] was selected because it represents the peak influenza season before the COVID‐19 pandemic, during which an increase in ILI and fatalities was observed in southern Iran. This period also offered a unique opportunity to analyze the pre‐pandemic viral evolution trend.


## Materials and Methods


## Sample Collection

A total of 200 nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal samples were obtained from deceased individuals with ILI, characterized by fever, sore throat, cough, and myalgia. These samples were obtained through hospital units between September [YEAR] and February [YEAR]. In all cases, sampling was performed within a short time after death (less than 6 h), and respiratory symptoms had been reported 2−5 days before death.

Patients diagnosed with infections other than influenza were excluded from the study. Nasopharyngeal specimens were promptly transferred into viral transport medium (VTM) and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis. Ethical approval was obtained from Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.REC.1399.689), and informed consent was obtained from the patients' parents or legal guardians. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.


## RNA Extraction and Real‐timePCR for Influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 Virus Detection

Viral RNA was extracted from 200 μL of the clinical specimens collected in transport medium using the high‐purity viral RNA extraction kit (Roche, Berlin, Germany). The purified RNAs were stored at −80°C until further analysis. At first, real‐time PCR was conducted to detect Influenza A in the samples. Subsequently, it was performed using SuperScript III Platinum One Step Quantitative RT‐PCR kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus detection. Each reaction was prepared with 4 μL of extracted RNA and 16 μL of the master mix, consisting of 10 μL 2x reaction mix, 4.4 μL RNase DNase‐free water, 0.4 μL of each primer and prob, and 0.4 μL of SuperScript III RT/Platinum Taq mix. Following the CDC protocol, reverse transcription was performed at 50°C for 30 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 95°C for 2 min, and PCR cycles consisted of 45 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, annealing at 55°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s. All reactions were carried out on a Corbett 6000 Rotor‐Gene system (Corbett, Victoria, Australia). Finally, real‐time PCR analysis was conducted. ## Conventional PCR for the Amplification of HA and NA Genes

RNA extracted from positive samples was used for cDNA synthesis, and the resulting cDNA was amplified using conventional PCR. Conventional PCR using specific primers was performed to amplify the full coding regions of the HA and NA genes. The resulting PCR products were purified, and the identity of each DNA fragment was confirmed by electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel. Due to limitations in RNA quality and quantity, as well as financial constraints associated with full‐genome sequencing, only 11 out of the 21 confirmed positive samples with optimal RNA concentration and integrity (RIN ≥ 7) were selected for complete HA and NA gene sequencing. Selection was also influenced by the availability of complete demographic and clinical data for these patients. Eleven positive samples of HA and NA were sent to Topazgene (IRAN) for Sanger sequencing.


## Molecular and Phylogenetic Analyses

To analyze the obtained nucleotide sequences and identify potential variations in the HA and NA proteins, a series of bioinformatics tools and approaches were employed. Initially, the quality of the nucleotide sequences was assessed through manual inspection of chromatogram peak patterns. To ensure sequence accuracy and determine similarity to previously reported influenza strains, BLASTn analysis was performed using the NCBI GenBank database. Subsequently, multiple sequence alignments were performed using CLC Genomics Workbench version 6 (CLC6), and mutation analysis was carried out by comparing the translated amino acid sequences with the reference strains.

A molecular phylogenetic analysis was performed on the complete set of 11 strains. This analysis was carried out with the HA and NA genes of 83 worldwide isolates accessible in GenBank [ 19 , 20 ]. The resulting phylogenetic tree was meticulously constructed using MEGA 7, employing the neighbor‐joining (NJ) methodology alongside 1000 bootstrap replicates.


## Results

Of these, 21 samples (70%) were confirmed as influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 using the real‐time PCR method. The patients' ages varied between 35 and 85 years. The group included 15 males and 6 females (Table 2 ). All of the deceased patients included in the samples experienced severe illness, necessitating hospitalization. The GenBank repository houses the HA and NA sequences from this study, which are accessible via the provided accession numbers (Tables 3 and 4 ). ## Sequence Comparisons

In a comparison of the 11 influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 strains in this study with the A/California/07/[YEAR] vaccine strain, the nucleotide sequence identity of HA ranged from 97.4% to 99.3%, while that of NA ranged from 97.7% to 99.6%. In addition to comparisons with the A/California/07/[YEAR] vaccine strain, our isolates were also aligned with the more recent vaccine strain A/Brisbane/02/[YEAR] (H1N1)pdm09, which was recommended by the WHO for the [YEAR]–[YEAR] influenza season. The nucleotide identity of HA and NA segments in our isolates compared to A/Brisbane/02/[YEAR] ranged from 97.8% to 99.1% for HA and 98.2% to 99.5% for NA. These results suggest that while our strains remain relatively close to the A/Brisbane/02/[YEAR] strain, several mutations were still present, potentially reflecting ongoing antigenic drift.


## Genetic and Evolutionary Analysis of Influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 Virus

Phylogenetic trees were constructed for the HA and NA genes of 11 influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 strains originating from Shiraz, Iran. These strains were compared with A/California/07/[YEAR] and A/Brisbane/02/[YEAR] vaccine strains, as well as with viruses circulating in various global regions (refer to Figures 1 and 2 ). Our analysis of the HA gene's phylogeny unveiled that all Shiraz strains formed a distinct and identifiable cluster within subclade 6B.1 (Figure 1 ), affirming their membership in the 6B.1 subclade. Variations were observed among strains isolated from deceased individuals between October [YEAR] and December [YEAR].

## Amino Acid Variations in the HA and NA Proteins of Influenza A(H1N1)pdm09

In this investigation, we undertook a comprehensive analysis by contrasting the amino acid sequences of the HA and NA proteins in the influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 strains from Shiraz, Iran, against the A/California/07/[YEAR] and A/Brisbane/02/[YEAR] reference vaccine strains. Among the pertinent findings, all isolates exhibited characteristic mutations in the HA gene compared with the A/California/07/[YEAR] vaccine strain, namely S185T, S203T, K163Q, and D222G, emblematic of the influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus during these two distinct seasons (as outlined in Table 5 ).

In the analysis of antigenic epitopes, all isolates from deceased patients in the [YEAR]−[YEAR] season bore particular mutations. These included K163Q (100%) and S162N (100%) in the Sa site, K283E (100%) and S203T (100%) in the Ca site, and a less frequent S185T (3%) mutation in the Sb site. The HA protein's receptor‐binding region (RBD), a trio of domains—the 190 helix, the 220 loop, and the 130 loop [ 20 ], which played a crucial role in interacting with α2–6‐linked glycans that serve as human influenza virus receptors. Notably, a subset of isolates (3%) exhibited the D222G mutation within the RBD of HA. This specific mutation has been previously associated with augmented binding to α2–6‐linked glycans in the lower respiratory tract of humans. Moving to the NA protein, our scrutiny of the amino acid sequences highlighted consistent substitutions across all isolated strains, including V81A and N449D (as indicated in Table 6 ).

Compared with the A/Brisbane/02/[YEAR] vaccine strain, all isolates from our study exhibited key amino acid differences in the HA protein, including K163Q, S162N, and S203T (Table 5 ). These mutations are situated within antigenic sites Sa and Ca1, which are involved in host antibody recognition. The K163Q and S162N substitutions have been reported to affect receptor‐binding and antigenicity, potentially influencing vaccine efficacy. Similarly, in the NA protein, a substitution such as T13I, which was observed in our isolates relative to A/Brisbane/02/[YEAR], could indicate ongoing adaptation (Table 6 ). Although this specific NA mutation has not been directly associated with antiviral resistance, its emergence warrants continued surveillance to monitor functional consequences.

While no mutations were detected within the NA antigenic sites, the H275Y mutation was identified in one of the studied isolates. The H275Y mutation reduces resistance to oseltamivir, and it was also observed in a Shiraz study from [YEAR], alongside the prevalent N369K mutation. Additionally, the Q51K (72.7%), F74S (72.7%), and D416N (81.8%) mutations, previously reported in Shiraz isolates from [YEAR], were uniformly noted across all strains (as depicted in Figure 2 ). Lastly, a spectrum of catalytic and framework sites within the NA protein was discerned, encompassing residues such as R118, D151, R152, R225, E277, R293, R368, Y402, E119, R156, W179, S180, D/N199, I223, E228, H275, E278, N295, and E425. These intricate observations collectively enhance our comprehension of the genetic variations and adaptive characteristics of the influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus strains in the specified regions and timeframes [ 21 , 22 ].


## Discussion

Understanding the significance of alterations in the HA and NA proteins is essential due to their potential to influence various aspects of the virus. These changes can directly impact the virus's antigenic properties, dictating how it interacts with the immune system, its pathogenic tendencies, affecting the severity of illness it causes, and its ability to resist pharmaceutical interventions, which has implications for treatment strategies.

In this context, our specific aim was to closely monitor the genetic changes of the influenza A(H1N1) pdm09 virus in individuals who succumbed to the infection in a specific timeframe in Shiraz, Iran. By focusing on this particular population and time frame, we aimed to contribute to the broader understanding of the virus's behavior and evolution, ultimately informing better public health strategies. To assess the long‐term evolution of the influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus, the A/California/07/[YEAR] strain was used as a baseline reference, given its status as the original vaccine strain following the [YEAR] pandemic. However, to capture more recent antigenic drift, we also compared our sequences with A/Brisbane/02/[YEAR], the WHO‐recommended vaccine strain during the [YEAR]–[YEAR] season. This dual approach allowed us to evaluate long‐term divergence and short‐term evolutionary changes relevant to current vaccine development.

Following the [YEAR] flu pandemic, the influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus has progressively displaced the seasonal influenza A(H1N1) virus, establishing itself as a prominent subtype within the global influenza epidemic. The influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus demonstrates its adaptability to novel hosts and environments through continual evolution [ 23 ]. Each year, various countries experience distinct influenza seasons with varying onset times and prevalent subtypes. Influenza strain subtypes circulating in the Northern Hemisphere during the [YEAR]−[YEAR] season illustrate this diversity. Notably, the strains isolated from [YEAR] to [YEAR] exhibited divergence from the A/California/07/[YEAR] vaccine strain. Over time, the similarity between isolates and the vaccine strain gradually diminished. All the isolates displayed differences from the A/California/07/[YEAR] vaccine strain, particularly evident in the HA antigenic epitopes S185T, S203T, K163Q, and D222G. These substitutions suggest a continuous mutation and progressive evolution of the surface proteins of these strains. Consequently, the current vaccines likely offer only moderate protection against the influenza A(H1N1) pdm09 virus. The HA protein encompasses four antigenic epitopes: Ca, Cb, Sa, and Sb. In a study by Oliveria et al., the HA1 domain sequences of H1 viruses were analyzed and compared with the A/New Caledonia/20/99 strain, which was the influenza vaccine constituent between [YEAR] and [YEAR]. The results indicated that H1 viruses from [YEAR] exhibited higher identity at both nucleotide and amino acid levels (97.9%−98.5% and 97.2%−98.2%, respectively) than the [YEAR] collection (97.3% and 97.2%, respectively). Examination of critical antigenic sites (Ca, Cb, Sa, and Sb) in this domain unveiled some amino acid substitutions compared to the vaccine strain. Notably, all studied H1 viruses exhibited an A169V substitution within the Ca1 site. Furthermore, at the same site, the [YEAR] virus exhibited a G240R substitution. Additionally, the [YEAR] viruses showed an A193T substitution in the Sb site [ 24 ]. Also, in another study by Mohebbi et al., the isolated HA sequences exhibited 10 shared glycosylation sites at positions 11, 23, 84, 87, 97, 162, 287, 451, 481, and 540. Notably, position 162 was situated within the Sa antigenic site, whereas positions 84 and 97 did not fall within the antigenic region of the globular head. A unique S162N substitution was introduced as a novel N‐glycosylation site within the Sa antigenic site. This substitution was present in 97% of the analyzed samples [ 25 ].

In our analysis, we identified common and less frequent mutations within the HA protein, potentially impacting the pathogenicity, host adaptation, and antigenic specificity of the influenza virus.

The NA protein can remove sialic acid from the cell surface, thereby facilitating viral release [ 1 ]. Mutations in the catalytic and framework sites of NA, such as H275Y and E119G, can lead to resistance to NAI, although these regions usually remain conserved [ 26 , 27 ]. Influenza A(H1N1) virus with H275Y mutation has biological defects, but drug‐resistance mutations are often detected in immunosuppressed patients [ 28 , 29 ]. Therefore, these mutant viruses require specific conditions for replication; as a result, drug‐resistance mutations are more frequently observed in immunosuppressed populations. According to the WHO report on the pandemic (H1N1) [YEAR], all influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 viruses were genetically and antigenically closely aligned with vaccine strains [ 30 ]. Variations occurring at or in the near region of the antigenic sites can affect vaccine efficacy [ 31 ].

Sequencing and analyzing the HA and NA proteins in influenza is vital. These proteins are prime targets for vaccine development, helping create vaccines with broad protection. Tracking their genetic changes aids in predicting new strains and potential pandemics. This data informs public health responses and interventions and monitors antiviral drug resistance. Additionally, studying HA and NA sequences deepens our understanding of viral evolution and host interactions, advancing basic research and practical applications in combating influenza outbreaks. A major limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size (21 positive cases and 11 cases subjected to sequencing), which may not fully represent the genetic diversity of influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 strains circulating across Iran. Therefore, future studies with larger, geographically diverse data sets are required to confirm these findings.


## Conclusion

In this study, an in‐depth analysis of the influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 strains circulating in Fars province from October [YEAR] to December [YEAR] was conducted. The results revealed a distinct genetic evolution of the strains within this specific timeframe. Mutations observed in the HA and NA proteins, particularly in the antigenic epitopes, signify potential changes in the virus's antigenic properties and interaction with the host's immune system. Mutations such as S185T, S203T, K163Q, and D222G provide insights into the virus's adaptability and potential implications for disease severity and vaccine effectiveness. Furthermore, the decline in genetic similarity between the isolated strains and the A/California/07/[YEAR] vaccine strain indicates a continuous evolution of the virus. This evolution could affect the vaccine's ability to confer protection against these strains, underscoring the need for ongoing surveillance and adaptive vaccine strategies. This study's results underscore the dynamic nature of the influenza A(H1N1)pdm09 virus and its potential effect on public health. The observed genetic variations highlight the need for continual monitoring and adaptive intervention strategies to combat the evolving virus effectively. This study contributes to the global effort of understanding and managing influenza outbreaks, providing valuable insights for future pandemic preparedness and response.
