## Abstract

Drug resistance remains a major obstacle to effective antiviral therapy. Predicting which mutations may arise under drug pressure is key to designing robust treatments. We apply a maximum entropy (MaxEnt) statistical model, which captures evolutionary constraints from sequences, to analyze resistance in viral proteases. In HIV protease, both resistance and vitality correlate not only with statistical energy, E M a x E n t , but also with the number of mutated positions, raising the question of whether E M a x E n t offers predictive insights beyond mutation count. To reduce this confounding effect, we examined HCV protease, where resistance substitutions cluster within a narrow, low- E M a x E n t range. This restricted mutational space allows E M a x E n t to filter evolutionary pathways and, combined with drug-specific vitality trends, helps identify therapies more resilient to viral escape.

Drug resistance is one of the most serious health threats confronting medicine in the 21st century. The World Health Organization has warned that the growing prevalence of drug-resistant infections could undermine the effectiveness of existing treatments and reverse decades of medical progress ( 1 , 2 ). If current trends continue, we may face a future where common infections once considered treatable become life-threatening again, evoking the medical uncertainty of the preantibiotic era. This potential catastrophe highlights the urgent need for predictive strategies that can anticipate resistance pathways and guide the design of more durable therapies.

Among the many challenges facing rational drug design, the emergence of resistance in chronic viral infections remains especially difficult to address. A prominent example is HIV, the virus responsible for AIDS ( 3 , 4 ). Although potent antiretroviral therapies have transformed the disease from fatal to manageable, the virus continues to acquire resistance-associated mutations ( 5 , 6 ) that undermine long-term treatment success ( 7 ). To illustrate the molecular basis of this phenomenon, Fig. 1 presents a schematic depiction of how drug-resistant mutations can alter the geometry of the target binding pocket. These conformational changes reduce inhibitor binding while maintaining substrate recognition, thereby diminishing drug efficacy without compromising viral fitness. As these mutations accumulate, maintaining effective viral suppression becomes increasingly difficult ( 8 , 9 ). This evolutionary pressure highlights the need for predictive frameworks that can anticipate resistance pathways and inform the design of more durable therapeutic agents.

A central challenge in designing effective strategies against drug resistance lies in our inability to anticipate how pathogens will evolve under drug pressure ( 10 ). Since target enzymes can accumulate resistance-conferring mutations through diverse and often stochastic pathways, conventional drug design approaches face inherent limitations ( 11 ). In particular, current computational methods often lack predictive power when confronted with the vast combinatorial space of possible mutations ( 12 – 15 ). Experimental approaches such as directed evolution or high-throughput screening also remain reactive, as they require waiting for resistance to emerge before countermeasures can be developed ( 16 ).

Several computational and structural approaches have been developed to investigate mutations that render drug resistance ( 17 – 19 ). A notable example is the substrate envelope hypothesis proposed by Schiffer and colleagues, which explains resistance based on the structural overlap between inhibitors and natural substrates ( 17 ). This approach suggests that inhibitors interacting with residues extending beyond the conserved substrate envelope are more susceptible to drug-resistant mutations ( 19 ). These mutations tend to selectively impair inhibitor binding while preserving substrate recognition, thereby reducing drug efficacy. However, this method explains only the geometric overlap and does not account for free energy contributions.

Another strategy involves decomposing the binding free energy into residue-specific contributions to identify sites with a significant influence on ligand binding ( 20 – 22 ). However, the calculated binding energies in such studies often show poor correlation with experimental measurements, limiting their predictive value ( 23 , 24 ). Moreover, binding affinity alone does not fully explain the mechanistic basis of resistance, particularly when catalytic efficiency and functional constraints, which serve as major evolutionary pressures, are not considered ( 25 ).

A more relevant metric for characterizing resistance is the vitality factor ( γ ), which integrates catalytic efficiency and inhibitor binding into a single expression ( 26 ): [1] γ = { K i ( k cat / K M ) } mut / { K i ( k cat / K M ) } WT .

Here, K i reflects the inhibitor binding affinity, and k c a t / K M represents the enzymatic efficiency toward the natural substrate. A mutation that preserves or enhances catalytic efficiency while significantly reducing drug binding (i.e., increasing K i ) will yield a high vitality value, indicating likely resistance. In contrast, mutations that impair catalytic function or fail to disrupt inhibitor binding will result in lower vitality values ( 14 ).

We have previously applied the vitality metric to evaluate HIV-1 protease resistance across several inhibitors, including ritonavir (RTV) and saquinavir (SQV). We also extended this framework to the HCV protease system ( 27 ). However, these efforts revealed two key limitations. First, the accuracy of computed vitality values is constrained by the cumulative uncertainty in kinetic parameters, including K i , k c a t , and K M . Second, and more critically, the sheer number of possible resistance-conferring mutations presents a major computational challenge. To address these issues, we sought to introduce additional constraints into our analysis, particularly constraints informed by evolutionary sequence data. This motivation was strengthened by recent advances in applying maximum entropy (MaxEnt) models to enzyme function and design ( 28 , 29 ). Encouraged by these developments, we explored whether a MaxEnt-based framework could enhance resistance prediction. Importantly, HIV-1 protease is exceptionally well-suited for this approach, owing to the availability of a large, curated dataset of resistance-associated sequences and phenotypes ( 30 , 31 ).

We began our analysis by examining the relationship between the MaxEnt statistical energy, E M a x E n t , and experimentally observed drug resistance, as quantified by ln ( F o l d n ) . Somewhat unexpectedly, we observed a consistent positive correlation across all eight HIV-1 protease inhibitors, indicating that mutants with higher E M a x E n t values, which reflect lower evolutionary conservation, tend to exhibit increased resistance. This trend suggests that drug resistance often emerges through destabilizing mutations ( 32 , 33 ): as protein stability decreases, the ability of inhibitors to bind effectively diminishes. However, beyond a certain point, further destabilization is likely to impair enzymatic function altogether. To explore this trade-off more directly, we analyzed the correlation between E M a x E n t and ln-transformed catalytic efficiency, ln ( k c a t / K M ) , and l n ( v i t a l i t y ) across the same mutation set. Interestingly, we found that for a subset of drugs, these quantities show negative correlations with E M a x E n t . This raised the question of whether vitality simply represents the accumulation of mutations, prompting us to examine its relationship with Hamming distance. In these cases, increased resistance appears to require a functional cost, as mutants must sacrifice catalytic efficiency or overall vitality to escape inhibition ( 34 , 35 ). Such drugs may therefore be more robust to resistance evolution, since viable resistance-conferring mutations are constrained by functional degradation. Further analysis revealed that vitality is also positively correlated with the number of mutations (i.e., Hamming distance from the wild type). This observation suggests that resistance evolution is constrained: the virus cannot easily acquire resistance through specific low-cost mutational pathways, and must instead sacrifice stability and function to escape inhibition.

Taken together, our findings highlight a functional trade-off underlying drug resistance evolution. While resistance is positively correlated with E M a x E n t , we also observed that both E M a x E n t and vitality increase with the number of mutations, suggesting that acquiring resistance often comes at a cost to stability or catalytic efficiency. In view of this number dependence in HIV protease, we extended our analysis to HCV protease, where resistance-associated substitutions are much more restricted. In this system, resistant variants cluster at low E M a x E n t values, reflecting a limited mutational space. This restriction enables E M a x E n t to act as an evolutionary filter, providing a basis for predicting accessible resistance pathways and guiding the design of robust therapeutic candidates.


## Results


## Maximum Entropy Modeling.

To assess the relationship between evolutionary constraints and phenotypic drug resistance, we first analyzed the fold resistance ( Fold n ) of HIV-1 protease variants across eight clinically relevant inhibitors. As shown in Fig. 2 and Table 1 , we observed a consistent positive correlation between MaxEnt statistical energy, E M a x E n t , and the ln-transformed resistance values ln ( F o l d n ) . Since higher values correspond to less conserved sequences, this trend suggests that resistance is often achieved through mutations that are less accessible under natural evolutionary pressure.

Previous studies have suggested that E M a x E n t may inversely relate to protein stability in certain functional regions ( 36 , 37 ), potentially reflecting the evolutionary cost of destabilizing mutations. While such findings may explain part of the observed resistance patterns, we do not consider E M a x E n t a direct mechanistic predictor of resistance. Instead, we interpret it as a proxy for evolutionary accessibility, indicating how likely a mutation is to arise under natural selection. To further investigate this interpretation, we examined whether both ln ( F o l d n ) and vitality are also correlated with the number of mutated positions (i.e., the Hamming distance from the wild type). As shown in SI Appendix , Fig. S1 , both quantities exhibit strong positive correlations with mutation count, mirroring their relationships with resistance. These results suggest that the statistical and functional trends observed may, in part, reflect cumulative mutational burden. In the following sections, we assess how drug-specific differences in the vitality— E M a x E n t relationship can inform the design of inhibitors that impose greater evolutionary constraints on the virus.


## Trade-Offs between Protein Stability, Catalytic Efficiency, and Drug Resistance Shape the Evolutionary Dynamics of Inhibitor Efficacy.

We next investigated how catalytic properties relate to evolutionary energy. SI Appendix , Figs. S2–S4 illustrate the correlations between E M a x E n t and the kinetic parameters k c a t and k c a t / K M , respectively. While k c a t remains approximately constant across variants, a clear negative correlation is observed between k c a t / K M and E M a x E n t , suggesting that substrate binding efficiency decreases with reduced protein stability ( 38 ).

To assess whether these changes affect overall enzymatic function in the context of inhibition, we analyzed the relationship between vitality ( γ ) and E M a x E n t . Fig. 3 and Table 2 summarize this analysis. Despite the compensatory definition of vitality—which balances catalytic efficiency and inhibitor binding—most inhibitors still exhibit a weakly positive correlation. This suggests that even as protein stability decreases, some variants retain sufficient catalytic activity despite reduced stability, allowing them to escape inhibition, highlighting the nuanced evolutionary trade-offs underlying drug evasion.

With these observations, we next examined how different drugs vary in their correlation between l n ( v i t a l i t y ) and E M a x E n t . As shown in Fig. 3 , most inhibitors exhibit weakly positive correlations. A few inhibitors, including lopinavir (LPV) and QF34 (a pseudopeptide protease inhibitor with subnanomolar potency and a noncanonical binding mode), show near-zero or negative slopes. This trend implies that these drugs may impose greater functional constraints on resistance evolution, as viable escape mutations tend to reduce enzymatic efficiency. These slope differences are observed under a shared set of mutation profiles, indicating that drug-specific vitality behavior is not solely driven by mutation count. In such cases, where the same mutations affect multiple drugs, differences in vitality can offer insights into how inhibitor-specific interactions influence functional outcomes. In cases where the same set of mutations confers resistance to multiple inhibitors, observed differences in vitality may reflect drug-specific variations in binding affinity. For a given mutant, catalytic efficiency, k c a t / K M , typically remains constant across drugs. Under this assumption, changes in vitality largely arise from differences in inhibitor binding, which is captured by K i . Therefore, vitality offers a practical metric for comparing functional consequences of resistance across drugs. This framework highlights the potential of using vitality— E M a x E n t correlations to guide drug selection under evolutionary pressure.

Similar to our observations with ln ( F o l d n ) , we also found that vitality is positively correlated with the number of mutations ( SI Appendix , Fig. S5 ). This result indicates that both statistical energy and functional metrics partially reflect cumulative mutational burden. Rather than diminishing the relevance of these findings, this correlation reinforces the notion that resistance cannot be easily achieved through single-step mutational pathways and typically requires the accumulation of multiple substitutions. Importantly, even when the same set of mutations is considered, different inhibitors exhibit distinct vitality values. This observation raises a key mechanistic question: What drives the drug-specific differences in vitality under a fixed mutational background? ## Structural Analysis of Binding Differences.

To investigate the molecular origins of the observed differences in vitality across inhibitors, we selected two representative drug–protease complexes for comparative analysis. The first comparison set consists of wild-type HIV protease bound to nelfinavir (NFV) and lopinavir (LPV), respectively. The second set includes a mutant protease variant with moderately elevated E M a x E n t , also bound to NFV and LPV. The calculation of absolute binding free energy in the present case is very challenging, since we are dealing with very large and flexible drugs. Nevertheless, we applied the PDLD/S-LRA-[YEAR] ( 40 – 42 ), in which solvent polarization is represented by Langevin dipoles and the binding free energy is obtained through the linear response approximation by averaging charged and uncharged states. A surface constrained all-atom solvent (SCAAS) model ( 43 ) with an explicit solvent sphere of 22 Å centered on the inhibitor was used, and the nonelectrostatic contribution was evaluated using the empirical approach originally described by Hansson and Åqvist ( 44 ), in which the nonelectrostatic component of binding free energy is scaled by an empirical c 2 factor ( 41 ). In these calculations, we employed a relatively high protein dielectric constant ( ε p = 20 ). This choice reflects the significant water penetration into the protease binding pocket, the high flexibility of the ligands, and the limited conformational sampling available in the present analysis ( 45 ). Under these conditions, the nonelectrostatic term, which incorporates the empirical c 2 correction, was able to reproduce the overall experimental trends. Hydrophobic, van der Waals, and entropic contributions emerged as the dominant factors determining binding affinity. Our results, while qualitative in nature, suggest that in HIV-1 protease, there are no single highly specific interactions that account for drug resistance. Rather, resistance appears to arise from broader changes in the balance of nonelectrostatic contributions. Consistent with the vitality analysis, the inhibitor that maintains lower vitality at high E M a x E n t can be considered the more effective drug. Further decomposition of binding interactions will be carried out using the PDLD/S-LRA approach.


## Exploring the Drug-Resistance of HCV Protease.

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) encodes the NS3 serine protease together with its NS4A cofactor, forming the NS3/4A complex that is essential for viral replication and serves as the molecular target of direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) ( 46 , 47 ). Inhibitors of this protease have been instrumental in achieving high cure rates for chronic HCV infection ( 48 ). Clinical and virological studies indicate that the evolutionary repertoire of the HCV protease is markedly constrained, with resistance-associated substitutions concentrated at a few characteristic positions ( 49 – 52 ). Most resistant isolates contain single substitutions, double substitutions are uncommon, and higher-order variants are rarely observed in clinical settings ( 50 , 53 ). This restricted mutational landscape contrasts sharply with HIV-1 protease, where a broad distribution of multisite mutation patterns results in a wide range of Hamming distances from the wild-type ( 31 ).

We next analyzed the relationship between E M a x E n t and the observed binding free energy Δ G drug ( o b s ) across four HCV protease inhibitors using experimental data compiled and transformed by Jindal et al. ( 27 ), as shown in Fig. 4 . With the wild-type sequence fixed at E M a x E n t = 0 while most variant data points lie farther from this reference, the fitted regressions tend to show an apparent positive trend. Under these conditions, asunaprevir, danoprevir, and vaniprevir show positive slopes, indicating that variants with higher E M a x E n t values tend to display weaker binding affinities. Thus, the correlation between E M a x E n t and binding affinity was observed for some inhibitors but not consistently across all four compounds. To further evaluate resistance phenotypes, we considered the relationship between E M a x E n t and the ln-transformed half-maximal effective concentration, ln ( E C 50 ) , using experimental data reported by Lenz et al. ( 54 ), as shown in SI Appendix , Fig. S6 . The fitted lines do not support a simple linear association between the two variables. Part of the apparent trend is driven by clusters of double mutants at higher E M a x E n t , which provide leverage to the fit and reflect the influence of Hamming distance. In these data, most substitutions fall within a narrow band of E M a x E n t values, approximately 4.5 to 6.8, where single substitutions cluster closely, while clusters of double mutants at higher E M a x E n t values provided leverage to the fit. As a complementary analysis, the relationship between E M a x E n t and the ln-transformed augmented vitality was evaluated ( SI Appendix , Fig. S7 ). Augmented vitality follows the same definition as γ , with calculated k c a t values substituted since experimental measurements were missing, which extends the dataset beyond variants with complete kinetics. Despite this broader coverage, the regression fits were fragmentary, as for several inhibitors only three variants were available. As a result, the observed slopes were inconsistent and did not establish a robust correlation.

In summary, analysis of HCV NS3/4A protease illustrates both the strengths and limitations of sequence-derived energy measures in the context of a constrained mutational landscape. Binding free energy comparisons revealed that E M a x E n t can sometimes reflect differences in inhibitor susceptibility, though the effect is not consistent across all compounds. Examination of E C 50 values showed that most substitutions cluster within a narrow range of E M a x E n t , underscoring the limited resolution of the MaxEnt model for distinguishing among single mutants. Finally, augmented vitality provided a composite view that incorporates both binding and catalysis, suggesting possible drug-specific responses but remaining inconclusive due to sparse kinetic data.


## Our Analyses Indicated that Statistical Energy Is Best Interpreted as a Descriptive Measure of Evolutionary Accessibility Rather than a Predictor of Resistance.

In both HIV-1 and HCV proteases, E M a x E n t captured broad differences in mutational space, with higher values corresponding to variants that are less frequently observed. The clustering of HCV substitutions within a narrow low-energy range reinforces that accessible mutations are limited, which can simplify the framing of resistance studies. At the same time, the lack of consistent correlations across drugs underscores that E M a x E n t alone cannot identify resistance pathways or guide inhibitor selection. Instead, its main value lies in providing an evolutionary context that complements functional measures such as vitality. Future progress will require broader kinetic and binding data, which can be integrated with statistical models to develop a more reliable understanding of how resistance emerges.


## Discussion

In this study, we analyzed resistance evolution in both HIV-1 and HCV proteases through the lens of sequence-derived statistical energy. In HIV-1, linear regressions between E M a x E n t and experimental resistance metrics captured broad trends but should not be interpreted as strict correlations, given the size and variability of the dataset. Much of the apparent association was in fact driven by Hamming distance, underscoring a key limitation of the MaxEnt model in this system. Moreover, vitality did not always increase with E M a x E n t : for some inhibitors, the relationship was weak or even negative, reflecting cases where escape requires sacrificing stability or catalytic efficiency. These drug-specific differences motivated us to examine HCV protease, where the mutational repertoire is far more restricted and thus provides a clearer context for evaluating how vitality– E M a x E n t relationships vary across inhibitors.

Within the HIV-1 protease system, drug-specific patterns became evident when vitality was examined as a function of E M a x E n t . For most inhibitors, vitality showed weakly positive slopes, indicating that resistance could emerge while variants retained sufficient catalytic activity. In contrast, for drugs such as lopinavir and QF34, the slopes were near zero or negative, suggesting that viable escape routes required a functional penalty, either in stability or catalytic turnover. Such inhibitors effectively force the protease to sacrifice performance to escape inhibition, making them comparatively more robust against resistance. These observations illustrate how the vitality– E M a x E n t relationship can capture mechanistic differences among drugs even under a common set of mutations. However, because these trends remain strongly influenced by mutation count, a system with a narrower mutational space is required to disentangle accessibility from function, motivating the subsequent analysis of HCV protease.

In contrast to HIV-1, the analysis of HCV protease provided a system where mutational burden is less confounding, since most resistance variants are limited to one or two substitutions. This restriction allowed correlations between E M a x E n t and drug response to be examined with greater clarity. For several inhibitors, variants with higher E M a x E n t values tended to show weaker binding affinities, suggesting that evolutionary accessibility and drug susceptibility may sometimes align. However, this effect was not consistent across all compounds—for example, telaprevir showed little or no correlation—highlighting that the relationship between statistical energy and resistance is drug-specific rather than universal. Moreover, most substitutions fall within a narrow band of E M a x E n t values, which limits the resolution of this metric for distinguishing among single mutants. This clustering is consistent with clinical evidence that HCV resistance is dominated by single substitutions, and it underscores that E M a x E n t alone cannot pinpoint which specific variants are most likely to arise. Nevertheless, recognizing that the accessible mutational space is highly constrained is itself valuable, as it reduces the trajectories that need to be considered in simulations and helps frame how drug robustness should be evaluated. A complementary analysis in HCV used the augmented vitality metric, which combines inhibitor binding with catalytic turnover and allows inclusion of variants lacking complete kinetic measurements. Although this expanded the dataset, the regression fits were fragmentary, since for several inhibitors only a handful of variants were available. As a result, the slopes observed were inconsistent and did not support a robust linear relationship. Still, the results hinted that the association between evolutionary penalty and drug robustness may differ across inhibitors, with some compounds potentially enforcing trade-offs in stability or catalytic activity as the protease escapes inhibition. Because augmented vitality integrates multiple functional dimensions, it remains a conceptually valuable metric, but additional experimental measurements will be required to establish its predictive power in the HCV system.

In summary, the comparison of HIV-1 and HCV proteases illustrates how sequence-derived statistical energy can inform but not fully define resistance trajectories. HIV-1 offered large datasets where trends between E M a x E n t and resistance metrics were apparent but heavily confounded by mutation count, while HCV provided a narrower mutational landscape that allowed drug-specific patterns to be seen more clearly. Across both systems, vitality added an important functional dimension, revealing when inhibitors forced trade-offs in stability or catalytic turnover. These results emphasize that resistance prediction cannot rely on E M a x E n t alone but benefits from combining evolutionary metrics with functional readouts. Continued integration of statistical models with experimental binding and kinetic data will be essential for developing antivirals that remain effective under evolutionary pressure.


## Materials and Methods


## Evaluating E MaxEnt .

To quantify the evolutionary constraints acting on HIV-1 protease, we employed a generative statistical model based on the maximum entropy (MaxEnt) principle, as developed in prior studies ( 36 , 37 , 55 ). Homologous sequences of HIV-1 protease were retrieved from sequence databases and aligned using multiple sequence alignment (MSA). From the MSA, we calculated position-specific conservation and pairwise residue covariation, which served as constraints to parameterize a MaxEnt Hamiltonian of the form: [2] E MaxEnt ( S ) = ∑ i h i S i + ∑ i > j J ij S i S j .

Here, E ( S ) represents the statistical energy of sequence S , and the parameters h i and J i j are optimized such that the model reproduces the observed single-site and pairwise frequencies in the MSA. The energy E ( S ) is inversely related to the sequence’s evolutionary plausibility; lower energy values correspond to higher evolutionary fitness.

In the context of enzyme function, E M a x E n t has been shown to correlate with both protein stability and catalytic activity in certain functional regions ( 36 , 37 ). We therefore calculated E M a x E n t values for a series of HIV-1 protease variants with known drug resistance profiles and examined correlations between E M a x E n t , enzyme kinetic parameters (e.g., k c a t , k c a t / K M ), and drug resistance measures such as fold-change in experimental drug-resistance values. This approach allowed us to evaluate whether evolutionary information captured by the MaxEnt model reflects biochemical properties relevant to drug resistance and catalytic performance.


## Stanford HIV Drug Resistance Database.

To analyze the relationship between HIV-1 protease mutations and drug resistance, we utilized publicly available data from the Stanford HIV Drug Resistance Database (HIVdb, refs. 30 and 31 ), available at https://hivdb.stanford.edu/ . Specifically, we accessed the “PI Mutation Pattern & Susceptibility” dataset, which aggregates phenotypic susceptibility data and resistance-associated mutation patterns from over 259,000 clinical HIV-1 Group M isolates. This dataset includes over 1,000 distinct mutation patterns, each associated with median fold resistance values to multiple protease inhibitors, as measured by the PhenoSense assay.

Mutation patterns containing mixtures at key drug resistance positions were excluded following the database’s default curation criteria. For each unique mutation profile, we extracted the median fold-change values, Fold n , for available drugs and used them to construct a mutation-resistance matrix. This matrix formed the basis for subsequent statistical and thermodynamic modeling analyses. Data were downloaded in tab-delimited format as provided on the HIVdb website. All ln-transformed quantities (e.g., Fold n , vitality) are expressed as natural logarithms (ln), consistent with thermodynamic formulations ( Δ G = - R T ln K ).


## Binding Free Energy Calculations Using PDLD/S-LRA-[YEAR].

The noncovalent binding free energies of inhibitors were estimated with the Protein Dipoles Langevin Dipoles method in its linear response approximation (PDLD/S-LRA-[YEAR]), as implemented in the MOLARIS-XG package ( 40 ). In this approach, water molecules are represented semimacroscopically as Langevin dipoles, while the binding free energy is obtained by averaging the electrostatic contributions of the charged and uncharged states within a thermodynamic cycle. An explicit solvation sphere of 22 Å centered on the ligand was employed and embedded in a bulk continuum. The protein dielectric constant was set to ε p = 20 , consistent with previous validations ( 45 ), to account for water penetration into the binding pocket, conformational flexibility of the ligand, and the limited sampling of conformational space. The nonelectrostatic component, which is system-dependent and less specific, was scaled empirically using the c 2 -scaling procedure ( 44 ) to balance the electrostatic term and reproduce experimental trends. Multiple snapshots from molecular dynamics relaxation trajectories were used to ensure adequate sampling of the binding energetics.
