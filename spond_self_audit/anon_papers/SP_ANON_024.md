## ABSTRACT

Understanding whether the neutralizing antibody (NAb) response impacts HIV-1 superinfection and how superinfection subsequently modulates the NAb response can help clarify correlates of protection from HIV exposures and better delineate pathways of NAb development. We examined associations between the development of NAb and the occurrence of superinfection in a well-characterized, antiretroviral therapy (ART)-naive, primary infection cohort of men who have sex with men. Deep sequencing was applied to blood plasma samples from the cohort to detect cases of superinfection. We compared the NAb activity against autologous and heterologous viruses between 10 participants with intrasubtype B superinfection and 19 monoinfected controls, matched to duration of infection and risk behavior. Three to 6 months after primary infection, individuals who would later become superinfected had significantly weaker NAb activity against tier 1 subtype B viruses ( P = 0.003 for SF-162 and P = 0.017 for NL4-3) and marginally against autologous virus ( P = 0.054). Lower presuperinfection NAb responses correlated with weaker gp120 binding and lower plasma total IgG titers. Soon after superinfection, the NAb response remained lower, but between 2 and 3 years after primary infection, NAb levels strengthened and reached those of controls. Superinfecting viruses were typically not susceptible to neutralization by presuperinfection plasma. These observations suggest that recently infected individuals with a delayed NAb response against primary infecting and tier 1 subtype B viruses are more susceptible to superinfection.

IMPORTANCE Our findings suggest that within the first year after HIV infection, a relatively weak neutralizing antibody response against primary and subtype-specific neutralization-sensitive viruses increases susceptibility to superinfection in the face of repeated exposures. As natural infection progresses, the immune response strengthens significantly in some superinfected individuals. These findings will inform HIV vaccine design by providing testable correlates of protection from initial HIV infection.

## INTRODUCTION

The isolation of highly broad and potent monoclonal neutralizing antibodies (NAb) ( 1 ) and passive immunization studies in animals ( 2 ) have led to renewed interest in the design of an effective antibody-based human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) vaccine. HIV-1 superinfection provides a unique opportunity to study the development of protective NAb, as the reinfection of an individual with a second virus can be analogous to the failure of a vaccine to produce a protective immune response to subsequent challenges ( 3 ). A recent study showed that HIV superinfection resembles primary HIV infection, supporting its use as a model to study immune correlates that may be able to prevent infection after HIV exposures ( 4 ). Understanding the impact of immune parameters on susceptibility to superinfection, as well as the impact of superinfection on the subsequent immune response, can bridge knowledge gaps associated with correlates of protection, broadening of humoral responses, optimization of vaccination strategies, and better use of animal models ( 3 , 4 ).

We previously found that, before superinfection, the NAb responses in three men who have sex with men (MSM) against baseline autologous and two heterologous viruses were weaker than in controls ( 5 ). Two of these individuals were included in the present study. Similarly, three participants from a Zambian serodiscordant couple cohort had a relatively weaker NAb response against their baseline autologous virus before superinfection ( 6 ). Alternatively, the NAb response to a cross-clade panel of heterologous viruses in six Kenyan female sex workers was less broad 1 year after primary infection but not immediately before superinfection ( 7 ). Also, when assessed 5 years after primary infection, superinfected Kenyan women developed significantly broader NAb responses than controls ( 8 ).

The comparison of these studies is hampered by several factors: small numbers of well-defined superinfection cases, mixed inter- and intrasubtype exposures, limited longitudinal assessment of NAb, and the lack of a standardized panel of globally representative heterologous viruses. Deep sequencing provides a more rapid and accurate platform to examine instances of intrasubtype superinfection, detecting circulating minority variants as infrequent as 0.25% of the viral population ( 9 ). Similarly, high-throughput neutralization assays have been optimized with standardized panels of heterologous viruses, allowing these assays to be highly predictive of neutralization breadth on a larger panel ( 10 – 12 ). We used these methodologies on the large, well-characterized San Diego Primary Infection Resource Consortium to detect cases of superinfection and to compare the development of NAb between superinfected participants and those who remained monoinfected.


## RESULTS


## Study group characteristics.

Superinfection was confirmed in 10 study participants, and monoinfection was confirmed in 19 case controls (see Table S1 and Fig. S1 in the supplemental material). All were MSM, the majority of whom reported unprotected receptive anal intercourse in the previous month and multiple sexual partners of unknown status in the previous 2 months (Table S1). Seven instances of superinfection occurred in the first year after the estimated date of infection (EDI), and three occurred in the second year ( Fig. 1 ). The superinfection window was defined as the period between the last time point with monoinfection and the first time point with dual infection, as defined by analysis of deep sequencing data. The superinfection date was calculated as the midpoint between the two time points ( 13 ). All infections were with HIV-1 subtype B viruses. ## NAb responses before superinfection.

Each superinfection case was matched to two monoinfection controls based on time after EDI and viral load (Table S3). At 3 months after EDI, neutralization titers against the tier 2 six-virus panel ( 11 ) were essentially zero. However, NAb responses were measureable and found to be significantly lower against SF-162 ( P = 0.003) and NL4-3 ( P = 0.017) ( Fig. 2B ). To assess whether the difference was specific to subtype B, neutralization was tested against tier 1 viruses of subtypes A and C (Table S4). No differences were observed (Fig. S2).

## Longitudinal NAb responses against heterologous viruses after superinfection.

Overview of the longitudinal NAb response against tier 2 viruses demonstrated a lack of breadth in the cohort until 2 years post-EDI, after which low-level breadth developed ( Fig. 3A ). Against tier 1 viruses, the NAb response of cases was initially lower than that of controls, but this pattern was reversed 1 year later ( Fig. 3B and C ). For a direct comparison, we studied one time point early after superinfection (median of 12.1 months after EDI; interquartile range [EDI], 7.3 to 22.1 months) and another time point late after superinfection (median, 15.9 months after EDI; IQR, 12.6 to 32.2 months). Each case was matched to two controls based on time after EDI and viral load (Tables S5 and S6). The NAb response was not significantly different at the early ( Fig. 4A ) or at the late ( Fig. 4B ) time points. Notably, case G59 developed substantial NAb breadth and potency (Table S7a). By 32 months after EDI, this participant's plasma was able to neutralize all six tier 2 viruses (plasma dilution conferring 50% inhibition [ID 50 ] of >50), achieving the highest neutralization score of the study cohort (2.14). Also of note, the presuperinfection plasma viral load was lower in cases than in controls, but this pattern was reversed after superinfection (Fig. S3a), similar to the NAb response ( Fig. 3 ). Among cases, there was a moderate correlation between the viral load and neutralization of NL4-3 (correlation coefficient [ r s ] = 0.29, P = 0.07) and SF-162 ( r s = 0.30, P = 0.06), almost reaching statistical significance (Fig. S3b and c). No correlation was observed in the monoinfection group.

## NAb response against autologous virus after superinfection.

We assessed the postsuperinfection NAb response against autologous virus at three discrete time points: 12, 24, and 36 months after the EDI. When 12-month plasma was evaluated against 3-month autologous virus (seven cases and 13 controls), controls mounted more potent NAb than the superinfected group ( P = 0.055) ( Fig. 5A ). Statistical significance was not reached due to case G59, who neutralized his 3-month autologous virus extremely potently (ID 50 of 13,336) (Table S7b). There was no difference between the groups (seven cases and 16 controls) in the NAb response to the 12-month (i.e., contemporaneous) autologous virus ( P = 0.25). At 24 months after EDI, the four cases remaining off antiretroviral therapy (ART) had significantly lower NAb activity against contemporaneous autologous virus than the 11 available controls ( P = 0.017) ( Fig. 5B ). There were no differences noted when the 24-month NAb response was evaluated against the 3-month and 12-month autologous viruses (four superinfected cases and 7 to 10 monoinfected controls) ( Fig. 5B ). By 36 months after EDI (IQR, 35.2 to 37.5 months), ART initiation had reduced the evaluable sample size to three cases and 6 to 9 controls, and no NAb differences were detected ( Fig. 5C ).

## Neutralization susceptibility of superinfecting viral strains.

To test the neutralization susceptibility of the superinfecting virus, two env clones were selected that represented the superinfecting viral lineage ( Fig. 1 ) and three that represented the primary infecting lineage (Fig. S2 and Table S8). Presuperinfection plasma (median, 4.0 months after EDI; IQR, 3.9 to 8.8 months) was tested against superinfecting clones. Each case was matched to two monoinfection controls based on time post-EDI ( Fig. 6A ). Superinfecting viruses generally were not susceptible to neutralization by presuperinfection plasma (median titer of <20; IQR, 20 to 29), and they were not neutralized by control plasma (median titer of <20; IQR, 20 to 34) ( Fig. 6A ). However, one superinfecting clone (L78 clone 2) was neutralized by the participant who would later become superinfected by that clone (ID 50 of 1,075) and, to a lesser degree, by the controls (ID 50 of 218 and 189). Presuperinfection plasma was also tested against the three clones that phylogenetically represented the initial strain from each case ( Fig. 6B ). Comparing mean titers, cases mounted stronger NAb responses against their primary infecting virus than controls ( P = 0.021), as expected ( 10 ).

## Binding to gp120 before superinfection.

Since tier 1 neutralization mostly reflects gp120-binding capacity ( 14 ), blood plasma drawn at a median of 3 months after EDI (IQR, 2.52 to 4.71 months) was tested against gp120 monomers of subtypes A (BG505), B (JR-FL), and C (IAVIC22) with enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) ( Fig. 7 ). Subtype B, but not subtype A or C, gp120 binding trended to be lower in individuals who would later become superinfected ( P = 0.058) ( Fig. 7B ). Furthermore, total plasma IgG levels were significantly lower in the presuperinfection group ( P = 0.027) (Fig. S3a) and were positively correlated with subtype B gp120 binding ( P = 0.033) (Fig. S3b). Comparing gp120 binding to viral neutralization ( Fig. 8 ), we observed that binding was positively correlated with NL4-3 neutralization in the monoinfection group but not in the presuperinfection group. Importantly, NL4-3 neutralization titers in the presuperinfection group were essentially zero, preventing an accurate evaluation. Binding of gp120 was positively correlated with SF-162 neutralization both in the presuperinfection group and in the monoinfection group ( Fig. 8 ).

## DISCUSSION

We sought to better understand how preexisting NAb responses are associated with HIV-1 intrasubtype B superinfection and how these responses change after superinfection. The main findings of this study were the following: (i) individuals who would become superinfected had lower NAb responses to heterologous tier 1 subtype B viruses and a trend for lower NAb responses to autologous viruses than individuals who remained monoinfected; (ii) following superinfection, the NAb response reached the same level as that in monoinfected controls; and (iii) one superinfected individual developed the broadest and most potent NAb response in the cohort. This may point to superinfected people having the ability, and even the strong ability, to generate an antibody response, just that this response is delayed for unclear reasons.

Comparing humoral immune responses in natural infection to responses elicited via vaccination and determining the characteristics associated with protection from superinfection may help inform vaccine design ( 4 ). Using a larger sample size than previous studies and a larger panel of heterologous viruses, this study confirmed that increased susceptibility to intrasubtype B superinfection was correlated with a delayed NAb response to the primary infecting virus and to subtype B tier 1 viruses. This conclusion supports the concept of a window of susceptibility in the first 1 to 2 years of natural HIV infection, during which individuals that develop a slower humoral immune response are rendered more susceptible to superinfection ( 5 ). As a corollary, the NAb response of monoinfected participants may have been sufficient to decrease susceptibility to superinfection. This notion would be corroborated by the finding that narrow V1V2 antibodies were associated with a lower risk of HIV infection in the RV144 trial and appeared to mediate antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and limited tier 1 neutralization ( 15 ). Also, in vaccinated macaques challenged with simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV), viremic control was mediated by ADCC and viral inhibition ( 16 ), suggesting that tier 1 neutralization is a surrogate for an unmeasured antibody-mediated correlate of protection. However, several participants in the present study mounted modest NAb against SF-162 and nevertheless became superinfected. Furthermore, primary tier 1 subtype B isolates were not tested in the present study, and in RV144, human vaccinees acquired HIV-1 infection despite eliciting NAb against tier 1 viruses ( 17 ). An alternative explanation is that despite matching for sexual risk factors, individuals remained monoinfected because they were somehow not sufficiently challenged with a superinfection exposure.

A study that investigated six Kenyan female sex workers with inter- and intrasubtype superinfection showed that, unlike most San Diegan and all Zambian cases ( 6 ), most Kenyan women could neutralize their autologous superinfecting variants before reinfection ( 7 ). Notably, the presuperinfection plasma that was tested against superinfecting virus was collected at a median duration of infection of 4 months in our study and 12 months among Zambian participants, in contrast to 29 months in the Kenyan cohort. Sampling later in the course of infection likely allowed time for more NAb breadth to develop ( 18 ). Also, given sample availability, the superinfecting clones of four participants in our study were isolated 4 to 8 months after superinfection, allowing for the generation of escape viral mutants which could have biased the neutralization resistance we observed. It is also unknown to what extent composition of the cohort (African versus Caucasian, women versus men), risk (sex worker versus MSM), and superinfection type (inter- versus intrasubtype) may have contributed to discordant observations between our studies.

One participant in this study developed substantial NAb breadth and potency after becoming superinfected. Interestingly, this participant's env sequences were the only ones that had evidence of recombination between initial and superinfecting viruses, as we reported previously ( 19 ). Three previous studies ( 8 , 20 , 21 ) reported that superinfected individuals develop broad and potent NAb (bNAb) responses, with some of them becoming elite neutralizers. Among the elite-level neutralizers in these studies, most were superinfected soon after initial infection, with a different subtype, or both, subjecting their immune system to relatively greater antigenic diversity for longer time periods than in the present study. However, our single case is consistent with the notion that antigenic diversity can prime certain pathways of bNAb development in some individuals. Previous studies have also demonstrated that neutralization breadth and potency correlates with viral load ( 12 , 22 ), and while this correlation was moderately strong in our superinfected cohort, it did not reach significance, perhaps due to early time point sampling.

Although this report represents the largest study of intrasubtype B superinfection to date, drawing statistical conclusions at years two and three after primary infection is limited by small numbers. The difficulty in identifying and monitoring large numbers of superinfected individuals for a prolonged period is made more challenging by the earlier and broader indications for the initiation of ART ( 23 ). Our classification algorithm for superinfection was stringent, so potential cases may not have been included because their dual infection was ambiguous, likely due to missed recombination events ( 19 ). Also, the 6-virus tier 2 panel was designed for NAb breadth prediction for the International AIDS Vaccine Initiative (IAVI) Protocol G cohort ( 11 ), so it may not be accurate when used with other cohorts. In addition, the number of tier 1 viruses being tested was small, and not all tier 1 viruses have the same neutralization sensitivity profile, making conclusions about subtype-specific differences difficult.

To what extent superinfection events were modulated by observed differences in NAb responses will require further investigation using larger standardized global cohorts. The current data suggest an effective antibody-based preventative vaccine will need to elicit humoral immune responses in a different and more directed way than in natural infection to achieve broad protection.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS


## Study participants and measurement of clinical parameters.

This study included participants from the San Diego Primary Infection Resource Consortium enrolled between January 1998 and January 2007 who had deferred antiretroviral therapy (ART) for at least 6 months and had at least two stored longitudinal blood plasma samples ( 24 ). Estimated date of infection (EDI) was calculated at baseline for each participant using a series of well-defined stepwise rules that characterize stages of infection on the basis of serologic and virologic criteria ( 25 ). The University of California San Diego Human Research Protections Program approved the study protocol, consent, and all study-related procedures. All study participants provided voluntary, written informed consent before any study procedures were undertaken.


## RNA extraction and viral sequencing.

Viral RNA was isolated from cryopreserved blood plasma, and cDNA was generated as previously described ( 26 ). env C2-V3 (HXB2 coordinates 6928 to 7344), gag p24 (HXB2 coordinates 1366 to 1618), and pol reverse transcriptase (RT) (HXB2 coordinates 2709 to 3242) were PCR amplified with region-specific primers. Deep sequencing was performed on a 454 GS FLX Titanium instrument (Roche, Branford, Connecticut, USA) ( 26 ). Single-genome sequencing of env V3 (HXB2 coordinates 6928 to 7344) and pol RT (HXB2 coordinates 2708 to 3242) was also performed for a subset of participants ( 13 , 26 ).


## Sequence analysis and bioinformatics.

Deep-sequence data sets were analyzed using the bioinformatics pipeline, available as a part of the [SOFTWARE] software package ( 27 ) and the [SOFTWARE] web server ( 28 ). The methodology has been described in detail previously ( 26 ). Filtered sequence alignments from longitudinal samples were reconstructed into phylogenies for each participant using a background of 15 to 20 unrelated subtype B sequences to interrogate for divergent viral lineages. If present, a contamination check was done against a database of all laboratory-generated viral sequences and against the Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) HIV sequence database ( www.hiv.lanl.gov ). If no contamination was found, a different blood sample from the participant's same (or nearby) study time point underwent the same procedure to confirm dual infection. Highlighter plots were generated using the Highlighter tool at www.hiv.lanl.gov ( 29 ).


## Neutralizing antibody assays.

Neutralization assays were performed by Monogram Biosciences (San Francisco, CA, USA) using a previously reported in vitro viral neutralization assay ( 10 ). Tier 1 isolates were defined as highly neutralization sensitive, and tier 2 viruses were defined as moderately neutralization resistant ( 11 , 30 ) based on systematic testing against diverse panels of HIV + plasma pools ( 31 ). Neutralization titers against each pseudovirus were calculated as the reciprocal of the plasma dilution conferring 50% inhibition (ID 50 ). A neutralization score was calculated for the six-virus tier 2 panel as the weighted average of log-transformed ID 50 titers for each pseudovirus [i.e., average of log 3 (ID 50 /100) + 1; if the score was <0, then the score = 0], a measure of both breadth and potency ( 11 ). An ID 50 titer less than or equal to 20 was considered 0 for statistical calculations.


## Generation of superinfecting virus clones.

Clones of env were generated for each superinfection case as previously described (Monogram Biosciences) ( 10 ). A median of 19 clones (IQR, 18.8 to 20) was generated for each selected study time point. The individual clones were grown, purified, and tested for infectivity in a rapid single-infection-cycle assay, and viable clones were tested for susceptibility to neutralization.


## gp120 ELISAs.

Half-area 96-well ELISA plates were coated overnight at 4°C with 50 μl phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 250 ng of gp120 per well. The wells were washed four times with PBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h. Serial dilutions of sera then were added to the wells. The plates were incubated at room temperature for 1 h and then washed four times. Goat anti-human IgG F(ab′)2 was conjugated to alkaline phosphatase (Pierce), diluted 1:1,000 in PBS containing 1% BSA and 0.025% Tween 20, and added to the wells. The plates were incubated at room temperature for 1 h, washed four times, and developed by adding alkaline phosphatase substrate (Sigma) diluted in alkaline phosphatase staining buffer (pH 9.8) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The optical density (OD) at 405 nm was read on a microplate reader (Molecular Devices). Endpoint titers of the plasma antibodies were defined as the last reciprocal serum dilution at which the background-corrected OD signal was greater than or equal to 0.1. EC 50 s were calculated using GraphPad Prism.


## Statistical analysis.

Neutralization scores and ID 50 titers from the case and control groups were compared using nonparametric tests (Mann-Whitney). Two-tailed P values were reported. Spearman correlation was used to test continuous variables (gp120 binding ID 50 titers, neutralization ID 50 titers, and total plasma IgG titers). Statistical software was available from GraphPad Prism. Two significant digits were used to report P values of ≥0.10, and three digits were used for P values of <0.10.
