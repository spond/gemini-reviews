# FEXP_ANON_126

## Abstract

Sensory nerves from contracting skeletal muscles can alter autonomic functions mediated by connections in the medulla and hypothalamus. We hypothesized that low-frequency, non-voluntary, rhythmical electrical stimulation of small muscles of the hand, in contrast to continuous sensory stimulation of the overlying skin, will change functional connectivity in the brain revealing specific regions of the muscle afferent–pituitary axis. Using a 2x2 design, eight healthy young participants underwent resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI) immediately before and after a 45-min electrical stimulation session with 2 Hz to the left hand muscles and a counterbalanced session with continuous 100 Hz skin stimulation to the same region. Using standardized CONN toolbox software, functional connectivity data were analyzed. Rhythmic muscle stimulation led to significant differences in connectivity associated with the autonomic and limbic systems such as the hypothalamus, amygdala, periaqueductal gray, thalamus, basal ganglia, plus insulae and cingulate cortices. In addition, the muscle stimulation led to changes in several resting state networks. Importantly, using a tightly controlled 2x2 rsfMRI design, the different stimulation protocols were applied to each subject (within-subject design), which led to significant differences in patterns of functional connectivity changes after the muscle versus skin stimulation conditions with only a small number of participants. In conclusion, significant differences in brain networks involving autonomic and limbic systems were activated by using low-frequency, rhythmical stimulation of small hand muscles. In contrast to skin electrical stimulation, which primarily entailed exteroceptive input, muscle electrical stimulation significantly engaged neural networks implicated in interoception, including the insula and hypothalamus.

Keywords: Autonomic nervous system (ANS), Hypothalamus, Resting state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI), Neuromuscular electrical stimulation, Insula


## Introduction

Physical activity is generally accepted as a requirement for optimal health. In people subjected to prolonged bedrest confinement, inactivity results in declines in multiple physiological systems ( Convertino, 1997 ) that can be attenuated by physical activity (exercise even while in bed) ( Greenleaf, 1997 ). Thus, skeletal muscle activity, or repeated muscle contractions, almost certainly play an important role in overall homeostasis.

Previous research has revealed that skeletal muscle serves not only as a mechanical organ responsible for movement but also as a regulator of systemic physiology through both biochemical and neural pathways. On the biochemical side, active skeletal muscle releases signaling molecules known as myokines ( Lee and Ju, 2019 ). Several of these myokines exert remote actions on distant tissues—for example, interleukin-6 (IL-6) influences hepatic and adipose tissue metabolism ( Febbraio and Pedersen, 2002 ), while irisin acts on the hippocampus to promote neuroplasticity ( Jodeiri Farshbaf and Alvina, 2021 ). In parallel, skeletal muscle communicates with the central nervous system (CNS) through somatic afferent nerves, providing feedback that may influence autonomic and neuroendocrine regulation. The neural signaling contributes to ergoreflexes, including both metaboreflex and mechanoreflex components, which rely on group III and IV muscle afferents that project to key brain regions such as the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS) located in the dorsolateral medulla ( Aimo et al., 2021 ). Despite this knowledge, the details of the specific brain regions and networks involved in skeletal muscle interoception - the brain's perception of internal muscle activity - remain limited.

While skeletal muscle interoception itself remains underexplored, there is ample evidence supporting a similar communication pathway between bone interoception and the CNS. Sensory nerves innervating bone detect changes in the bone's internal environment and transmit this information to hypothalamic neuroendocrine neurons that regulate bone density and metabolic activity ( Xiao et al., 2023 ). By analogy, we hypothesize that skeletal muscle possesses a comparable, but less well characterized, interoceptive system that allows CNS feedback to enhance or preserve muscle function resulting from active movement ( e.g., muscle contractions) – the subject of this study.

Overt exercise, wherein participants exert volitional control over muscle movements, recruits numerous physiological and cognitive systems that may present as nuisance variables. Thus, for the present study, we sought to compare 45 min of non-voluntary, rhythmical electrical stimulation (2 Hz) to small muscles of the hand (Condition A) in an effort to primarily isolate the interoceptive effects of muscle contraction sensory afferents. As a critical control, we implemented 45 min of continuous stimulation (100 Hz) over the same area of the hand (Condition B), but with no muscle involvement, to primarily isolate exteroceptive effects from skin sensory afferents. We used resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI) as an objective measure before and after each stimulation session to measure possible CNS changes at a network level. Whole brain rsfMRI allows for an objective measure of potential CNS network processing changes ( Morris et al., 2022 ). This method is non-invasive and only requires participants to lie still in the scanner ( Lv et al., 2018 ), enabling the measure of synchronous activations between brain areas and networks that occur during the absence of any specific physical or cognitive task. Previous rsfMRI studies have estimated a need for sample sizes of 20–25 subjects to attain significant differential cross-group and single contrast task design results ( Thirion et al., 2007 ), which are numbers that are not easily obtained for many clinical populations of interest. Thus, we sought to implement a tightly controlled 2x2 within-subject design to determine if lower numbers of participants would be feasible for future studies that could include patient populations that are more difficult to recruit in large numbers (i.e. due to availability and/or cost limitations). From the present study, a better understanding of presumed CNS mechanisms that underlie the reported health effects of non-voluntary, low-frequency rhythmical muscle stimulation will likely lead to improvements in therapeutic protocols for bedridden patients or people unable to engage in daily exercise.


## Materials and methods


## Participants

To demonstrate feasibility for this pilot study, we recruited a closely matched set of eight (n = 8) right-handed young adult participants (average age 26 years; four females, aged 21, 24, 25 and 28, and four males aged 23, 27, 27, and 31). Participants were screened to ensure their likely availability and compliance with completing two pairs of MRI scanning sessions over the course of one to four weeks. Handedness was assessed based on 12 questions from the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory ( Oldfield, 1971 ). All participants had higher education and were either students or worked in their professions. All could perform activities of daily living independently, had a self-reported normal range of hearing and vision, and none had any previous history of major neurological or psychiatric disorders. Informed consent was obtained for all participants, and all methods were performed in accordance with guidelines approved by the West Virginia University Institutional Review Board (Initial approval date Dec 11, 2012, and approved recruitment for the present study with Aug 11, 2016 as start of protocol, and recruitment ending in Jan 12, 2018), which is accredited by the Association for the Accreditation of Human Research Protection Programs, Inc., (AAHRPP).


## Experimental design

Using a 2x2 design, participants were informed that the purpose of this study was to examine how the brain responds to different types of electrical stimulation to the left hand using a commonly available transcutaneous electrical neural stimulation (TENS) unit (ComfyTENS, TENSproducts Inc., Grand Lake, CO 80447). The participants were told that there would be two pairs of resting-state functional-connectivity magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI) scanning sessions on different weeks, including an rsfMRI scanning session before and after Condition A and another session before and after Condition B: This order was counterbalanced across participants. The first session used Condition A then B (2 Hz then 100 Hz) for participants 2, 4, 5, and 7. Scanning personnel (technicians) were blinded as to which stimulation condition was being administered. Though participants could easily distinguish the two stimulation conditions, they were blinded to the study's objectives.

After arrival, each participant was seated in a comfortable chair in a quiet room outside the MRI scanning room where they watched 10 min of a video DVD (“Planets” by National Geographic). Importantly, this pre-scanning condition served to help normalize participants’ arousal level and state of mind prior to scanning, as pre-scanning task effects have been reported to be potential factors that could bias rsfMRI outcomes ( Hasson et al., 2009 ; Lewis et al., 2009 ; Van Dijk et al., 2010 ). Heart rate and blood pressure were measured during this time. Participants then underwent a baseline “pre-stimulation” MRI scanning session that included three consecutive rsfMRI scans (that allowed for short breaks between scans if desired by the participant) plus an anatomical scan, detailed further below. They were instructed to keep as still as possible and have their eyes closed during rsfMRI scanning, which, relative to eyes open and fixated is reported to be less susceptible to effects of age, gender, and social status ( Agcaoglu et al., 2019 ). Participants came out of the scanner and back to their chair in the waiting room where heart rate and blood pressure measures were taken again. Gel electrodes were placed on their hand ( Fig. 1 A) and electrical stimulation of either Condition A or Condition B was set at a comfortable level for 45 min while they sat resting in a controlled quiet room (not watching any video). Heart rate and blood pressure was measured again after the electrical stimulation session.


> **Fig. 1:** (A) Example of electrode placement. (B) Illustration of manually created ROIs (from Table 1 ), including hypothalamus (left and right), dorsolateral medulla (left and right), and periaqueductal gray (PAG).

For Condition A, the stimulation condition hypothesized to lead to interoceptive physiological changes, the left hand was stimulated with a symmetrical biphasic current (duration of 100 microsec) at 2 Hz frequency at an amplitude set to elicit strong visible rhythmic muscle contractions of the hand (thenar and other small hand muscles) that were comfortable for the participant ( Kaada, 1982 ). Many muscles located within the hand were activated resulting in opposition movements involving the thumb to 5th digit contractions, but the fingers did not bend or touch (no recruitment of the flexor digitorum muscles).

In contrast, using the same electrode placements on the hand, Condition B consisted of a similar symmetrical biphasic current (duration of 100 microsec) that continuously stimulated only the skin afferents to produce sensation (“buzzing”), which would predominantly lead to exteroceptive inputs without any muscle contractions or discomfort ( Cheing and Luk, 2005 ). In our experience, 2 Hz stimulation at lowered intensity levels that did not activate muscles also did not lead to much sensation in the skin, and thus not suitable as a robust control condition. Thus, 100 Hz stimulation without muscle movement was selected as a critical control. With this stimulation, which is also commonly used in clinical and therapy settings, leads to activation of many sensory nerves located in the hand. Immediately after each stimulation protocol, the participants went back into the MRI scanner for a post-stimulation rsfMRI scanning session (three rsfMRI plus one anatomical scan) using identical scanning parameters as for the pre-stimulation scanning. Heart rate and blood pressure measures were obtained again at the end of this MRI scanning session. Sessions for Condition A and B were separated by 1 week or more to avoid potential carry-over effects by either stimulation type.


## Data acquisition

The imaging was conducted on a 3 T S Verio MRI scanner using a 12-channel head coil. We acquired whole-head brain volumes using a resting state design (36 axial interleaved slices at 4 × 4 × 4 mm 3 isometric voxel resolution). Blood oxygen-level dependent (BOLD) signals were collected using a continuous acquisition echo planar pulse sequence (ep2d: TR = 2.210 s, TE = 27 msec, FOV = 256 mm, 75° flip angle). Three sequential rsfMRI scans were collected, each lasting ∼6 min (164 measurements per scan), typically with only a very short break between scans (<30 s) to check the status of the participant. T1-weighted anatomical MR images were collected using a magnetization-prepared rapid acquired gradient-echo (MPRAGE) pulse sequence (1.5 mm sagittal slices, 0.625 × 0.625 mm 2 in-plane resolution, FOV = 195 mm, TI = 1100msec, 5 min 13 s), which included 128 slices that attained whole brain coverage for all participants.


## Data preprocessing

Functional and structural images were analyzed using a MATLAB/SPM-based software CONN (version 22. a) ( Morfini et al., 2023 ; Nieto-Castanon and Whitfield-Gabrieli, 2022 ; Whitfield-Gabrieli and Nieto-Castanon, 2012 ). Data were preprocessed using a standard pipeline, described by sub-sections below, using Statistical Parametric Mapping software (SPM12, Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, University College London) ( Penny et al., 2007 ) running under MATLAB Release 2021b (The MathWorks, Inc., Natick, MA, United States) and Unix running on Macintosh computers. Our hypothesis was that autonomic systems would be involved in physiological changes induced by the 2 Hz rhythmic muscle stimulation protocol (Condition A). The CONN atlas provides a standard set of 132 brain regions of interest (ROIs) and eight identified resting state networks, to which we supplemented with five additional ROIs more specifically related to the autonomic system. Guided by drawings from a neuroanatomy atlas ( Haines, 2019 ). This included ( Fig. 1 B) a left and right hypothalamus, which was not (yet) a standardized ROI in the CONN atlas. While the CONN atlas included a generic ‘brainstem’ ROI, it did not include subdivisions therein. We sought to include a left and right dorsolateral ROI of the medulla oblongata that would encompass the solitary nucleus and tracts (aka. Nucleus tractus solitarius, NTS), due to its known muscle afferents ( Aimo et al., 2021 ). We also included a bilateral periaqueductal gray (PAG) volume due to its known involvement in pain-related modulation ( Sjolund and Eriksson, 1979 ). These volumetric ROI renderings, created using the MNI152 brain atlas as the template (see Table 1 ), are available for download as NIFTI files (see journal website).

Raw-level MRI anatomical images and rsfMRI functional runs were visually inspected across all participants using the CONN toolbox. Anatomical data were segmented into gray matter, white matter, and cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) tissue classes using SPM unified segmentation and normalization algorithm ( Hallquist et al., 2013 ; Nieto-Castanon, 2020 ) with the default IXI-549 tissue probability map template. The three functional runs were concatenated into one long timeseries file (NIFTI; *.nii format) using AFNI software plugin 3dTcat ( Cox, 1996 ). All raw data were then uploaded into the CONN toolbox for subsequent analyses.


## Denoising

Functional data were denoised using a standard denoising pipeline ( Nieto-Castanon, 2020 ) including the regression of potential confounding effects characterized by white matter timeseries (5 CompCor noise components), CSF timeseries (5 CompCor noise components), session effects and their first order derivatives (2 factors), and cubic trends (4 factors) within each functional run, followed by bandpass frequency filtering of the BOLD timeseries between 0.008 Hz and 0.09 Hz. CompCor noise components within white matter and CSF were estimated by computing the average BOLD signal as well as the largest principal components orthogonal to the BOLD average within each subject's eroded segmentation masks ( Behzadi et al., 2007 ; Chai et al., 2012 ). From the number of noise terms included in this denoising strategy, the effective degrees of freedom of the BOLD signal after denoising were estimated to range from 344.7 to 345 (average 345) across all subjects ( Nieto-Castanon, 2020 ).


## First-level analyses

Seed-based connectivity maps (SBC) and ROI-to-ROI connectivity (RRC) matrices were estimated to characterize the patterns of functional connectivity with either (i) 21 ROIs germane to the autonomic system function (see Table 1 ) or (ii) ROIs for analyses utilizing the complete CONN atlas and networks (132 ROIs plus 5 manually created ROIs, see above). This method included predefined shapes and locations derived from the human connectome project (HCP) atlas ( Nieto-Castanon, 2020 ) adjusted to each volume and were used to assess the eight predefined resting state networks (RSNs) including Default Mode, Cerebellar, Fronto-Parietal, Language, Salience, SensoriMotor, Dorsal Attention, and Visual networks. Functional connectivity strength was represented by Fisher-transformed bivariate correlation coefficients from a weighted general linear model (weighted-GLM), defined separately for each pair of seed and target areas, modeling the association between their BOLD signal timeseries. In order to compensate for possible transient magnetization effects at the beginning of each series of scans, individual scans were weighted by a step function convolved with an SPM canonical hemodynamic response function and rectified.


## Group-level analyses

Group-level (Second-level) analyses applied to each of the above first-level analyses were performed using a General Linear Model (GLM) ( Nieto-Castanon, 2020 ). For each individual voxel a separate GLM was estimated, with first-level connectivity measures at this voxel as dependent variables (one independent sample per subject and one measurement per task or experimental condition, when applicable), and groups or other subject-level identifiers as independent variables. Voxel-level hypotheses were evaluated using multivariate parametric statistics with random-effects across subjects and sample covariance estimation across multiple measurements. Inferences were performed at the level of individual clusters (groups of contiguous voxels). Cluster-level inferences were based on parametric statistics from Gaussian Random Field theory ( Nieto-Castanon, 2020 ; Worsley et al., 1996 ). Primary results were derived by thresholding using a combination of a cluster-forming p < 0.01 voxel-level threshold plus a familywise error corrected p-FWE <0.01 cluster-size threshold ( Chumbley et al., 2010 ). These FWE corrections were applied within the relevant ROI sets (i.e. the 21 a priori ROIs or the standard atlas ROIs), thus ensuring appropriate corrections for multiple comparisons were taken across each ROI family. Cluster-level inferences based on Threshold Free Cluster Enhancement analyses ( Smith and Nichols, 2009 ) aim at removing the dependency of other cluster-level inference methodologies on the choice of an a priori cluster-forming height threshold.


## Results


## Behavioral examination of effects of 2Hz non-voluntary rhythmic muscle stimulation

After administration of Condition A, receiving 2 Hz stimulation to the hand for 45 min that induced rhythmical muscle contractions, six of eight participants reported the feeling of being relaxed or sleepy, consistent with endorphin release ( Facchinetti et al., 1986 ; Veening and Barendregt, 2015 ). Notably, this effect was not reported after Condition B, the 100 Hz skin stimulation condition, by any of the eight participants. No significant differences in heart rate or blood pressure were observed (data not shown). This lack of vital sign changes was likely reflective of a floor effect since all healthy participants were placed into a relatively relaxed condition in the MRI waiting room area during the time periods prior to each of the rsfMRI scanning sessions (see Methods).


## Examination of 2 Hz non-voluntary rhythmic muscle stimulation on autonomic nervous systems

The region of interest (ROI) selection for analyses included the 132 atlas ROIs provided by the CONN software, and the eight pre-defined resting state networks therein. Five additional ROIs not in the CONN atlas were created manually ( Fig. 1 B). This included the left and right hypothalamus, left and right dorsolateral medulla, and PAG, which were regions selected based on relevant literature regarding anticipated exteroception networks (see Introduction). Thus, we sought to focus our exploration of autonomic nervous system networks through analyzing this a priori set of 21 ROIs ( Table 1 ) of predefined shape and location. For Condition A (2 Hz muscle stimulation), the ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity analysis using the spatial pairwise clustering (SPC) method revealed significant changes in interconnectivity between several regions, including the insula, plus thalamus, basal ganglia, and anterior cingulate cortex ( Fig. 2 A, Table 2 A; p < 0.01, FWE-corrected, two-sided). For Condition B (100 Hz continuous skin stimulation), the analysis revealed significant changes between the left putamen and left insula ( Fig. 2 B– Table 2 B; p < 0.01, FWE-corrected, two-sided). Moreover, as a critical control, there were significant differences between the 2 Hz muscle stimulation relative to changes occurring after the 100 Hz skin stimulation condition as the baseline ( Fig. 2 C–D; Table 2 C). This differential analysis for the 2 Hz rhythmic muscle activation condition additionally revealed significant involvement of the left and right hypothalamus ROIs, right amygdala, right hippocampus, and PAG. An ROI-to-ROI connectivity analysis of order effect (Condition A then B or vice versa) revealed no significant effects (p < 0.05 uncorrected).

## Examination of the hypothalamus through seed-to-voxel analyses

The left and right hypothalamus ROIs were separately selected as seed regions to reveal connectivity patterns between the ROIs in the CONN atlas plus the additional ROIs we created (see Methods). With the significance threshold set to voxel-wise p < 0.05, and a cluster size correction of p < 0.05, this analysis for the after (vs before) 2 Hz muscle stimulation (Condition A) revealed several brain regions showing change in interconnectivity. These changes were greater for the left hypothalamus as the seed region ( Fig. 3 , Table 3 A; 2793 mm 3 ) than for the right hypothalamus (data not illustrated, Table 3 B; 1891 mm 3 ). A Seed based connectivity analysis of order effect (Condition A then B or vice versa) revealed mild connectivity differences within and near the left hypothalamus regions (p < 0.05 uncorrected), but the effect size was 0.05, indicating that this was not a significant effect due to being underpowered. Thus, there were no significant order effects (and thus no carry-over effects) that we could detect, albeit a small sample size.

## Examination of predefined network changes

Significant differences in functional connectivity network patterns after (vs before) 2 Hz muscle stimulation (Condition A; Fig. 4 A, Table 4 A; p < 0.01, FWE-corrected, two-sided) included the Default Mode, Cerebellar, and Fronto-Parietal networks. In contrast, patterns after (vs before) 100 Hz skin stimulation (Condition B; Fig. 4 B– Table 4 B; p < 0.01, FWE-corrected, two-sided) led to changes in the Fronto-Parietal, Language, Salience, and SensoriMotor networks. However, interpretation of the significance of these global network changes went beyond the scope of the present exploratory study.

## Discussion

The health benefits of regular physical activity are well established ( Macera et al., 2003 ). Most of these physical activities involve rhythmical or intermittent activations of skeletal muscles for periods of around 30 min or longer, performed multiple times a week. While evidence supports the role of skeletal muscle–derived myokines in mediating some systemic health effects, relatively little is known about how sensory activity originating from contracting skeletal muscles (i.e., muscle interoception) has on neuroendocrine and brain regions involved in homeostasis.

In the present study, skeletal muscle sensory afferent activation was produced by non-voluntary, electrical stimulation to rhythmically contract human skeletal muscles of the hand, independent of voluntary activation and the nuisance variables associated with volitional motor tasks. The resulting neural responses were examined using resting-state functional connectivity MRI (rsfMRI) to characterize brain network activity associated with skeletal muscle interoception and its potential role in central homeostatic regulation.

The insula appears to serve as a key integrative hub that determines whether interoceptive or exteroceptive processing dominates cortical network activity: More specifically, the dorsal–anterior insula, particularly on the right side, is proposed to act as a switch for network transitions, integrating internal (autonomic and interoceptive) and external (multisensory) inputs to direct executive control ( Craig, 2005 ; Molnar-Szakacs and Uddin, 2022 ). In our rsfMRI analysis, right insula–left putamen connectivity was enhanced following rhythmic (2 Hz) muscle stimulation, while left insula–left putamen connectivity increased following 100 Hz skin stimulation. Thus, lateralized insular processing with right-sided networks appear to preferentially support interoceptive (muscle-based) information; whereas left-sided networks preferentially support exteroceptive (cutaneous-based) information.

With the exception of fatiguing contractions ( Noakes, 2012 ), the role of afferent nerve activity from skeletal muscles to correct imbalances in homeostasis and maintain self-renewal processes has received little attention. However, in response to activation of skeletal muscle afferents, hypothalamic neuroendocrine pathways that control energy balance ( Ibeas et al., 2021 ) and support skeletal muscle growth mediated by the release of bGH by the pituitary ( McCall et al., 1985 ) could be activated to modulate skeletal muscle mass and metabolism ( i.e., skeletal muscle homeostasis).

In the present study, rsfMRI revealed increased hypothalamic activation following rhythmic muscle stimulation ( Fig. 2 , Fig. 3 ), consistent with engagement of these neuroendocrine circuits. Taken together, skeletal muscle afferents probably contribute to central homeostatic regulation, linking peripheral muscle activity to neuroendocrine modulation of muscle mass and metabolism.

Although cognitive outcomes were not a primary focus of this study, notable behavioral differences were observed. Rhythmic (2 Hz) muscle stimulation induced sensations of relaxation or sleepiness in six of eight participants, an effect not reported following 100 Hz skin stimulation for the same individuals. This observation may reflect endorphin-mediated neuroendocrine activation ( Sjolund and Eriksson, 1979 ). Furthermore, rsfMRI revealed significant alterations across some of the eight major resting-state networks following 2 Hz muscle stimulation, suggesting downstream modulation of cognitive and affective states consistent with post-exercise somnolence. Parallel modulation of subcortical structures—including the hypothalamus, amygdala, hippocampus, caudate, and putamen,—supports the concept that muscle-driven afferent input can regulate both physiological and cognitive domains of homeostasis.

In summary, these findings collectively reveal that skeletal muscle can function as an interoceptive organ, transmitting rhythmic afferent signals that engage the neuroendocrine system and homeostatic brain circuits. These findings parallel those of reported for bone sensory afferent functions in interoception. In contrast, skin afferents—representing exteroceptive input—engage distinct cortical networks and do not appear to participate directly in homeostatic regulation.


## Future directions

Patients who walk during hospitalization are reported to have better outcomes ( Chen and Bonham, 2010 ; Cohen et al., 2019 ; Fisher et al., 2010 ; King et al., 2021 ); however walking requires voluntary muscle activation and often major physical assistance by staff. Since walking involves rhythmically active skeletal muscles, somatic afferent activity from skeletal muscles most likely plays a role in enhanced recovery. For example, involuntary stimulation of the hand muscles at low frequencies, similar to that used in the present study, has been shown to enhance ulcer healing ( Kaada, 1983 ), increase vasodilation in Raynaud's patients ( Kaada, 1982 ), reduce pain ( Song et al., 2022 ), and increase plasma endorphins ( Facchinetti et al., 1986 ). Some of these outcomes outlast the activity by several hours and may be mediated by activating brain regions that regulate autonomic control of skeletal muscle mass and metabolism ( i.e., muscle homeostasis) as we propose ( Fig. 5 ).

In addition, preliminary studies by our group have revealed that, although challenging logistically, this protocol can be applied to the most severely affected patients in the ICU at Ruby Memorial Hospital by our clinical collaborators with minimal extra training (Swisher and Stauber, unpublished observations). Development of such therapies and judicious patient selection may ultimately help decrease the length of stay of patients in the ICU, reduce time on ventilators or assisted ventilation, and improve the quality of life for survivors of intensive care.

Importantly, because our research design was able to reveal relatively small effect sizes in brain network changes using only 8 healthy participants, the 2x2 neuroimaging approach may be adapted for a much wider variety of rsfMRI studies (i.e., treatment versus sham/placebo within the same small group) to explore the neurophysiology of health effects resulting from non-voluntary, rhythmical muscle stimulation of small muscles of the hand (or other physical activities). Additionally, testing the neural activation and connectivity patterns using rsfMRI during the electrical stimulation, though with some technical equipment challenges, might provide additional insights regarding the neural correlates of such therapeutic interventions ( Manson et al., 2022 ). Finally, collecting blood samples for assays of various biomarkers (such as bGH) would allow objective modification of treatment protocols and correlation of CNS changes, derived using rsfMRI, with neuroendocrine function.


## Limitations

While three 6 min rsfMRI scans were collected during each pair of brain scanning sessions (to enable breaks for participants, which were generally not needed for our young, healthy participants), they were not contiguous scans. One study revealed that a longer scan length, of 13 min or more, would allow one to capture lower frequency fluctuations and attain greater intrasession reliability ( Birn et al., 2013 ). Thus, resting state correlations at these lower frequencies will have been missed, and may be worthwhile addressing in future studies. Also, the sizes of the ROI volumes were highly variable, with, for example, the dorsolateral medulla being relatively small ( Table 1 ). These differences in size and definition of ROIs will influence detectable interconnectivity patterns ( Song et al., 2016 ). Imaging at higher spatial resolution (smaller voxels and/or higher field strength) would also be beneficial for studying precise brain circuitry involved in autonomic control of human skeletal muscle mass and metabolism. Likewise, stimulation (treatment) exposure durations may need to be modified and/or applied multiple times a day to better design patient specific therapy plans.
