## Abstract

With the acceleration of global population aging, sarcopenia (SA) and cognitive impairment have become major public health concerns. Observational studies suggest associations between them, but such findings may be biased by confounding and reverse causation. Previous Mendelian randomization (MR) studies have yielded inconsistent results. Therefore, this study applied a bidirectional MR design to clarify their causal relationships. We obtained genetic instrumental variables for SA-related traits, including hand grip strength, lean mass, and usual walking pace, from the UK Biobank. Data on cognitive performance were derived from a large genome-wide association studies meta-analysis published in Nature Genetics in 2018 (n = 257,841), with replication performed in an independent cohort from the IEU Open genome-wide association studies database (n = 22,593). Bidirectional MR analyses were primarily conducted using the inverse variance weighted method, supplemented by MR Egger, weighted median, and weighted mode approaches to validate robustness. Heterogeneity and pleiotropy were evaluated by Cochran Q and MR Egger intercept tests, while leave-one-out and Steiger directionality tests assessed robustness. Inverse variance weighted analysis demonstrated significant positive causal effects of genetically predicted right hand grip strength (odds ratio [OR] = 1.112, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.032–1.198, P = .005), right arm fat-free mass (OR = 1.060, 95% CI: 1.021–1.100, P = .002), left arm fat-free mass (OR = 1.046, 95% CI: 1.008–1.085, P = .017), right leg fat-free mass (OR = 1.060, 95% CI: 1.023–1.098, P = .001), left leg fat-free mass (OR = 1.050, 95% CI: 1.014–1.087, P = .006), whole body fat-free mass (OR = 1.081, 95% CI: 1.045–1.119, P < .001), and walking pace (OR = 1.414, 95% CI: 1.172–1.705, P < .001) on cognitive performance. In the reverse MR analysis, no significant causal effects of cognitive performance on SA-related traits were observed, except for walking pace. Genetic prediction of SA-related traits may indicate that cognitive impairment is a potential pathogenic factor. Monitoring the muscle health of older adults and preventing the onset or progression of SA could potentially slow cognitive decline. Furthermore, bidirectional MR results suggest a strong causal relationship between walking pace and cognitive performance.


## 1. Introduction

With the increasing trend of global population aging, chronic diseases such as musculoskeletal disorders, cognitive dysfunction, and cardiovascular diseases have become major health threats for older adults. Moreover, the health and quality of life of the elderly remain pressing global public health concerns. Today, the prevalence of sarcopenia (SA) and cognitive impairment (CI) is rapidly increasing, severely impairing the quality of life of older adults and imposing substantial medical and economic burdens on society. [ 1 , 2 ] Many observational studies have identified a strong association between SA and CI. Specifically, older adults with SA have a higher risk of developing CI. SA-related diagnostic indices, muscle strength and walking pace, have been shown to correlate with cognitive performance. [ 3 ] The prevalence of CI among patients with SA ranges from 20.5% to 31.9%. [ 4 , 5 ] A cross-sectional survey involving 201 elderly Koreans showed that patients with SA were 6 times more likely to develop CI than non-sarcopenic patients. [ 6 ] Both SA and CI are degenerative conditions closely associated with aging, [ 7 ] sharing common risk factors such as malnutrition, reduced physical activity, neurological dysfunction, altered hormone levels, inflammatory response, and insulin resistance. [ 8 ] Given their shared pathophysiology and frequent co-occurrence, exploring the relationship between SA and CI remains a critical focus of current research.

American scholar Irwin H. Rosenberg proposed the concept of SA in 1989, defining it as an age-related loss of muscle mass and function. [ 9 ] In 2010, the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People issued the first consensus on SA, defining it as a syndrome of aging-related loss of muscle mass, a decline in muscle strength or somatic dysfunction in the elderly. [ 10 ] Later, in 2019, the Asian Working Group on Sarcopenia refined this definition, highlighting slow gait speed and reduced hand grip strength as diagnostic criteria. [ 11 ] In 2021, the Chinese expert consensus adopted the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2010 criteria, [ 12 ] suggesting 3 key indicators: reduced muscle strength (grip strength: < 28 kg in men and < 18 kg for women); decline in physical performance (e.g., 5-times sitting-up time > 12 seconds or 6-meter walking pace < 1 m/s); decreased skeletal muscle mass (DXA: < 7 kg/m 2 for men and < 5.4 kg/m 2 for women). Due to varying diagnostic thresholds and criteria, the reported prevalence of SA differs across studies, ranging from 10% to 16% globally, and reaching 17.4% among Chinese community-dwelling older adults aged 65 and above. [ 13 , 14 ] As a hallmark of geriatric syndromes, SA is associated with adverse outcomes including decreased self-care ability, falls, fractures, disability, and even death. [ 15 – 17 ] In addition, it is linked to chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease and CI, making it a valuable predictor of poor health outcomes. [ 18 ] Thus, early identification and intervention based on SA characteristics are crucial for maintaining function and improving life quality among older adults.

CI is a chronic progressive neurodegenerative condition associated with aging. It is characterized by deficits in language, memory, executive function, and reasoning, and encompasses a continuum from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to dementia, including Alzheimer disease (AD). [ 19 ] CI significantly affects individuals’ ability to perform daily activities and participate in social engagement. [ 20 ] An epidemiological survey reported that the prevalence of MCI among individuals aged 60 years and older in China was 15.5%, while the prevalence of dementia was 6.0%. [ 21 ] MCI represents a predementia stage, where cognitive decline surpasses that associated with normal aging but has not yet progressed to the severity of dementia. Without early intervention, individuals with MCI may progress to dementia, leading to irreversible cognitive decline. The number of people suffering from CI continues to rise with the aging population. According to the World Alzheimer Disease Report 2023, over 55 million people worldwide currently suffer from dementia, a figure projected to increase to 139 million by 2050, placing a heavy burden on families and the social healthcare system. [ 2 ] Currently, there is no effective treatment for dementia. [ 22 ] So identifying early risk factors and implementing timely interventions is essential to delay or prevent the onset of dementia.

SA not only leads to physical deterioration but also has a negative impact on cognitive function, while CI, in turn, contribute to the development of SA. Although numerous studies have examined the relationship between SA and CI, the specific biological pathways connecting them remain unclear. Several hypotheses have been proposed regarding how SA affects cognitive function: SA may impair the regenerative capacity of skeletal muscle stem cells and inhibit the production of myokines, which have been shown to improve cognitive function. Therefore, a lack of myokines may negatively impact brain function and contribute to CI. [ 23 ] Loss of muscle mass leads to reduced physical activity, which may result in lower levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and insulin-like growth factor 1. These neurotrophic factors are critical for maintaining synaptic plasticity, and their decline may ultimately impair cognitive performance. [ 24 ] Elevated concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, commonly observed in individuals with SA may contribute to neuroinflammation and subsequent cognitive decline. [ 25 ] Conversely, CI may also promote the progression of SA. Physical activity in individuals with CI is significantly reduced, [ 26 ] which may accelerate muscle loss and decrease muscle strength, [ 27 ] ultimately leading to SA. This may be due to oxidative stress associated with CI, which could disrupt the balance between protein synthesis and degradation, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and cell apoptosis, thereby triggering the onset of SA. [ 28 ] Furthermore, in individuals with CI, abnormalities in neurotransmitter levels, activity in the central nervous system, and insufficient brain oxygenation may reduce muscle activity, contributing to the development of SA. [ 29 ] However, the relationship between SA and CI is controversial, and some studies have not found an association between them. A logistic regression analysis conducted in Turkey found no independent correlation between dementia and SA among older adults. [ 30 ] Similarly, the results of another American study showed that there was no association between SA and CI in adults aged 60 to 69 years, but observed a significant association in those aged 70 years and older. [ 31 ] And it is still unclear whether there is a causal relationship between SA and CI, so it still needs to be further explored.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a genetic epidemiological method that uses germline genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to assess the causal impact between exposure factors and disease. [ 32 ] Since genetic variants can be randomly assigned to offspring, it can be interpreted as a genetically randomized controlled trial that overcomes the effects of confounding factors and reverses causal associations on causal inference that cannot be measured in traditional epidemiological studies. In recent years, multiple studies have applied MR to investigate the causal relationship between SA and CI. Duan et al conducted a bidirectional MR analysis and demonstrated causal associations between appendicular lean mass (ALM), walking pace, grip strength, and cognitive function, while also revealing a reverse causal effect of cognitive function on ALM and walking pace. [ 33 ] Liu et al reported a positive causal effect of ALM and walking pace on cognitive function but found no causal association between grip strength and cognitive function. [ 34 ] Sha et al further validated, through a combination of cohort study and MR analysis, that genetically predicted increases in ALM, grip strength, and walking pace were significantly associated with cognitive function, with physical activity acting as a partial mediator. [ 35 ] Despite these advances, inconsistencies remain, particularly regarding the causal link between grip strength and cognitive function. Moreover, most studies relied on a single data source and lacked validation in independent cohorts. In addition, the differential effects of various SA-related traits, such as whole-body muscle mass versus regional muscle mass, have not been adequately addressed. ALM and whole-body lean mass (WBLM) are key indicators of muscle mass and essential components of SA. [ 36 , 37 ] To provide a more comprehensive assessment, the present study employed both ALM and WBLM as indicators of muscle mass in SA. Therefore, this study employed a bidirectional 2-sample MR design to explore the causal relationship between SA-related traits and cognitive performance, which aims to provide more precise and credible evidence to support early screening and targeted interventions for cognitive decline in aging populations.


## 2. Materials and methods


## 2.1. Study design

We employed a bidirectional 2-sample MR study design to estimate the causal relationship between SA-related traits and cognitive performance (Fig. 1 ). In the 1st stage, MR analysis was performed to evaluate the causal effect of SA-related traits on cognitive performance. In the 2nd stage, a reverse MR analysis was conducted to assess whether cognitive performance exerted a causal influence on SA-related traits. During the MR analysis, IVs must satisfy 3 core assumptions, [ 38 ] which include: relevance assumption: IVs are significantly associated with the exposure; independence assumption: IVs are independent of any confounders related to the exposure or outcome; exclusion restriction assumption: IVs affect the outcome solely through the exposure, as illustrated in Figure 2 . All analyses were based on publicly available genome-wide association studies (GWAS) summary data. Therefore, no additional ethical approval or informed consent was required for this study. Furthermore, our findings were reported in accordance with the MR-STROBE criteria. [ 39 ]

## 2.2. Data sources

Data on SA-related traits in this study were obtained from the UK Biobank, a biomedical database of a large prospective cohort study that includes in-depth information on the genetic make-up and health status of more than 500,000 individuals aged between 40 and 69 years participating in a study in the United Kingdom. [ 40 ] SA is generally characterized by 3 key traits: hand grip strength, lean mass, and usual walking pace. In this study, summary statistics for SA-related traits were derived from the MRC-IEU UK Biobank GWAS, published in 2019. [ 41 ] SA is generally characterized by 3 features: hand grip strength, lean mass, and usual walking pace. The GWAS summary statistics for grip strength included 461,089 individuals for right hand grip and 461,026 individuals for left hand grip, with a total of 9,851,867 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) analyzed. The GWAS summary data for lean mass included whole body fat-free mass (N = 454,850) and ALM (left leg, N = 454,805; right leg, N = 454,835; left arm, N = 454,672; right arm, N = 454,753), all derived from European participants in the UK Biobank. The GWAS dataset for usual walking pace (GWAS ID: ukb-b-4711) comprised 459,915 individuals of European ancestry and 9,851,867 SNPs.

In the discovery cohort, GWAS summary statistics for cognitive function were obtained from a study published in Nature Genetics in 2018, [ 42 ] which included 257,841 participants and 10,066,414 SNPs. The dataset was accessed from https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/datasets/ebi-a-GCST006572/ . To validate the robustness of our findings, we further employed an independent GWAS dataset of cognitive function (ID: ieu-b-4838) in the replication cohort. This dataset was derived from individuals of European ancestry, comprising 22,593 participants and 6719,661 SNPs, and was obtained from the IEU Open GWAS database. ## 2.3. Selection of IVs

In MR studies, the independent genetic variants used as IVs must satisfy the 3 core assumptions of relevance, independence, and exclusivity. [ 38 ] We applied the same criteria to extract genetic instruments for SA-related traits and cognitive performance. First, we selected all associated SNPs with genome-wide significance ( P < 5 × 10 -8 ) from each GWAS. Next, to eliminate variants with strong linkage disequilibrium and ensure the independence of each SNP, we applied standard parameters ( r ² < 0.01, window size = 1000 kb). [ 43 ] Pairs of SNPs with a r ² value greater than the specified threshold ( r ² > 0.01) and the smallest P -value were retained. SNPs with a minor allele frequency <0.010 were excluded. Finally, ambiguous SNPs with inconsistent alleles (e.g., A/G vs A/C) and palindromic SNPs (e.g., A/T or G/C) were excluded from the process to ensure strand alignment the exposure and outcome datasets. Subsequently, the F -statistic was utilized to assess whether there was a weak IV bias ( F -statistic < 10 was used to define a weak IV), and the IVs of F -statistic < 10 were removed from our analysis. ## 2.4. Statistical analysis


## 2.4.1. MR analysis

We employed 4 different MR methods to investigate the causal relationship between SA-related traits and cognitive performance: inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR Egger regression, weighted median (WM), and weighted mode. [ 44 ] The IVW method served as the primary analytical approach, [ 43 ] with MR Egger, WM, and other additional methods were used as complementary analyses to assess the robustness of the results. [ 45 ] A causal effect was considered statistically significant if the P -value was <.05, indicating a potentially increased risk of the outcome attributable to the exposure. Odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to assess the potential causal relationship between SA-related traits and cognitive performance. In the replication cohort, we conducted the same MR analyses to validate the robustness of the results from the discovery cohort, using identical analytical strategies, with IVW as the primary method and MR Egger, WM, and other complementary approaches as sensitivity analyses.


## 2.4.2. Sensitivity analysis

We performed a series of sensitivity analyses to assess the reliability of the results. First, Cochran Q test was applied to assess heterogeneity among the IVs. [ 45 ] Second, the MR Egger intercept test was used to detect potential horizontal pleiotropy, with a P -value < .05 indicating the presence of horizontal pleiotropy. [ 46 ] Third, a leave-one-out analysis was performed to evaluate the influence of individual SNPs on the overall causal estimates, thereby preventing any single IV from disproportionately driving the results. [ 47 ] Furthermore, to rigorously assess the directionality of the causal relationships, we applied the Steiger directionality test, which compares the variance explained by the exposure and outcome to confirm the validity of the inferred causal direction. [ 48 ]


## 2.4.3. Statistical software

All statistical analyses in this study were conducted using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.6.8) in R software (version 4.4.0, www.r-project.org/ ). [ 49 ] A two-sided P -value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

## 3.2. The reverse MR

To explore the possibility of reverse causation, we applied a stringent threshold of P < 5 × 10 −8 and carefully identified SNPs strongly and independently associated with cognitive performance. Detailed information on the IVs is provided in Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q570 . Using the IVW method, we found a significant causal relationship between cognitive performance and walking pace (OR = 1.067, 95% CI: 1.046–1.088; P < .001). The WM analysis was consistent, while the weighted mode showed marginal significance (Fig. 9 ). However, the MR Egger method did not reveal any discernible causal effect (Fig. 10 ). To ensure the robustness of these findings, we conducted MR Egger pleiotropy tests on the included SNPs, which revealed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy ( P > .05). Cochran Q statistic indicated significant heterogeneity among the combined SNP effects ( P < .001) (Table 3 ). Therefore, the random-effects IVW model was employed for the reverse MR analysis. Additionally, we performed a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis to evaluate the impact of each individual SNP on the overall causal relationship. Remarkably, when individual SNPs were systematically excluded and the MR analysis was rerun, no substantial deviation was observed in the causal estimates (Fig. 11 ). The Steiger directionality test indicated that the causal direction from cognitive performance to walking pace was supported, whereas no significant directional effects were observed for other SA-related traits (Table 3 ).

## 4. Discussion

In this study, we conducted a bidirectional 2-sample MR analysis using GWAS data to investigate the causal relationships between SA-related traits and cognitive performance. Forward MR analyses revealed that right hand grip strength, muscle mass, and walking pace were causally associated with cognitive function, suggesting that genetically predicted SA-related traits may be potential contributors to CI. In the replication cohort, left leg and whole-body muscle mass remained positively associated with cognitive function, while other traits did not reach statistical significance, although the direction of effect was consistent with the discovery cohort. This indicates that the primary causal effects were partially validated in an independent cohort, reinforcing the robustness of the findings. Multiple sensitivity analyses, including WM, weighted mode, MR Egger, and leave-one-out analyses, supported the robustness of the positive causal relationships. Moreover, Steiger directionality tests further confirmed the plausibility of the causal directions. Reverse MR analyses showed a significant causal effect of cognitive performance on walking pace but did not detect significant effects on grip strength or muscle mass. Steiger directionality tests supported the reverse causal direction at the variance level; however, considering the overall MR results, there may be a bidirectional causal relationship between walking pace and cognitive function, whereas the causal effects of grip strength and muscle mass on cognitive function appear predominantly unidirectional. Taken together, our study provides genetic evidence supporting a causal role of SA-related traits in cognitive function and suggests a potential bidirectional causal relationship between walking pace and cognition. These findings offer a theoretical basis for exploring SA prevention and intervention strategies aimed at preserving cognitive function, and provide guidance for future mechanistic and clinical studies.

Previous studies have shown that SA is highly associated with CI. SA may be an independent risk factor and early predictor of CI. [ 50 , 51 ] Data from a study of 6 countries, including China, Ghana, India, Mexico, South Africa, and Russia, revealed a significant positive correlation between SA and MCI. [ 52 ] Furthermore, SA not only directly affects cognitive function but may also influence it through mediating factors. For example, Chen Xiaoyu explored the association between SA, depression, and MCI in community-dwelling older adults, and found that depression can impair cognitive function and served as a mediator between grip strength and cognitive function. [ 53 ] Similarly, Yao et al reported that physical activity played a mediating role between SA and cognitive performance in a survey of 420 elderly individuals residing in care facilities. [ 54 ] Currently, both SA and CI are increasingly prevalent in the elderly population, and they show a strong correlation in pathogenesis. However, the relationship between SA and cognitive performance is still controversial. While some studies have reported strong associations, others found no significant correlation. For instance, 1 study conducted among older adults in Taiwan and mainland China found no significant correlation between SA and cognitive dysfunction. [ 55 ] Such inconsistencies may be partly attributable to confounding factors, such as differences in dietary habits. For example, adherence to the Mediterranean diet has been shown to reduce the risk of cognitive decline and attenuate muscle loss. [ 56 ] In addition, there is no clear evidence regarding the causal relationship between SA and cognitive performance. Therefore, we employed MR analysis to assess the potential causal relationship between SA-related traits (grip strength, muscle mass, and walking pace) and cognitive performance.

Our findings are largely consistent with Sha et al, who reported causal associations of genetically predicted ALM, handgrip strength, and gait speed with cognitive function. Notably, our study further suggests a potential bidirectional causal relationship between gait speed and cognitive function. [ 35 ] Duan et al observed causal effects of ALM, gait speed, and handgrip strength on cognition, as well as reverse causal effects of cognition on ALM and gait speed. [ 33 ] Similarly, we detected a reverse causal effect of cognition on gait speed, but not on muscle mass. This discrepancy may reflect our more refined phenotypic assessment, which included regional ALM combined with WBLM, allowing for more precise capture of genetic effects and revealing heterogeneity across different muscle mass indicators. In addition, Liu et al reported positive causal effects of ALM and gait speed on cognition, but found no causal association for handgrip strength. [ 34 ] In contrast, our analysis identified a positive effect of right hand grip strength on cognition, likely due to differences in GWAS sources, phenotypic definitions, and IV selection. The causal relationship between handgrip strength and cognition thus remains inconsistent and warrants further validation in larger and multi-ethnic populations. Overall, our study partially confirms the causal effects of muscle mass and walking pace, and strengthens these conclusions through multiple sensitivity analyses. Importantly, we provide additional genetic evidence for the causal effect of handgrip strength on cognition and for the bidirectional relationship between gait speed and cognition, offering more comprehensive insights for SA prevention and cognitive function protection.

Hand grip strength is a widely used, objective measure of upper body strength and overall physical function. [ 57 ] Research has shown that hand grip strength is closely related not only to physical health but also to cognitive function. [ 58 ] A 20-year follow-up study found a strong association between hand grip strength and cognitive decline, suggesting that lower grip strength may serve as a predictor of cognitive deterioration. [ 59 ] Similarly, a prospective study involving 1096 elderly individuals over a 10-year period found that low baseline hand grip strength could predict cognitive decline over the subsequent decade. [ 60 ] However, the relationship between hand grip strength and cognitive function remains controversial. Kobayashi-Cuya et al reported that the association between hand grip strength and cognitive function was not clear. [ 61 ] Another randomized controlled trial observed that while baseline grip strength in women was associated with overall cognitive function, it was not predictive of changes in cognition at follow-up. [ 62 ] These inconsistencies may be due to variations in study design, sample characteristics, or follow-up duration. To clarify the relationship, we employed a bidirectional 2-sample MR analysis and found that low hand grip strength might be a causal factor for CI. However, no evidence supported a reverse causal effect of cognitive performance on grip strength. These findings suggest, that in clinical practice, early monitoring and targeted improvement of grip strength may be a valuable strategy for delaying cognitive decline in older adults.

Muscle mass, as a key indicator of muscle quality, has been shown to influence cognitive status in patients with dementia. [ 63 ] Kim et al reported a significant correlation between muscle mass and cognitive function. [ 64 ] Specifically, better skeletal muscle mass was associated with a lower likelihood of developing AD among the elderly, suggesting that muscle mass may play a protective role in cognitive health. A study by Chinese scholars Luo Jinhua et al also confirmed the correlation between muscle mass and cognitive function in the elderly, further supporting the positive impact of muscle health on cognitive function. [ 65 ] However, not all studies have reported consistent findings. A study conducted among elderly men in Australia found that muscle mass was associated with psychomotor function in cognitive subdomains, but not with other cognitive domains such as attention, working memory, or visual learning with attention. [ 66 ] Another community-based study indicated that low muscle mass did not increase the risk of dementia in the elderly. [ 67 ] Such differences may be attributed to variations in study design, measurement methods, and population characteristics. Therefore, this study utilized a bidirectional 2-sample MR analysis to further examine the causal relationship between muscle mass and cognitive performance. The results revealed that decreased muscle mass could potentially lead to cognitive dysfunction, while cognitive performance did not exhibit a causal effect on muscle mass. These results provide new evidence supporting the causal association between muscle mass and cognitive performance and underscore the potential value of muscle mass in the early identification and prevention of cognitive decline.

In previous studies, walking pace has often been considered an overt indicator of physical function, while cognitive function has been more closely linked to its neurobiological basis. Several studies have indicated that walking pace is a crucial assessment marker in individuals with MCI and a strong predictor of the progression from MCI to dementia. [ 68 – 70 ] Additionally, our study found a significant bidirectional causal relationship between walking pace and cognitive performance. This result further confirms that walking pace can serve as an early warning indicator of cognitive decline. Therefore, monitoring walking pace is of great significance for the early identification of cognitive decline. Our findings are consistent with the perspective proposed by Verghese et al, who, in 2013, introduced the concept of motoric-cognitive risk syndrome – a clinical condition characterized by slow gait and CI. [ 70 ] This syndrome highlights the strong bidirectional relationship between physical mobility and cognition and further underscores the importance of assessing walking pace to identify individuals at risk of progressing to dementia.

From a biological perspective, we hypothesize that this causal relationship may be closely related to neuroplasticity processes in the brain. As one of the most common daily activities, walking has a beneficial stimulating and regulating effect on the nervous system, particularly in brain regions associated with cognitive function, such as the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus. Studies have shown that the activity in the left prefrontal cortex increases significantly during fast walking compared to moderate or slow walking. [ 71 ] This enhanced activity is believed to be linked to increased gray matter volume in the prefrontal cortex with faster walking pace, which in turn improves cerebral blood flow, oxygen supply, and nutrient delivery, while simultaneously reducing inflammation and oxidative stress. These physiological changes contribute to a favorable physiological environment that supports neuronal repair and functional recovery, and enhanced cognitive functions such as memory and attention. [ 72 ] In addition, regular walking training can promote neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity in the hippocampus, thereby improving the hippocampus’s ability to regenerate and delaying cognitive decline. [ 73 , 74 ] This effect is closely related to the elevation of BDNF, which plays a crucial role in cognitive function by enhancing neuronal survival and promoting synaptic remodeling. [ 75 ]

Secondly, walking may indirectly promote cognitive function by improving systemic health indicators such as cardiovascular function. When atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease develops, it often progresses along the carotid artery, leading to ischemic brain injury, which in turn causes vascular dementia, CI, and brain atrophy. [ 76 ] Regular walking, as a moderate aerobic exercise, can significantly enhance cardiopulmonary function and improve cardiovascular health, thereby increasing the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood. This enhanced oxygenation delivers more oxygen and nutrients to brain tissue and is associated with increased prefrontal cortex volume in older adults. It may also help delay atrophy in brain regions such as the hippocampal gyrus, thereby improving memory and enhancing executive functions. [ 70 ] Additionally, insulin plays an important neuroprotective role, promoting neuronal metabolism, synaptic plasticity, and the maintenance of memory function. [ 77 ] Studies have shown that systemic insulin resistance may lead to insufficient insulin action in the brain, resulting in the phosphorylation of tau protein and the development of AD-related pathological changes. [ 78 ] Exercise, such as walking, can significantly enhance insulin sensitivity and improve systemic glucose metabolism, thereby playing an important protective role in cognitive function. [ 72 ]

Finally, walking pace not only reflects an individual’s physical health status but may also be influenced by lifestyle factors. [ 79 ] Moreover, lifestyle factors such as physical activity and social engagement play important moderating roles in the relationship between walking pace and cognitive performance. Physical activity has long been recognized as a protective factor for cognitive function in healthy older adults. Studies suggest that engaging in moderate to high-intensity physical activity is particularly beneficial for individuals at high risk for dementia. [ 80 ] Such activity has been shown to enhance brain volume, improve synaptic plasticity, and thereby support cognitive function. [ 81 ] Additionally, participation in physical activity can promote greater social interaction and strengthen social networks, which further contribute to cognitive protection by improving psychological well-being and emotional stability. [ 82 ] Therefore, walking, as a multifaceted lifestyle intervention, provides multi-level protection for cognitive health. However, many middle-aged and older adults lack awareness of the benefits of structured walking exercises. Consequently, healthcare professionals should not only focus on strengthening health education about walking exercise for this group but also raise awareness of the value of walking pace as an indicator of brain health. Regular walking exercise should be actively encouraged, with an emphasis on walking pace. Moreover, it is essential to tailor exercise programs to the individual needs of middle-aged and older adults to ensure safety and adherence during the activity, thus improving walking pace and delaying cognitive decline.

This study has several strengths. First, we utilized data on SA-related traits and cognitive performance from 2 independent GWAS datasets, ensuring sufficient sample size and statistical power to reduce bias in causal inference. Second, a series of comprehensive sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness and reliability of the results. Moreover, the data on SA-related traits were more comprehensive and multidimensional, incorporating grip strength, muscle mass, and walking pace. This enabled a more thorough exploration of the relationship between SA and cognitive performance.

However, this study has certain limitations. First, our analysis mainly relies on GWAS data from European populations, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups. Given the substantial genetic heterogeneity across populations, the observed associations may not be directly transferable to non-European cohorts. However, similar relationships between gait speed and cognitive function have been observed across different ethnic groups. [ 70 , 80 ] Future studies should aim to validate these findings in more diverse populations to ensure broader applicability and external validity. Second, the use of summary-level GWAS data limited our ability to perform stratified analyses based on individual-level clinical characteristics. This makes it difficult to uncover subgroup-specific causal relationships, such as those that may vary by age, sex, or genetic background. Future studies should consider stratified MR designs to explore heterogeneity across subpopulations and identify unique risk factors or protective mechanisms. Such efforts could facilitate the development of personalized risk prediction models and targeted intervention strategies. Finally, although MR minimizes confounding and reverse causation, the underlying biological mechanisms remain poorly understood. Future research could incorporate brain imaging, metabolomics, and single-cell sequencing to elucidate how SA affects cognitive performance through pathways such as neuroplasticity, inflammatory pathways, and metabolic regulation, which will would provide deeper mechanistic insights and serve as a basis for optimizing intervention strategies aimed at preventing or delaying cognitive decline.


## 5. Conclusion

Our study found that hand grip strength, muscle mass, and walking pace are positively associated with cognitive performance, suggesting that these traits may be causal factors. These findings underscore the importance of monitoring muscle health in the elderly and implementing strategies to prevent the onset or progression of SA as a potential means to delay cognitive decline. Furthermore, compared to hand grip strength and muscle mass, walking pace, as an external indicator of physical function, may be more suitable for studying the causal relationship between SA-related traits and cognitive performance. This is supported by our finding of a significant bidirectional causal relationship between walking pace and cognitive performance, suggesting that improving walking pace could reduce the risk of cognitive decline, particularly in older populations. Therefore, future clinical research should place greater emphasis on walking pace in the elderly and encourage appropriate walking exercises to maintain physical function and preserve cognitive health. However, the causal effects of cognitive performance on hand grip strength and muscle mass require further investigation to clarify.
