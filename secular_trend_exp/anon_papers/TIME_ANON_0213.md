## Abstract

Viruses maximize the functional potential of their small genomes by utilizing structured RNA sequences to perform functions beyond serving as messenger RNAs (mRNAs) coding for viral proteins. One of the first discovered retroviruses, the Rous sarcoma virus (RSV), uses an RNA sequence, called the RSV frameshifting stimulatory element, to regulate the ratio of translation of two large viral proteins. Through X-ray crystallography and cryogenic sample electron microscopy (cryoEM), we have determined multiple structures of the RSV frameshifting element, which differ at a multihelical junction critical for frameshifting. Our analyses suggest that the RSV frameshifting element adopts multiple conformations to promote the optimal amount of frameshifting. This work furthers our understanding of these highly conserved viral sequences and future efforts to inhibit virus replication by disrupting frameshifting.

Significance Viruses maximize the functional potential of their small genomes by utilizing structured RNA sequences to perform functions beyond serving as messenger RNAs (mRNAs) coding for viral proteins. One of the first discovered retroviruses, the Rous sarcoma virus (RSV), uses an RNA sequence, called the RSV frameshifting stimulatory element, to regulate the ratio of translation of two large viral proteins. Through X-ray crystallography and cryogenic sample electron microscopy (cryoEM), we have determined multiple structures of the RSV frameshifting element, which differ at a multihelical junction critical for frameshifting. Our analyses suggest that the RSV frameshifting element adopts multiple conformations to promote the optimal amount of frameshifting. This work furthers our understanding of these highly conserved viral sequences and future efforts to inhibit virus replication by disrupting frameshifting.

In the 1980s, newly sequenced retroviral genomes revealed the major viral polyproteins Gag and GagPol to be out of frame ( 1 ). Further studies demonstrated that these two proteins are translated from one RNA through a form of translational recoding that was termed “programmed –1 frameshifting” ( 2 ). First confirmed in Rous sarcoma virus (RSV) ( 2 , 3 ) and later in a wide variety of other viruses (reviewed in ref. 4 ), including coronaviruses ( 5 ), these recoding events are each stimulated site specifically by an RNA element composed of a slippery sequence (a heptanucleotide sequence containing two homopolymeric triplet codons) followed by a frameshifting stimulatory RNA structural element. The latter is highly variable between different viruses ( 4 ), being, for instance, a simple stem-loop in the case of HIV ( 6 ), and a canonical (H-type) pseudoknot ( 7 ) in the case of SARS-CoV-2 ( 8 ). Regardless of the specific architecture of each frameshifting stimulatory element, the translating ribosome pauses at the slippery sequence, shifts to a new reading frame at some frequency, and subsequently continues translation in either the original or shifted frame, thus producing either of the two encoded proteins. Frameshifting pseudoknots are ubiquitous in RNA viruses and thus have attracted interest as antiviral drug targets ( 9 – 11 ).

Structural studies of ribosomes engaged with different frameshifting stimulatory elements ( 12 – 14 ) have failed to reveal a congruent set of functionally important interactions between the ribosome (or associated translation factors) and the mRNA structure at the frameshifting site (reviewed in ref. 11 ). Rather, and consistent with the structural diversity of frameshifting stimulatory RNA elements, it appears that multiple mechanisms may have convergently evolved to achieve the same functional effect. Biophysical, and in particular, force-spectroscopy studies ( 15 , 16 ) suggest that rather than the thermodynamic or mechanical stability of the frameshifting element, its conformational plasticity (i.e., the ability to adopt multiple conformations) gives rise to efficient frameshifting. It has been proposed for the case of the West Nile virus that the efficiency of frameshifting is linked to the frequency at which the RNA element changes conformation while under tension as the ribosome attempts to translocate through it ( 17 ). If this principle of conformational entropy applies broadly, it might be the case that, even at thermodynamic equilibrium, frameshifting elements are able to sample multiple, near isoenergetic (i.e., degenerate) but structurally distinct conformations.

The ~120 nt RSV frameshifting stimulatory element, which frameshifts ~5 to 10% of translation events, was predicted to fold as four stems separated by large loops ( 2 ) brought together by a pseudoknot ( 3 ). At the time of its discovery, pseudoknots were an RNA structural motif that had just been described, and for which 3-dimensional (3D) structures were yet to be determined ( 7 ). While interactions between the loops of the RSV element have been postulated speculatively ( 18 ), the 3D structure of the element has remained unknown. Moreover, it also remains unclear whether the RSV frameshifting element can adopt multiple 3D structures at thermal equilibrium. While conformational variability contributes to frameshifting ( 15 ) and RNA function more broadly ( 19 ), deconvoluting the states that compose such heterogeneity remains challenging. In order to provide a structural framework to understand the molecular mechanism of action of one of the first described frameshifting stimulatory element, we have now determined X-ray crystallographic and cryoEM structures of the RSV element. Our structures reveal the most complex frameshifting stimulatory RNA structure determined thus far, one that can adopt at least three distinctly different conformations. Structure-based mutagenesis and translation assays demonstrate that a key conserved purine is the lynchpin controlling the partition of the structural ensemble, as well as the frameshifting efficiency. Thus, our analyses indicate how RSV RNA dynamics contribute to structural heterogeneity and translation recoding.


## Results


## The RSV Frameshifting Element Is a Double Pseudoknot.

The crystal structure of the RSV frameshifting element was determined de novo through the single-wavelength anomalous dispersion method using X-ray diffraction data collected from an iridium hexammine-soaked crystal ( SI Appendix , Fig. S1 A ) and refined at 2.7 Å resolution. The experimental electron density maps were of high quality and allowed unambiguous tracing of the RNA chain ( Materials and Methods and SI Appendix , Fig. S1 B and Table S1 ). Our crystal form contains two 111-nt RNA molecules in the crystallographic asymmetric unit. The two nonequivalent copies adopt globally similar structures (RMSD = 3.9 Å, all nonhydrogen atoms) and pack in the crystals as dimers of approximate dimensions 35 × 60 × 145 Å 3 ( Fig. 1 A ) and approximate C2 symmetry.

Each of the two independent RNA copies (hereafter chains A and B) in the crystal structure is composed of five A-form duplexes whose connectivity is that of two sequential, interdigitated pseudoknots. The five helices (paired elements P1, P2, P3, and pseudoknot helices PK1, PK2) fold into a structure that resembles a butterfly, with the coaxially stacked P1 and P3 comprising one “wing,” P2 and PK2 the other, and the three base-pair pseudoknot helix PK1 the “thorax.” Of the pseudoknots, only PK2 was predicted, being the pseudoknot postulated in the literature ( 5 ). P2 and PK2 stack coaxially through the pyrimidine–pyrimidine wobble pair U2517•U2494 (RSV Prague C strain reference genome numbering is used throughout). P1 and P3 stack coaxially in chain B but not in chain A ( Fig. 1 B and D ). In the crystallographic asymmetric unit, PK1 from one chain packs against P1 from the second chain ( Fig. 1 A ), juxtaposing their 5′ and 3′ ends. In the double pseudoknot fold present in both chains, PK2 and P1 are separated by the intervening PK1 and a partially resolved, unpaired linker (residues 2579 to 2590, Fig. 1 B and D ). In addition, the distal loops L2 and L3, which were engineered to promote crystallization, are partially disordered in the two RNA molecules.


## CryoEM Analysis Reveals Multiple Structural States.

Our crystallographic analysis revealed that the RSV frameshifting stimulatory element can exist in at least two distinctly different conformations. To investigate whether other conformations are accessible to the RNA, we performed single-particle cryoEM reconstruction with RNA samples identical to those used for crystallography ( Materials and Methods ). Electron micrographs contained monomeric and dimeric particles in a ~5:1 ratio, similar to what was observed in size-exclusion chromatography of the samples prior to application on grids ( SI Appendix , Fig. S2 A and B and Table S2 ). The smaller (~37 kDa) monomeric particles did not produce high-quality reconstructions; however, even with limited resolution, it was apparent that these particles overall resemble the structures of the single chains determined crystallographically. On the other hand, the C2-symmetric dimer particles produced high-quality 3.2 to 3.3 Å resolution Coulomb potential maps ( Fig. 2 and SI Appendix , Fig. S2 C – E ).

The atomic structures built and refined with the cryoEM maps comprise the five duplexes present in the crystal structures, as well as a sixth paired element along the dimer symmetry axis (hereafter, PD) that is absent in the crystal structures ( Fig. 2 B – D ). This region of the cryoEM map was well resolved ( Fig. 2 E ), allowing PD to be confidently built ( SI Appendix , Fig. S2 F ). This dimer interface helix has three consecutive Watson–Crick base pairs (A2578•U2589 sym , C2579•G2588 sym , C2580•G2587 sym ), the noncanonical base pair C2582•A2586 sym ( Fig. 2 F ), as well as the base triple U2583•G2584 sym •A2585 sym ( Fig. 2 G ). This arrangement is duplicated symmetrically, and results in an overwound, narrow helix that fits between the other helical stacks ( Fig. 2 D ). Thus, the cryoEM analysis shows that the RSV frameshift stimulatory element in solution has the same overall secondary structure as in our crystals, with an additional duplex PD at the dimer interface.

Further classification of single particles through 3D variability analysis revealed displacements normal to the major molecular axis ( Fig. 3 A – C ). In addition to the major “straight” species, a ~4 Å resolution “bent” reconstruction in which PD is more poorly resolved was obtained ( Fig. 3 A and B ). Variability analysis suggests the presence of multiple conformations that differ in the degree of bowing out of PD away from the major particle axis, with displacements of P2 in the opposite direction ( Fig. 3 C ). Additional displacements among final reconstructions include those of P1/P3 normal to the major particle axis, as well as twisting of P2. While the detailed atomistic analysis of the continuum of bent conformations will have to await the availability of larger datasets, this analysis indicates that the RSV frameshifting element undergoes conformational switching, with the most common species likely represented by our current crystallographic and cryoEM-derived atomic structures.

## A Conformational Switch at the P1–PK1–P3 Junction.

Among our three structures, the PK1–PK2–P2 three-helix junction is largely unchanged (RMSD = 1.9 Å between chains A and B, 2.1 Å between chain A and cryoEM, 0.6 Å between chain B and cryoEM, pairwise comparisons of all nonhydrogen atoms; SI Appendix , Fig. S2 G ). This suggests that the junction is relatively rigid under these conditions. In contrast, the three structures vary considerably in P1 and P3 ( Fig. 4 A – H ) and especially at the toggle residue A2546, where the cryoEM structure resembles chain B more than chain A ( Fig. 4 C , D , F , and G ). In both chain B and the cryoEM structure, P1 bends by ~40° (angle between helical axes) at a pyrimidine-rich bulge (U2498–C2500) containing the pyrimidine–pyrimidine Watson–Crick pair U2498•C2568 ( Fig. 4 B ). In this fold, P1 pairing resumes between U2501–U2505 and A2563–A2567, and in both structures, U2505•A2563 stacks on C2547•G2562 of P3. The cryoEM structure contains the pyrimidine–pyrimidine Watson–Crick pair C2504•U2564 ( Fig. 4 C ), whereas these two residues are flipped out in chain B ( Fig. 4 D ), leading to an ~5 Å difference in the location of P1. In chain B, C2500 forms a structured bulge, whereas density for this residue is not clearly defined in the cryoEM maps. In both, however, A2564 stacks on PK1 and points toward U2594 in PK2 ( Fig. 4 F and G ), forming a hydrogen bond between N3 of A2564 and the pro - S P nonbridging phosphate oxygen (NBPO) of U2494 in chain B ( Fig. 4 F ). In contrast to these two structures, an alternative pairing register in P1 of chain A results in noncanonical wobble pair U2501•U2565 forming, and residues U2502–U2505 lacking clear density features. In this state, A2546 flips away from PK2 toward the WC face of A2563 ( Fig. 4 E and H ). As a result, in chain A, A2546 facilitates P3 stacking on PK1, and P1 rotates away from P3. Overall, our structures show that the core of the RSV frameshifting stimulatory element can adopt multiple discrete conformations and that A2546 likely plays a central role in organizing these.

## Functional Consequences of RSV Pseudoknot Structural Switching.

To investigate how structural features and conformational differences present between our three structures contribute to frameshifting, we performed structure-guided mutagenesis and examined translation of the variant RNAs in a dual luciferase frameshifting assay in vitro using a 600-nt portion of the RSV genome ( 8 , 21 ) ( SI Appendix , Table S3 ). Deleting three or six nucleotides (Δ2587-9 and Δ2584-9, respectively) from PD did not affect frameshifting, suggesting that dimerization, as present in the cryoEM structure, is not needed for frameshifting ( SI Appendix , Table S3 and Fig. 5 A ). In comparison, deleting nts 2498 to 2500 from the pyrimidine-rich bulge in P1 reduced frameshifting by ~25-fold ( SI Appendix , Table S3 ). Disrupting PK1 pairing by point or multiple mutations resulted in at most ~twofold reduced frameshifting relative to WT, suggesting that PK1 plays a supporting role to A2546 in organizing the core. Mutations to P1 at the pyrimidine-rich bulge had greater effects on frameshifting than mutations to P1 in U2501–U2505 ( Fig. 5 A and SI Appendix , Table S3 ). Interestingly, C2568A, which is expected to stabilize the U2498•C2568 pair by converting it into a conventional Watson–Crick U•A pair, enhanced frameshifting by more than 1.5-fold ( P = 0.01, one-tailed t test, n = 3), as did the C2502U mutation, which is present in other RSV strains ( 18 ). Mutation of the conformational toggle residue A2546 to a pyrimidine reduced frameshifting ~25-fold, and A2546G enhanced frameshifting by ~1.5-fold ( P = 0.008, one-tailed t test, n = 3; Fig. 5 B ), presumably by enabling a hydrogen bond interaction with the pro - S P NBPO of U2594 ( Fig. 3 D ). Likewise, disrupting the U2517•U2494 homopyrimidine pair by mutating U2517 to C reduced frameshifting to a greater extent than mutating the residue to A ( Fig. 5 C ), and U2494 mutation to either C or A reduced frameshifting ~threefold to fourfold ( Fig. 5 C ). This analysis, in which frameshifting efficiency could be tuned by a >50-fold range, provides strong evidence for the critical functional role of A2546–PK2 interactions and also shows that the pyrimidine-rich bulge is particularly sensitive to sequence alterations. Thus, prominent features revealed by our comparative structural analysis have key roles in the biological function of the RSV frameshifting stimulatory RNA element.

## A2546 Is Key to Organizing the RSV Double Pseudoknot in Solution.

To further investigate the role of A2546 as a linchpin in the structure and conformational switching of the pseudoknotted RSV frameshifting element in solution, in-line probing ( Fig. 6 A and SI Appendix , Fig. S3 ) and dimethyl sulfate (DMS) probing ( Fig. 6 B and SI Appendix , Fig. S4 ) of the wild-type RNA as well as each A2546 point mutant were performed. In-line probing reports on spontaneous strand cleavage (via nucleophilic “in-line” attack by the ribose 2′-OH) as a readout for RNA backbone flexibility, which is reduced by base pairing and folding of the molecule ( 22 ). The overall cleavage patterns of each RNA variant were similar—regions of protection in predicted paired elements and greater cleavage at predicted loops—and patterns were similar in low- and high-salt buffers ( Fig. 6 A ). Interestingly, enhanced cleavage at positions 2517-2519 was observed for both the A2546C and A2546U variants, but neither for the WT nor the A2546G variant ( Fig. 6 A ). This is consistent with only a purine being able to make stabilizing interactions between 2546 and U2594. Increased cleavage was also observed at position 2546 for A2546C and A2546U, indicating increased flexibility at this position when a pyrimidine is present ( Fig. 6 A ). Taken together, these results further support a key role for A2546 in making interactions that stabilize the RSV double pseudoknot fold.

Widely used to probe RNA secondary structure in vitro and in cells, DMS probing reports on the relative accessibility of A and C residues through methylation of their Watson–Crick faces ( 23 , 24 ). To confirm DMS reactivities among each A2546 RSV RNA variant, time-course reactions were performed and visualized by denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis ( Fig. 6 B and SI Appendix , Fig. S4 ). While band compression obscured reactivities for A2544–C2548—including the residue of interest A2546—many reactive residues were observed ( Fig. 6 B – D ). For example, L2 residue A2534 is highly reactive to DMS for all four A2546 RNA variants ( Fig. 6 C ). While DMS-reactive residues largely behaved similarly among RNA variants, A2563 displayed greater DMS reactivity for A2546C than A2546 WT or A2546G ( Fig. 6 D ). In contrast, A2546U was less reactive ( Fig. 6 D ), possibly due to the mutation facilitating pairing between A2546U and A2563. Taken together with in-line probing, the similar probing profiles among A2546 variants are consistent with globally similar RNA folds. Detecting multiple RNA conformations by chemical probing ( 25 – 27 ) or other methods ( 19 , 28 ) for the RSV RNA may be possible in the future.


## Discussion

Forty years after its discovery, our crystallographic and cryoEM analyses reveal that rather than a simple H-type pseudoknot, the RSV frameshifting stimulatory element is composed of five duplexes that are arranged as two pairs of coaxially stacked helices that pack side by side, with a fifth helix at the interface ( Fig. 1 ). This arrangement, which has minimal tertiary interactions spanning the intramolecular interface, is stabilized by the connectivity of the RNA mainchain. The helical elements are linked as a double, interdigitated pseudoknot. Pseudoknots can give rise to specific 3D structures that are constrained by the length and composition of the nominally single-stranded elements; pseudoknotting can precisely stabilize folds of even relatively small RNAs such that they can perform exacting molecular recognition [e.g., the ~30 nt PreQ1 riboswitch ( 29 )] or efficient general acid–base catalysis [e.g., the ~60 nt hepatitis delta virus ribozyme ( 30 )]. This is, thus far, one of the most complex RNA structures identified in viral frameshifting stimulatory elements ( Fig. 7 A ), which have ranged in complexity from simple stem-loops (HIV) ( 6 ) ( Fig. 7 B ) and minimal H-type pseudoknots ( Fig. 7 C and D ) ( 31 , 32 ) to the more elaborate severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pseudoknot composed of a three coaxially (albeit flexibly) arranged duplex elements ( Fig. 7 E ).

Our structural and biochemical analysis also indicates that the RSV frameshifting stimulatory element can adopt multiple distinct conformations. Our three snapshots show how a dynamic region in an RNA motif can interact with a relatively rigid region to impart functional heterogeneity. The interaction at the bend in the backbone between pseudoknots PK1 and PK2 allows the P1–P3 wing to communicate with the PK1–PK2–P2 wing through A2546. Strengthening the interaction through the A2546G mutation enhances frameshifting, and weakening the interaction through the A2546C, A2546U, or U2517C mutations eliminates frameshifting. As the slippery site at nts 2476-82 immediately precedes the stop codon at nts 2483-5 ( Fig. 5 A ), translation termination at the stop codon in-frame 0, which occurs during the majority of translation events, could partially unwind P1, which would first encounter the translating ribosome. The PK1–PK2–P2 junction could remain folded during 0-frame translation, possibly contributing to downstream stability ( 35 ) and avoiding nonsense-mediated decay by concealing single-stranded motifs ( 36 ). Unlike other frameshifting elements in which the pseudoknot is adjacent to P1 to interact with the translating ribosome ( 12 ), the RSV motif separates PK2 from the more dynamic P1/P3. In this model, interactions between the two wings via A2546 determine the extent of frameshifting that occurs, and the dynamic P1/P3 junction is buttressed by two pseudoknots.

The pyrimidine-rich motif in P1 observed here in multiple states is reminiscent of metastable U-rich RNA thermometers ( 37 , 38 ) and highlights the base-pairing flexibility of uridine, which is observed to pair with all four nucleobases in these structures. Assuming no P1 stem unwinding, the pyrimidine-rich bulge would be positioned ~11 to 12 bp into P1, a length critical for bacterial ribosome frameshifting by HIV-1 and dnaX elements ( 39 ). As mutation of the pyrimidine-rich bulge reduces frameshifting and nearby mutations (e.g., C2502U, C2568A) enhance frameshifting ( Fig. 5 A ), and as the region varies considerably among structures ( Fig. 4 ), flexibility in this region is likely key to triggering the right amount of frameshifting. Observed motions in P1–P3 ( Fig. 3 C ) possibly capture part of the heterogeneity that gives rise to ~5 to 10% frameshifting, the observed ratio of retroviral proteins.

Recently, the importance of conformational degeneracy in frameshifting has been rationalized in terms of Shannon entropy [( 16 ) and references therein], which describes the degree of heterogeneity in populating a set of states. For frameshifting elements, the relevant set of states is the conformational ensemble of the RNA, not the ultimate outcome (i.e., readthrough, translation termination, etc.). This views the ribosome as a decoder that accepts a set of states and decides on the outcome. Among RNA elements that promote frameshifting, those with high Shannon entropy promote frameshifting to a greater extent ( 16 ), as judged by single-molecule force spectroscopy experiments. Observing multiple states for RNA is inherently difficult, typically sacrificing certainty in real-time observation for clarity in resolution or vice versa. Here, we have determined the atomistic details of multiple structural states ( Figs. 1 and 2 ) for the RSV frameshifting element. However, these are only the states that were successfully crystallized and phased or were classified to interpretable maps. As the relatively low frameshifting of RSV suggests that the Shannon entropy should also be relatively low, it is tempting to speculate that the RNA adopts relatively few states compared to other frameshifting elements. This could in part be due to the “winged” organization of the RSV element, which is rigidified by the PK1–PK2–P2 junction ( SI Appendix , Fig. S2 G ) and only partially unfolds during normal translation termination. Along these lines, stabilizing mutations increase the Shannon entropy by populating a relatively minor state, possibly the frameshifting-promoting one exemplified by the cryoEM structure. Our structural analysis provides a starting point toward an atomistic understanding of the mechanism of action of this regulatory RNA. More broadly, our work sheds light on the structural complexity and heterogeneity of frameshifting stimulatory elements.


## Materials and Methods

Detailed methods can be found in SI Appendix .


## RNA Preparation, Crystallization, and CryoEM Sample Preparation.

RNAs were transcribed, purified, and refolded at 6 g/L in a buffer containing 25 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.4, 0.15 M KCl, and 10 mM MgCl 2 . For size exclusion chromatography, aliquots of folded RNA were injected onto a Superdex 200 10/300 GL Increase column (Cytiva). For crystallization, 1.4 µL of folded RNA was mixed with 0.7 µL of reservoir solution containing 20% PEG 3350 (w/v), 0.25 M NaCl, and 0.1 M Tris-HCl, pH 8.5, at 21 °C. For phasing, crystals were transferred to a cryoprotectant solution containing 2 mM iridium hexammine. The two copies of the RNA apparent in the asymmetric unit were separately traced. For cryoEM samples, 3 µL folded RNA was pipetted onto R 1.2/1.3 Quantifoil grids, 300 mesh, and plunged into liquid ethane. Data were collected on a Titan Krios (ThermoFisher Scientific) equipped with a Gatan BioQuantum K3 camera and imaging filter. The X-ray model for chain B was used as a starting point for cryoEM model building.


## Dual Luciferase Frameshifting Assays.

Frameshifting assays were performed using the TnT T7-Coupled Rabbit Reticulocyte Lysate System (Promega), incubated for 90 min at 30 °C, and recorded using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) in a Tecan Infinite F200 plate reader.


## In-Line Probing and DMS Probing.

114-nt RSV RNAs containing A (WT), C, G, or U at position 2546 were used for probing studies. For in-line probing, these RNAs were 32 P-end-labeled, incubated on the bench for 40 h, and visualized on 8% polyacrylamide sequencing gels. For DMS probing, these RNAs were modified with DMS and reverse transcribed with 32 P-end-labeled DNA primer, the products of which were visualized on 8% polyacrylamide sequencing gels.
