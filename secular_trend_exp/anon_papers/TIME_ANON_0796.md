## Abstract

RNA interference (RNAi) is an important pathway for gene regulation and immunity. Dicer is a conserved RNAi pathway protein that is found in eukaryotes, with many species expressing multiple dicer gene copies. However, there remains entire lineages such as the phylum Tardigrada with little information about the presence and diversity of dicer genes, limiting our understanding of the evolution and diverse functions of dicer . Our study sought to understand dicer evolution in tardigrades and its phylogenetic relationship to other invertebrate dicer s, including the dicer gene duplication in arthropods.

Comparative genomic analyses with ten tardigrade species revealed an expansion of dicer genes in tardigrades. The eight Eutardigrades investigated – Hypsibius exemplaris , Ramazzottius varieornatus , Paramacrobiotus metropolitanus , P. fairbanksi , P. richtersi , Richtersius cf. coronifer , Acutuncus antarcticus and Milnesium tardigradum – and two Heterotardigrades Echiniscoides cf. sigismundi and Echiniscus testudo each contain two to four copies of dicer in their genome. Our results are consistent with a Dicer duplication in the common ancestor of Tardigrada and Arthropoda, followed by lineage-specific duplications that form a sister clade to the arthropod Dicer-2. The tardigrade Dicers contain canonical conserved domains including helicase, PAZ, and RNase III, and their domain-specific phylogenetic diversification suggests functional differentiation after duplication, especially for helicase.

Our study identified multiple distinct tardigrade-specific dicer duplications following an ancient duplication that is shared in both Eutardigrades and Heterotardigrades. Phylogenetic analyses are consistent with a Dicer duplication before the split of Tardigrada and Arthropoda that led to Dicer-2, which also groups with duplications found in Kinorhyncha and Platyhelminthes. Our study provides insights into Dicer evolution in Tardigrada by characterizing dicer duplications and divergence across various lineages.

## Background

RNA interference (RNAi) is a highly conserved post-transcriptional biological process that reduces mRNA levels to limit the expression of genes [ 1 ]. The RNAi molecular machinery recognizes and cleaves double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) to generate small single-stranded RNA that binds to complementary mRNA, which is then degraded via Argonaute proteins [ 2 – 4 ]. RNAi mediates both gene regulation and antiviral immune responses against viruses in protists, animals, plants, and fungi [ 3 , 5 , 6 ]. The initiating protein for RNAi is Dicer, the endoribonuclease that recognizes and cleaves dsRNA, producing micro RNAs (miRNAs) that are involved in gene regulation [ 1 , 2 ] or small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) that are used in viral defense [ 4 ]. Dicer proteins are found in every organism that uses RNAi for gene regulation [ 2 , 5 , 7 ], suggesting strong natural selection maintaining Dicer function.

Dicer contains highly conserved structural elements that contribute to its function. These domains are present in organisms such as Porifera and Chordata [ 8 ]. Three of the most conserved domains are the helicase domain, the Piwi Argonaute Zwille (PAZ) domain, and the RNase III domains. The helicase and PAZ domains are both implicated in binding dsRNA; the helicase domain shows preference for either blunt/5’ overhangs, while the PAZ domain binds 3’ overhangs [ 9 – 11 ]. Dicers also contain two RNase III domains at the C-terminus, which cleave the dsRNA as part of the process to create miRNA or siRNA [ 12 ]. Dicer consists of additional domains, such as a dimerization domain to bind partner proteins, and a dsRNA binding domain (dsRBD) to assist in binding RNA, although these are not required for Dicer function [ 12 – 14 ]. The conserved nature of Dicer makes it an interesting candidate to understand phylogenetic relationships between highly diverged organisms.

The use of post-transcriptional gene regulation via RNAi is highly conserved among animals, but the number of Dicers that are used to carry out this function varies across species. While most animals have a single Dicer, numerous non-bilaterians such as poriferans, cnidarians, and placozoans have multiple dicer genes [ 7 , 15 ], suggesting that animals had an ancestral dicer duplication that was later lost among different lineages [ 16 – 18 ], although it is unclear whether each copy contributes to RNAi functions [ 7 ]. Among bilaterians, arthropods have a dicer gene duplication that has given rise to two specialized Dicer proteins: Dcr-1, which is solely associated with generating miRNAs for endogenous RNAi, and Dcr-2, which is involved in exogenous siRNA [ 19 ]. It is not entirely clear whether this duplication occurred before or after arthropods split from other closely related animals, including tardigrades.


## Tardigrades represent an opportunity to understand the emergence of early dicer duplications

Tardigrades diverged from other animals around 500–700 million years ago [ 20 ] and recent studies suggest they are most closely related to the phyla Arthropoda and Onychophora [ 21 , 22 ]. The phylogenetic placement of tardigrades allows us to study dicer evolution in a lineage that emerged near the split of arthropods (which have 2 dicer genes) and nematodes (which have 1 dicer gene). Several tardigrade species have genome and transcriptome assemblies available allowing us to assess if this phylum shares the same dicer duplication seen in arthropods, or if they underwent their own lineage-specific dicer duplications.

In addition to their phylogenetic position, tardigrades can be used to understand the evolution of dicers among diverged and functionally specialized species. Tardigrades are well-known for their ability to survive extreme conditions by entering a state of cryptobiosis [ 23 ], and are found throughout the globe in a variety of environments, such as in salt and fresh water, moss and lichen [ 24 – 26 ]. Their biological characteristics might have uniquely shaped tardigrade RNAi; the process of cryptobiosis may have required specialized endogenous gene regulation to tolerate and survive desiccation, while the wide range of habitats may have led to the evolutionary diversification of RNAi components, as different tardigrades would potentially need to respond to vastly different communities of dsRNA viruses. Previous research has established that RNAi can be used to reduce gene expression in tardigrades [ 27 , 28 ], and so they must contain conserved RNAi machinery such as dicer or another dicer -like gene, but little else is known. The goal of our study was to identify dicer genes in tardigrades, characterize their protein domain structure, and determine their sequence relationships with other animal dicers .

There are two major evolutionary lineages of tardigrades: Eutardigrades and Heterotardigrades [ 29 ], which diverged around 500 million years ago [ 20 ] shortly after the emergence of tardigrades themselves. Eutardigrades live in freshwater/semiterrestrial environments and tend to have very conserved morphology across species, while Heterotardigrades vary greatly in morphology and live in marine/semiterrestrial environments [ 30 ]. We investigated dicers among ten species of tardigrades using published genomics and transcriptomics datasets: eight Eutardigrade species Hypsibius exemplaris , Ramazzottius varieornatus , Paramacrobiotus metropolitanus , P. fairbanksi , P. richtersi , Richtersius ci. coronifer , Acutuncus antarcticus and Milnesium tardigradum [ 31 – 35 ], and two Heterotardigrades, Echiniscoides cf. sigismundi and Echiniscus testudo [ 36 , 37 ]. We discovered multiple dicer duplications in each species including some that were shared and unique among the tardigrade species that we categorized into five (tDicer) groups. A conserved domain analysis revealed canonical Dicer motifs, including helicase, PAZ, and two RNase III domains. Protein domain analyses indicate substantial sequence and structural divergence among the copies that are suggestive of functional diversification, particularly in the helicase domain. We show that tDicer1, tDicer2, and tDicer3 are expressed differently throughout the lifespan in H. exemplaris and R. varieornatus , hinting that tDicers are involved to different degrees during development. While phylogenetic topologies are consistent with an ancient duplication shared with the one that gave rise to Dicer-2 in arthropods, they also indicate multiple subsequent independent duplications of dicer in tardigrades.


## Methods


## Phylogenetic analysis of tardigrades using 18S

Mitochondrial 18S ribosomal RNA sequences of tardigrades from NCBI, in addition to D. melanogaster and C. elegans , were aligned using MAFFT v7.481 [ 38 ] with default settings. Redundant and most short (< 500aa) sequences were removed, while keeping representative sequences from the 10 species with Dicer information and from each of the four major tardigrade orders (Additional file 1: Table S1 ). Remaining aligned sequences (Additional file 3) were cleaned with BMGE [ 39 ] and a phylogeny was generated in IQ-TREE 2 [ 40 ] after inferring the maximum-likelihood tree using the best-fit substitution model from ModelFinder [ 41 ] and applying both the SH-like approximate likelihood ratio test [ 42 ] and branch supports using the ultrafast bootstrap approximation method [ 43 ], with 1000 replicates each. A phylogeny using a Bayesian CAT model implemented in PhyloBayes v4.1 [ 44 ] was also generated for comparison. Archaeopteryx (sites.google.com/view/archaeopteryxjs) was used to root and visualize the phylogenies.


## Tardigrade dicer gene identification

Protein sequences were acquired from ten tardigrade species, including those derived from the genome assemblies of H. exemplaris (originally nHd.3.1.5 from the Ensembl tardigrade server, GCA_002082055.1 from NCBI), R. varieornatus (originally Rv101 from the Ensembl tardigrade server, GCA_001949185.1 from NCBI) and P. metropolitanus (GCF_019649055.1 from NCBI), as well as those derived from the transcriptomes of P. fairbanksi ( GKQV00000000.1 from GenBank), P. richtersi ( GFGY00000000.1 from GenBank), R. cf. coronifer (PRJNA475961 from SRA), Acutuncus antarcticus ( GKQX00000000.1 from GenBank), M. tardigradum ( GFGZ00000000.1 from GenBank), Echiniscus testudo (PRJNA669587 from SRA), and E. cf. sigismundi (PRJNA357357 from SRA). Transcriptome assemblies from the two Heterotardigrades from Mapalo et al. ([YEAR]) were also used. The BUSCO [ 45 ] completeness scores for these assemblies ranged from 43% ( Milnesium tardigradum ) to 83% ( Paramacrobiotus fairbanksi ) complete BUSCOs using the metazoan_odb12 (Additional file 1: Table S2 ), suggesting that some dicers may be missing or partially assembled. For transcriptomes with only nucleotide sequences available, TransDecoder v5.5.0 ( [GITHUB_REPO_REDACTED] ) was used to infer protein sequences. The program Diamond version 2.0.4 [ 46 ] was used to identify homologous protein sequences between pairs of species among the ten tardigrades, C. elegans (accession GCF_000002985.6) and D. melanogaster (accession GCF_000001215.4), using default settings. Tardigrade sequences whose match with the Dicer sequences from C. elegans ( NP_498761.2 ) or D. melanogaster (Dcr-1: NP_524453.1 ; Dcr-2: NP_523778.2 ) were deemed putative Dicer orthologues (Additional file 1: Table S3 ). After initially analyzing alignments and phylogenies, some sequences from the same species were identical or near identical, in which case only the longest sequence was kept. In three cases, partial transcript sequences from the same species were merged because they formed a monophyletic group while covering different regions of the dicer gene (Additional file 1: Table S3 ).


## Phylogenetic analysis of Dicers

Phylogenies were created with all tardigrade Dicer protein sequences and sequences from other species to determine the evolutionary relationships among Dicers. First, NCBI’s protein-protein BLAST was used to find the most similar non-tardigrade sequences for each tardigrade Dicer from various lineages across Ecdysozoa and Lophotrochozoa (using the accession numbers from Table 1 ) . Additionally, other animal Dicer sequences were sourced from transcriptomes generated in Herranz et al. [ 47 ], in which we found the orthogroup (OG0001057) that contains the Dicer sequence in C. elegans , and using Diamond (following the same procedures as above for tardigrades) to identify Dicers among transcriptomes from Mapalo et al. [ 36 ], Sato et al. [ 48 ] and Lord et al. [ 49 ]. The sequences (Additional file 1: Table S4; Additional file 3) were used to generate phylogenies with IQ-TREE 2 using the posterior mean site frequency (PMSF) model [ 50 ] computed from a guide tree with the LG + C60 + F model, and with PhyloBayes as described above.


## Domain analysis

Phylogenetic analyses of protein domains were used to determine the evolution of specific protein motifs. To identify conserved Dicer domains present in tardigrade sequences, the Conserved Domain (CD) (CDD v3.21) search [ 51 ] was used on all tardigrade, D. melanogaster , and C. elegans Dicer protein sequences using default settings except for requiring the E-value to be < 0.001 ( Additional file 1: Table S5 ). After obtaining the locations of the conserved domains, the amino acid region was extracted for each of the domains and were each aligned with MAFFT for phylogenetic reconstruction using IQ-TREE 2 and PhyloBayes as performed for the full Dicer sequence phylogenies. Tardigrade Dicers lacking a conserved domain (e.g., M. tardigradum tDicer1 does not contain a helicase domain) could not be included in the specific domain phylogenies.


## AlphaFold helicase structure prediction and comparison

AlphaFold2 (ColabFold v1.5.5: AlphaFold2 using MMseqs2) [ 52 ] was used to predict helicase secondary and tertiary structure across the tardigrade Dicers. Helicase domains obtained from the CD search were entered into AlphaFold and ran with default settings. Unrelaxed structures were obtained with no template mode. MSA was mmseqs2_uniref_env and unpaired_paired. Alphafold2_ptm was used with 3 recycles. The pairing strategy was greedy. Rank 1 structures were displayed using Mol* [ 53 ]. To quantify the similarity in helicase structure, the package bio3d v2.4-5 [ 54 ] was used to produce a root mean square deviation (RMSD) matrix after aligning structures with MUSCLE v3.8.31 [ 55 ].


## Gene expression of dicers in H. exemplaris and R. varieornatus

To determine the expression of dicer genes in H. exemplaris and R. varieornatus , RNA-seq datasets were acquired from a variety of developmental stages (Additional file 1: Table S6) previously generated by Yoshida et al. [ 35 , 56 ], and the assembled genome and annotation (GFF3) files were obtained from the Ensembl tardigrade server ( http://ensembl.tardigrades.org ) in the summer of [YEAR]. Trim Galore version 0.6.3 [ 57 ] was used to remove low-quality reads from the RNA-seq data using the default settings. STAR version 2.7.5b [ 58 ] was then used on the trimmed reads to map them against the respective reference genomes using default settings. After converting the GFF3 files to GTF files using gffread version 2.2.1.0 [ 59 ], featureCounts version 1.6.4 [ 60 ] was used to assign reads to genes. The featureCounts counts were then downloaded for analysis in R [ 61 ] where the data were transformed to transcripts per million (TPM) and dicer gene expression plotted across developmental stages.


## Results

Before our investigation of dicer evolution in tardigrades, we first wanted to establish the phylogenetic relationships among the ten species used in this study. To do this, we constructed a phylogeny using 18S rRNA sequences from 60 tardigrade species across four orders, including all ten of our tardigrade species of interest (Additional file 1: Table S1 ). We see the two distinct clades of Heterotardigrades (including E. testudo and E. cf. sigismundi ) and Eutardigrades (including the other eight target tardigrade species) with strong bootstrap support (Fig. 1 ; Additional file 2: Figure S1 ). Among Eutardigrades, Hypsibius groups with Acutuncus and Ramazzottius , while the Paramacrobiotus species form a monophyletic group and are most closely related to Richtersius . Milnesium is distantly related but still groups with other Eutardigrades. This phylogeny showing the evolutionary relationship between our ten tardigrade species of interest demonstrates the wide breadth of tardigrade diversity represented in our study spanning both major evolutionary clades.

## Tardigrades have multiple dicer genes

We sought to identify members of the tardigrade Dicer gene family using comparative sequence analysis between ten tardigrade species and Dicer proteins from C. elegans and D. melanogaster (Additional file 1: Table S3 ). This revealed that all investigated tardigrades contain multiple copies of Dicer. Six tardigrades had three copies of dicer ( H. exemplaris , A. antarcticus , R. varieornatus , P. metropolitanus , P. richtersi and P. fairbanksi ). In the three species with a genome assembly ( H. exemplaris , R. varieornatus , and P. metropolitanus ), the three corresponding dicer genes were found at different chromosomal positions, indicating distinct loci rather than allelic differences of the same gene. The four other tardigrade species, R. cf. coronifer , M. tardigradum , E. testudo and E. cf. sigismundi , which do not yet have fully assembled genomes, contained four putative copies (Table 1 ). It should be noted that several Dicer sequences from three of these species may be partial Dicer sequences including different parts of the same Dicer copy, as they are relatively short in length (< 1,000 amino acids) and missing key protein domains (see protein domain section below), potentially due to incomplete sequencing or assembly of the transcriptomes.

## Lineage-specific duplications of dicer in tardigrades

After establishing that tardigrade species contain dicer duplications, we sought to understand their evolutionary relationships and when the duplications occurred. Because arthropods also contain a dicer duplication, one possibility is that a common ancestor of arthropods and tardigrades had at least two dicer copies that were kept in both lineages. Alternatively, tardigrades may have their own lineage-specific dicer duplications independent of the arthropod duplication. To evaluate the relationships among Dicer sequences, phylogenetic trees were generated using Dicer protein sequences from tardigrades and other Ecdysozoans (from the phyla Arthropoda, Nematoda, Nematomorpha, Onychophora, Kinorhyncha, and Priapulida) and Lophotrochozoans (Mollusca, Nemertea, Platyhelminthes) as outgroups (Additional file 1: Table S4). Our phylogenetic analysis enabled us to identify five tardigrade Dicer groups, which we refer to as tDicer1 to tDicer5 (Table 1 ; Fig. 2 ; Additional file 2: Figure S2 ). The phylogeny separated tardigrade Dicers into two separate major clades: tDicer1 contains a single Dicer copy from nine of the ten species and is sister to nematode Dicers, and another clade that exclusively contains multiple tardigrade Dicers (tDicer2-5) that is sister to Dicer-2 from Arthoropoda, Kinorhyncha and Platyhelminthes with strong bootstrap support (99%) and a posterior probability of 1 (Fig. 2 ; Additional file 2: Figure S2 ). Five Eutardigrades ( H. exemplaris , A. antarcticus , R. varieornatus , P. richtersi and P. fairbanksi ) each have one copy of tDicer1, tDicer2 and tDicer3. P. metropolitanus also has three dicers , with one tDicer2 and two tDicer3, but interestingly it lacks a tDicer1 unlike the two other species of Paramacrobiotus . R. cf. coronifer contains four dicers , one each in tDicer1, tDicer2, and tDicer3, and an additional tDicer4 that is separate from tDicer2 and tDicer3. Milnesium tardigradum also has four putative Dicer copies, one tDicer1 and three other sequences we labeled as tDicer 4 that are sister to tDicer2-3 - they all are short sequences less than 400 amino acids long, which might have affected phylogenetic placement, The Heterotardigrades E. cf. sigismundi and E. testudo each have four putative dicer sequences, one tDicer1 and three more in tDicer5, which is a clade exclusive to Heterotardigrade Dicers.

## Tardigrade Dicers contain conserved protein domains

After establishing that tardigrades contain multiple copies of dicer , we wanted to determine if tardigrade Dicers contained the canonical functional domains found in other species. Dicers typically consist of a N-terminal helicase domain, a dimerization domain, a PAZ domain, two RNase III domains, and a C-terminal double-stranded RNA-binding domain (dsRBD), although variations in structure do exist across lineages [ 12 , 62 ]. We annotated Dicer domain structure along tardigrade sequences by identifying conserved superfamily motifs (Additional file 1: Tables S4 and S5). Almost all tardigrade Dicers contain a helicase, PAZ, and two RNase III domains (among other motifs), with some notable exceptions (Fig. 3 ). P. richtersi tDicer1 and tDicer2 do not contain helicase domains, each M. tardigradum Dicer contains only portions of canonical domains, and most Heterotardigrade Dicers also lack various domains: E. testudo tDicer1 and tDicer5a do not contain helicases while tDicer5b and tDicer5c contain only helicases and no other domains, and E. cf. sigismundi tDicer1 and tDicer5c contain RNase III domains but no PAZ or helicase domains, while tDicer5a contains no RNase III domains. As these sequences was derived from transcriptome datasets, they could represent incomplete (artificially truncated) dicer gene sequences or some other artifact.

## Phylogenetic relationships of Dicer domain sequences

To explore the possibility of functional diversification among tardigrade Dicers, as seen in arthropod duplicate Dicers, we created phylogenies of individual protein domains using the amino acid sequence from helicase, PAZ, and RNase III domains (Additional file 1: Table S5). Across the different domain phylogenies, the evolutionary relationships among tardigrade Dicer duplicates were largely consistent with the full-length Dicer phylogeny but with some inter-domain differences both among tDicer groups and with their placement relative to arthropod Dicer-1 and Dicer-2 (Fig. 4 ; Additional file 2: Figure S3 ). In the helicase domain phylogeny, tDicer1 forms a clade with arthropod Dicer-1 with moderate node support (87% bootstrap, 0.95 posterior probability), arthropod Dicer-2 is a sister to all tardigrade Dicers with somewhat moderate support (69% bootstrap, 0.6 posterior probability), and tDicer3 is not monophyletic (Fig. 4 A; Additional file 2: Figure S3 A). In the PAZ phylogeny, most nodes between different tDicers have weak support, and tDicer4 is sister to all other tardigrade and arthropod Dicers (Fig. 4 B; Additional file 2: Figure S3 B). The RNase III phylogeny clearly separates the upstream RNase IIIa from the downstream RNase IIIb (Fig. 4 C; Additional file 2: Figure S3 C). In the RNase IIIa clade, the relationships among tDicers mirror the full-length Dicer phylogeny but with weaker node supports among lineages, including the grouping of arthropod Dicer-2 with tDicer5 (63% bootstrap support, 0.65 posterior probability). In the RNase IIIb phylogeny, tDicer3 is sister to all other RNase IIIb sequences (53% bootstrap support, 0.34 posterior probability), and tDicer5 groups with the nematode Dicer and both arthropod Dicers (82% bootstrap support, 0.52 posterior probability), forming a sister clade to tDicer1 but with weak support (56% bootstrap support, 0.53 posterior probability). Overall, the tDicers (i.e., tDicer1-5) form monophyletic groups across the domain phylogenies except with helicase. This identifies the helicase domain as an interesting exception, which may contribute to functional differences in dsRNA selectivity.

## tDicer3 shows high heterogeneity in helicase structure

To test the potential for functional differences in the helicase domain, which demonstrates a unique topology (Fig. 4 A), AlphaFold2 via ColabFold [ 52 ] was used to compare structural differences among all tDicers as well as C. elegans Dicer and D. melanogaster Dcr-1 and Dcr-2 (Fig. 5 A). To quantify the difference in structure, a root mean square deviation (RMSD) matrix was used to measure the average distance between atoms after alignment in 3D space (Fig. 5 B). Based on the RMSD, Dcr-1 in D. melanogaster is the most dissimilar in structure to all other Dicers. tDicer1 helicases form their own cluster with similar secondary structure between species within tDicer1 but diverged from other tDicers, and they are among the most dissimilar to the structures in C. elegans and D. melanogaster. tDicer2 clusters with several tDicer3 helicases, which show the most heterogeneity in structure across species; while R. varieornatus and R. cf. coronifer tDicer3 helicases share high similarity with tDicer2, other tDicer3 helicases are more similar to the structure from nematode Dicer and arthropod Dcr-2. The two tDicer4 sequences with helicases have variable similarities with other tDicers and do not form their own cluster. Among the four tDicer5 copies with helicases, tDicer5c in E. testudo is the most dissimilar to all other Dicers. The structural differences among the tDicer helicase domain could be responsible for functional differentiation in dsRNA binding among tardigrade Dicers after duplication.

## H. exemplaris and R. varieornatus dicer genes are each expressed across development and in tun state

After identifying putative dicer s in tardigrades, the expression patterns of those genes were examined to determine if the duplicate genes were actively expressed. We used published RNA-seq data from H. exemplaris and R. varieornatus across their life cycle (from egg to juvenile to adult) and in the dormant tun state to quantify the expression of all three dicer s across the lifespan of the two tardigrade species (Fig. 6 ; Additional file 1: Table S6). All three H. exemplaris dicers are expressed at higher levels (up to two-fold change) in eggs during the first three days after laying, implicating them in early development (Fig. 6 A). All three dicers are also expressed at higher levels (up to and over two-fold) during tun, further implicating them in maintenance of homeostasis during cryptobiosis. R. varieornatus dicers are each expressed at variable levels across the organism’s lifespan (Fig. 6 B). The R. varieornatus tDicer1 is most highly expressed in mature, active adults (more than two-fold change compared to most egg and juvenile stages), which would imply this Dicer is involved in homeostasis primarily in adults. Similarly, tDicer2 is most highly expressed in mature adults, but has much more uniform expression across the R. varieornatus life cycle. tDicer3 however is expressed most highly in eggs during the first three days of development (more than two-fold greater than all juvenile stages), but is also highly expressed in mature, active adults. As with H. exemplaris dicers , this would indicate tDicer3 as being active during early development, in addition to adults, but less important during tun in R. varieornatus . Overall, these results indicate that the three tardigrade dicers are expressed to different extents throughout development and may be involved in different functions between the two species, and possibly from one another.

## Discussion


## Tardigrades contain lineage-specific dicer duplications

In this study, we identified multiple dicer homologues in tardigrades and investigated their evolutionary diversification. We found that the dicer gene family underwent clade-specific duplications: six Eutardigrade species have three dicer genes, while four other tardigrades (Eutardigrades R. cf. coronifer and M. tardigradum , and Heterotardigrades E. cf. sigismundi and E. testudo ) have up to four genes (Table 1 ). The three most prevalent copies (tDicer1, tDicer2 and tDicer3) are constitutively expressed throughout development and in adults, suggesting that each copy is functional. Based on our phylogenetic and comparative sequence analyses with other animals [ 7 ], the first dicer duplication occurred before the split of Eutardigrades and Heterotardigrades, separating tDicer1 and tDicer2-5. There is strong support that this duplication event is shared with the one that gave rise to the arthropod Dicer-2. However, tardigrades subsequently underwent lineage-specific duplications. Within Eutardigrades, a duplication gave rise to tDicer2 and tDicer3 (Fig. 2 ). Three Eutardigrades have an extra duplicate copy that appears to have originated from independent duplication events, although there remains uncertainty about their origins. While R. cf. coronifer has a full-length tDicer4 that is consistently sister to tDicer2 and tDicer3, M. tardigradum has three short tDicer4 sequences that may lack the resolution to place phylogenetically; neither of these sequences overlap with one another, raising the question whether they represent partial sequences of a single gene (or two) that were incompletely sequenced in the available transcriptome. Given the divergence in the sequences that do overlap, this species has at least two Dicer copies if not more. P. metropolitanus is the only species with a missing tDicer1 but is also the only species that has two genes in tDicer3 that always cluster together phylogenetically. This possibly originated from an independent recent duplication, as other Paramacrobiotus species do not share this variation, but it could also be the result of concerted evolution in P. metropolitanus or an artifact from the reference genome assembly. Ultimately, the result is that P. metropolitanus , M. tardigradum and R. cf. coronifer appear to have different Dicer repertoires, not only from one another but also from the other Eutardigrades investigated. Meanwhile, the Heterotardigrades E. cf. sigismundi and E. testudo both have tDicer1 and three sequences that form a tDicer5 clade, sister to tDicer2-tDicer3-tDicer4. Their tDicer5 expansion appears separate from the tDicer2-3-4 diversification in Eutardigrades, although questions remain as to how many duplications occurred and if they are shared in both species given their phylogenetic placement (Fig. 2 ) and some of the short sequences (Fig. 3 ) that might represent partial sequencing of different regions of the same copy.

To further understand the nature of tDicer diversification, we analyzed canonical Dicer domains present within proteins. We uncovered that there is a high degree of structural conservation in tardigrade Dicer domains, including the helicase, PAZ, and RNase III domains, indicating that selective pressures do not favor global changes to tDicer structure. However, not all tardigrade species contain full-length Dicer proteins with the expected domains, possibly due to incomplete sequence assemblies from transcriptomic data. P. richtersi tDicer1 and 2 lack helicase domains, while M. tardigradum , E. testudo , and E. cf. sigismundi tDicers do not have any Dicer containing all the canonical Dicer domains. The loss of helicase function is seen in D. melanogaster Dicer-1 [ 63 ] and Giardia [ 12 ], suggesting that helicase activity may not be required for certain tDicer functions. There may also be different selective pressures that segregate tDicer functions and allow further regulation of small RNAs in tardigrades as seen in D. melanogaster . For instance, distinct helicase-domain-proteins possibly bind dsRNA and activate cell-signaling pathways that could then recruit RNase III-domain-proteins to generate siRNAs or miRNAs. Domain-specific phylogenies revealed topologies that differed from the full-length Dicer phylogeny, which might suggest distinct evolutionary pressures among the domains, but could also be the result of assembly artifacts.

Dicer domain evolution has been linked to altering RNA-binding specificity and binding partners [ 8 ], which may explain why certain tardigrade domains are most closely related to different species depending on the phylogeny. Certain tardigrade Dicer domains may have evolved specificity for certain substrates and co-factors, specificity that may be shared with the most closely related species; for instance, tDicer4 of R. cf. coronifer is distinctly different from other tDicers in the PAZ phylogeny, which might suggest that this species-specific duplication evolved novel functions for interacting with dsRNA. Meanwhile, the helicase phylogeny suggests diversification among tDicer3 copies. Structural prediction of the helicase domain revealed variable 3D structures, particularly among copies from the tDicer3 clade. As helicase in Dicer is known to confer binding-specificity [ 64 ], this may suggest different binding activity among copies in tDicer3. Overall, we show that tardigrade tDicer domains are largely conserved among copies and species, but are evolving at different rates and may underlie functional diversification.

Our expression data analysis reveals that expression of tardigrade duplicate dicers vary across species and age. In H. exemplaris , expression of all three of their dicers (tDicer1, tDicer2, and tDicer3) is highest in eggs in the first three days after laying, followed by tun state expression. It is known in C. elegans that miRNAs are involved with developmental processes [ 65 , 66 ], a function which may be conserved in H. exemplaris and potentially other tardigrades. Expression of all three dicers is also high in the tun state, which suggests that the RNAi pathway is important to maintain homeostasis during cryptobiosis. Meanwhile, R. varieornatus shows different expression among their dicers with all three dicers expressed highly in active adults. tDicer3 is most highly expressed in early development, which implies that this dicer may be involved in development, as seen in H. exemplaris . The miRNA pathway has been shown in C. elegans to be involved in germline development and differentiation [ 67 ], which may also be the case in R. varieornatus . However, as we do not have expression data of dicers during active viral infection, it is unclear which dicers may also be involved in antiviral defense. In addition, it is unclear how expression may vary across the other tardigrade species we investigated, other than retrieving dicer sequences from transcriptomes of active pools of individuals. Regardless, the expression data suggests divergence in regulation among genes and provides evidence that some dicer functions may be involved in similar processes as dicers in C. elegans .

Although this study has established lineage-specific dicer duplications in tardigrades, functional assays are needed to determine their exact roles in exogenous and endogenous gene silencing. Prior reverse genetic studies have established that tardigrades do contain an RNAi pathway [ 27 , 28 ]. However, it is not clear whether these Dicer copies are involved in siRNA pathways or endogenous miRNA gene regulation. It is also intriguing to speculate that, due to their unique ability to survive extreme conditions, tardigrade Dicers might have evolved other non-canonical functions such as heat shock response, in addition to antiviral defense, as seen in honey bees [ 68 ]. It is possible that the different tardigrade dicer copies are involved in distinct pathways like in D. melanogaster . Dcr-1 and Dcr-2 in D. melanogaster generate distinct small RNAs; Dcr-1 generates miRNAs for endogenous gene regulation, while Dcr-2 generates siRNAs for exogenous gene regulation [ 19 ]. The helicase domain displayed the most structural variability in superfamily composition and in predicted protein structure among tardigrade dicer copies, which supports the idea that the helicase domain contributes to differences in RNA-binding specificity [ 69 ]. Based on these findings, the expansion of dicers in Tardigrada has given rise to a rich diversity of dicer content and sequence divergence that has potentially catalyzed the evolution and diversification of dicer functions among tardigrade species.


## Conclusions

Our study has identified tardigrade lineage-specific duplications of the RNAi-related gene dicer . Our results suggest that the duplication that gave rise to Dicer-1 and Dicer-2 in Arthropoda occurred in their common ancestor with Tardigrada, which also retained both duplicates from this duplication event. After this ancient duplication shared with arthropods, several independent duplications occurred among different tardigrade lineages. Each species investigated appears to have at least three distinct copies of dicer . While most copies contain conserved domains found in other animal dicers , sequence and structural domain divergence suggest functional differentiation of dicers in tardigrades. These findings shed light on lineage-specific duplication and diversification among tardigrade dicers . Future research could focus on characterizing tardigrade small RNAs across various developmental stages or during cryptobiosis to better understand how multiple dicer copies are functioning in tardigrades.
