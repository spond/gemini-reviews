# FEXP_ANON_078

## Abstract

The gut microbiota is associated with cardiometabolic disorders (including coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes, stroke, heart failure, and hypertension) through the gut–heart or gut–brain axis, among which metabolic processes play crucial roles. However, the exact causal mechanisms remain unknown. Our study sought to uncover the causal relationships between the gut microbiota, circulating metabolites and cardiometabolic diseases via Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. Forward MR was utilized to investigate the causal effects of the gut microbiota and circulating metabolites on the risk of cardiometabolic diseases. Reverse MR was subsequently performed to analyze the significant gut microbiota and circulating metabolites. Two-step MR was employed to examine the impact of circulating metabolites on the relationship between the gut microbiota and cardiometabolic diseases and to determine the mediated fractions. Our results revealed a nominal causal relationship with 23, 23, 22, 20, and 19 gut microbiota constituents for coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes, stroke, heart failure, and hypertension, respectively. We also identified 2 significant associations and 13 suggestive associations between circulating metabolites and these cardiometabolic diseases. Reverse MR analysis revealed that genetically predicted type 2 diabetes was suggestively associated with 3 circulating metabolites, whereas stroke demonstrated a suggestive association with 3 distinct gut microbiota. Further screening identified 4 circulating metabolites as potential mediators in the pathway from the gut microbiota to cardiometabolic diseases. Our study revealed a causal link between gut microbiome components and cardiometabolic diseases and that circulating metabolites potentially act as intermediaries in this association.

Keywords: cardiometabolic diseases, gut microbiota, mendelian randomization, metabolites


## 1. Introduction

Cardiometabolic diseases are a group of complex disorders caused by genetic and metabolic drivers, including hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, stroke, and other diseases. [ 1 ] These diseases pose a substantial public health challenge worldwide because of their high incidence and mortality rates. Insight into disease pathogenesis and curative treatments are therefore of paramount importance.

The gut microbiota is a vast microbial population that resides in the intestine and coexists with its host. [ 2 ] These gastrointestinal microbiota play important roles in human health by digesting food, synthesizing vital vitamins, stimulating and regulating the immune response, and supporting intestinal functions. [ 3 ] Recent studies have indicated that alterations in the gut flora are closely associated with the onset of diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular diseases, suggesting that modulating the intestinal microbiota could serve as a promising strategy for mitigating the risks associated with these diseases. [ 4 ] In addition, the gut microbiota is an endocrine organ that acts on distal organs via microbial metabolites and affects various host physiological or pathological events. [ 5 ] These gut microbiota-derived circulating metabolites include, but are not limited to, short-chain fatty acids, amino acids, lipopolysaccharides, and various hormones. [ 6 ] For example, short‐chain fatty acids, such as butyrate, which are produced by anaerobic fermentation of dietary fiber, have beneficial effects on cardiometabolic health. [ 7 ] In contrast, endotoxins such as lipopolysaccharides may adversely affect cardiometabolic health by increasing inflammation. [ 8 ] Thus, understanding the role of circulating metabolites in the interaction between the gut microbiota and the host is crucial for analyzing the pathogenesis of cardiometabolic diseases. Furthermore, this knowledge may aid in the development of new treatments and prevention strategies targeting the gut–heart or gut–brain axis, thereby improving the clinical management of cardiometabolic diseases.

Although potential associations have been established among the gut microbiota, metabolites, and cardiometabolic diseases, the specific causal links and mediators warrant further investigation. Mendelian randomization (MR) has provided promising approaches to solve this issue. MR is a novel method that uses genetic variations as instrumental variables to make causal inferences from observational data. [ 9 , 10 ] The basic principle behind this approach is that alleles are randomly transmitted from parents to offspring, thereby avoiding issues of confounding or reverse causality, similar to that of random treatment allocation in a randomized controlled trial. [ 9 ] In the present study, we conducted a comprehensive MR analysis to explore the causal relationships among the gut microbiota, circulating metabolites, and cardiometabolic diseases (including coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes, stroke, heart failure, and hypertension) via summary data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Additionally, we investigated whether circulating metabolites act as mediators in the pathway from the gut microbiota to cardiometabolic diseases.


## 2. Materials and methods


## 2.2. Study design

The study design is depicted in Figure 1 . First, we used bidirectional MR to explore the associations among the genetically predicted gut microbiota, circulating metabolites and the presence of cardiometabolic diseases. Second, we used mediation analysis to elucidate how circulating metabolites mediate the association between the gut microbiota and cardiometabolic diseases. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were defined as instrumental variables. The MR approach builds upon 3 core assumptions: the instrumental variables exhibit a robust correlation with the exposure; the instrumental variables are not associated with confounding factors; and the instrumental variables do not directly affect the outcome but can influence the outcome only via the exposure. [ 9 ] A completed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology Using Mendelian Randomization (STROBE-MR) statement was provided to increase the reliability of our findings (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q882 ).


> **Figure 1.:** Diagram of the study design. The basic principles of MR studies. Assumption 1: the instrumental variables must be associated with the exposure factors. Assumption 2: the instrumental variables are not associated with confounding factors. Assumption 3: the instrumental variables are not directly related to the outcomes. Mediation analysis: evaluated via a two-sample MR approach. MR = Mendelian randomization.


## 2.3. Data source

The GWAS summary data for the gut microbiota are publicly available in the GWAS Catalog under accession numbers, GCST90032172 to GCST90032644, which includes 473 gut microbial taxa. [ 11 ] Genetic variants associated with the gut microbiome were derived from genome-wide association tests applied to a total of 7979,834 genetic variants from 5959 Europeans recruited in the FINRISK 2002 cohort. [ 11 ] The genetic data for circulating metabolites came from a previous GWAS (33 cohorts comprised of 136,016 individuals), including 213 lipid and lipoprotein parameters or fatty acids and 20 nonlipid profiles. [ 12 ] In this study, most cohorts included individuals of European ancestry (6 Finnish and 21 non-Finnish) only; however, a subset of individuals with Asian ancestry (1 Han Chinese and 5 South Asian) was also included. The circulating metabolite GWAS summary dataset was downloaded from the GWAS Catalog (accession ID: GCST90301941-GCST90302173).

The summary data of coronary artery disease were obtained from the CARDIoGRAMplusC4D consortium and UK Biobank, encompassing 122,733 patients and 424,528 control individuals. [ 13 ] Coronary artery disease was defined by an inclusive diagnosis, including myocardial infarction, coronary stenosis > 50%, chronic stable angina, or acute coronary syndrome. The GWAS summary statistics for type 2 diabetes were derived from a reanalysis of publicly available data, comprising 12,931 cases and 57,196 controls. [ 14 ] Type 2 diabetes was diagnosed on the basis of self-reported medical history and International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes documented in electronic medical records. For the summary statistics of stroke and stroke subtypes, we utilized a recent, large-scale GWAS (MEGASTROKE) confined to European populations, which included 446,696 individuals (406,111 controls and 40,585 cases of any stroke, including ischemic stroke, large artery stroke, small vessel stroke, and cardioembolic stroke). [ 15 ] All cases of stroke were defined on the basis of the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria. The aggregated data for heart failure were obtained from a meta-analysis of 26 European ancestry GWASs conducted by Shah et al., which included 47,309 cases of heart failure and 930,014 controls. [ 16 ] Heart failure was confirmed by a review of hospitalization records, physician diagnoses or death records. Summary data for hypertension from the MRC-IEU UK Biobank OpenGWAS are based on the study by Elsworth et al. [ 17 ] Hypertension diagnosis was defined by patients who had ICD codes for hypertension or who received a prescribed medication for hypertension. The abovementioned populations were predominantly of European descent and largely independent of each other.


## 2.4. Instrumental variable selection

Given the limited availability of genome-wide significant SNPs ( P < 5 × 10 –8 ) for gut microbiota and metabolites, we adopted a threshold of P < 1 × 10 –5 to ensure sufficient instrumental variables for MR analysis, as previously described in similar MR studies. [ 18 , 19 ] We subsequently clumped the SNPs by removing linkage disequilibrium. The linkage disequilibrium of the selected SNPs, which are strongly associated with the gut microbiota and circulating metabolites, should meet the criteria of r 2 < 0.001 and a distance > 10,000 kb. [ 20 ] We further harmonized the SNPs for exposure and outcome from the same allele and removed all palindromic SNPs from the analysis. One of the assumptions of MR is that the SNPs do not directly influence the outcome. Therefore, each SNP was individually scrutinized through the Phenoscanner [ 21 ] to exclude associations with any confounding factors potentially related to the outcome.

The corresponding chromosome, effect allele (EA), other allele (OA), effect allele frequency (EAF), effect size (β), standard error (SE), and P value were extracted. The strength of the instrumental variables was assessed by F statistics and variance explained (R 2 ). We calculated the R 2 and F statistics via the following formula: R 2 = (2 × EAF × (1 − EAF) × β 2 )/(2 × EAF × (1 − EAF) × β 2 + 2 × EAF × (1 − EAF) × N × SE 2 ), F = R 2 × (N − 2)/(1 − R 2 ), where N is the sample size. [ 22 ] Weak instrumental variables were defined as those with an F statistic <10, and all weak instrumental variables were excluded. [ 23 ]


## 2.5. Data analysis

To evaluate a potential causal relationship between the gut microbiota, circulating metabolites, and cardiometabolic diseases, we conducted two-sample MR analysis. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was utilized as the primary MR analytical method because it provides the most precise effect estimates, and it was predominantly adopted as the principal analysis approach in numerous publications. [ 9 , 10 ] By conducting Wald ratio estimations for individual SNPs, the IVW method synthesizes them into a unified cumulative causal estimate. [ 24 ] To further validate the robustness of our findings, we employed several supplementary analytical techniques, including MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode. While MR-Egger regression can provide effect estimates adjusted for horizontal pleiotropy, this comes at the cost of reduced statistical precision. [ 25 ] The weighted median method aggregates effect estimates from genetic instruments through a median-based approach, while still yielding reliable causal effect estimates even when up to 50% of the instruments may be invalid. [ 26 ] The simple mode and weighted mode methods focus on evaluating the numerical majority and weighted majority of genetic instrument estimates, respectively, to determine both the directionality and effect magnitude of the causal relationship. [ 24 ] If the IVW method shows statistically significant results ( P < .05), even if other methods do not reach statistical significance, the finding can still be regarded as a positive discovery provided that no evidence of pleiotropy or heterogeneity is detected and the β estimates from other methods are directionally consistent. [ 10 ] A two-sided P value corrected by the Bonferroni method was defined as statistically significant, with values of 1.057 × 10 −4 (0.05/473) for the gut microbiota and 2.146 × 10 −4 (0.05/233) for circulating metabolites. A P < .05 but greater than the threshold corrected by the Bonferroni correction was considered suggestive of an association.

To assess the bidirectional causality between the gut microbiota, circulating metabolites, and cardiometabolic diseases, we treated cardiometabolic diseases as “exposure” and the gut microbiota or circulating metabolites associated with cardiometabolic diseases as “outcomes.” The SNPs that were significantly associated with cardiometabolic diseases ( P < 5 × 10 −5 ) were selected as instrumental variables.

We employed two-step MR analysis to investigate the potential mediating role of circulating metabolites in the relationship between the gut microbiota and cardiometabolic diseases. The gut microbiota and circulating metabolites, with significant causal relationships with cardiometabolic diseases, were included in the mediation analysis. First, we employed MR analysis to estimate the impact of the gut microbiota on cardiometabolic diseases (β). Using MR analysis, we subsequently identified circulating metabolites significantly associated with cardiometabolic diseases and assessed their effects on these conditions (β2). Finally, we utilized MR analysis to evaluate the influence of the gut microbiota related to cardiometabolic diseases on these circulating metabolites (β1). The total effect of the gut microbiota on cardiometabolic diseases (β) is decomposed into indirect effects mediated by the gut microbiota through mediators (β1×β2) and direct effects of the gut microbiota on cardiometabolic diseases (β−β1×β2). [ 27 ] Mediation effects can be either negative, diminishing the relationship, or positive, enhancing it. Negative mediation weakens the effect of independent variables on dependent variables, while positive mediation strengthens this effect.

To validate the reliability of the MR results, we performed multiple sensitivity analyses focusing on 3 types of analysis: heterogeneity tests, pleiotropy tests, and leave-one-out analysis tests. The IVW method was used to detect heterogeneity, which was measured via Cochran Q statistic and with a P > .05, indicating the absence of heterogeneity. [ 28 ] The MR–Egger intercept and MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) global test were applied to evaluate pleiotropy, with a P > .05, indicating no evidence of pleiotropy. [ 25 , 29 ] Results with significant heterogeneity or pleiotropy were systematically excluded from the analysis. Furthermore, we conducted leave-one-out analysis to assess whether the exclusion of a single SNP would impact the results. All the statistical analyses were executed via the “TwoSampleMR” and “ggplot2” packages via R software (version 4.3.0).

## 3.1.2. Type 2 diabetes

A total of 23 gut microbiota were found to be related to type 2 diabetes (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q882 ). Detailed SNP information for 23 gut microbiota is available in Table S3, (Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q882 ). The results revealed that Phocea massiliensis (OR = 1.472, 95% CI: 1.101–1.969, P = .0090), CAG-877 (OR = 1.184, 95% CI: 1.039–1.348, P = .0111), and Megasphaera sp900066485 (OR = 1.482, 95% CI: 1.091–2.013, P = .0118) increased the risk of type 2 diabetes. ## 3.2. Sensitivity analysis

The MR–Egger regression intercept approach revealed that genetic pleiotropy did not introduce bias into the findings, and the MR-PRESSO analysis demonstrated the absence of horizontal pleiotropy in the MR study ( P > .05, Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q882 ). The Cochran Q test did not reveal any significant heterogeneity ( P > .05, Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q882 ). The results derived from the “leave-one-out” analysis corroborate the reliability of MR analysis (the null line is not encompassed within the cumulative confidence intervals of all SNPs, Figures S1–S5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q883 ). Scatter plots illustrated the comprehensive impact of the gut microbiota and circulating metabolites on cardiometabolic diseases (Figures S6–S10, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q883 ). Additionally, the forest plot revealed causal relationships among the gut microbiota, circulating metabolites, and cardiometabolic diseases (Figures S11–S15, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q883 ).


## 3.3. Bidirectional causal effects of cardiometabolic diseases on the gut microbiota and circulating metabolites

As illustrated in Tables S7 and S8, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q882 , no inverse effects were detected between the gut microbiota, circulating metabolites, and 3 cardiometabolic diseases (coronary artery disease, heart failure, and hypertension). Type 2 diabetes exhibited a causal effect on lactate levels (OR = 1.019, 95% CI: 1.008–1.029, P = .0006), the cholesteryl ester-to-total lipid ratio in very small VLDLs (OR = 0.988, 95% CI: 0.977–0.999, P = .0280), and the phospholipid-to-total lipid ratio in chylomicrons and extremely large VLDLs (OR = 1.019, 95% CI: 1.009–1.030, P = .0004) but not on the gut microbiota (Tables S7 and S8, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q882 ). Stroke had a causal effect on GCA-900199385 sp900320755 (OR = 0.974, 95% CI: 0.950–0.999, P = .0381), Intestinimonas massiliensis (OR = 0.962, 95% CI: 0.932–0.992, P = .0143), and Megasphaera sp900066485 (OR = 0.964, 95% CI: 0.934–0.995, P = .0224) but not on circulating metabolites (Table S7 and S8, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q882 ).


## 3.4. Mediation analysis

This study demonstrated that both the gut microbiota and circulating metabolites exert causal effects on cardiometabolic diseases. Consequently, metabolites may serve as mediators in the pathway from the gut microbiota to cardiometabolic diseases. On the basis of the outcomes of MR analysis, we identified 4 circulating metabolites as potential mediators and evaluated their respective mediation effects, along with the corresponding 95% CIs (Table S9, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q882 ). With respect to the relationship between CAG-488 sp000434055 and coronary artery disease, the lactate level (mediation effect: 0.0146, 95% CI: −0.0504 to 0.0796) was found to be the mediator. With respect to the impact of UBA3855 sp900316885 on type 2 diabetes, the ratio of omega-3 fatty acids to total fatty acids (mediation effect: 0.0078, 95% CI: −0.0034 to 0.0191) was deemed a mediator. The triglyceride-to-total lipid ratio in chylomicrons and extremely large VLDLs (mediation effect: −0.0050, 95% CI: −0.0221 to 0.0121) was also identified as a mediator between Ruminococcus C sp000437255 and heart failure. Concomitantly, the phospholipid-to-total lipid ratio in very large VLDLs played a mediating role in the relationships between CAG-882 sp003486385 (mediation effect: 0.0010, 95% CI: −0.0002 to 0.0022), Atopobiaceae (mediation effect: 0.0022, 95% CI: −0.0040 to 0.0083) and hypertension.


## 4. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to use comprehensive state-of-the-art data to scrutinize the potential causal relationships among the gut microbiota, circulating metabolites, and the incidence of cardiometabolic diseases. The results revealed a nominal causal relationship with 23, 23, 22, 20, and 19 gut microbes for coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes, stroke, heart failure, and hypertension, respectively. We also identified 2 significant associations and 13 suggestive associations between circulating metabolites and these cardiometabolic diseases. Mediation analysis identified 4 circulating metabolites as potential mediators in the pathway from the gut microbiota to cardiometabolic diseases.

Our study indicated that Barnesiellaceae and Barnesiella may increase the risk of coronary artery disease, whereas Desulfovibrionaceae could diminish the risk of developing this disease. A greater level of Barnesiellaceae, known to produce a substantial amount of short-chain fatty acids linked to hypercholesterolemia and hypertriglyceridemia, was previously reported in patients with cardiovascular diseases than in healthy controls. [ 30 ] In contrast, Desulfovibrionaceae seemed to act as a protective factor for coronary artery disease. A key characteristic of Desulfovibrionaceae is its ability to convert sulfate into hydrogen sulfide through the process of sulfate reduction. Hydrogen sulfide has been found to prevent various types of cardiovascular diseases, including myocardial infarction, atherosclerosis, and hypertension. [ 31 , 32 ] Given that Desulfovibrionaceae is an important endogenous source of hydrogen sulfide, targeting Desulfovibrionaceae may facilitate future studies in this direction. Additionally, an increase in the risk of coronary artery disease was observed with elevated levels of lactate. Research has consistently demonstrated that elevated plasma lactate levels are correlated with a greater risk of all-cause and coronary artery disease mortality. [ 33 ]

In this study, Phocea massiliensis and Megasphaera sp900066485 may be risk factors for type 2 diabetes; Enterococcus seemed to act as a protective factor for type 2 diabetes. The genus Phocea is associated with energy metabolism and insulin sensitivity, potentially exacerbating metabolic disorders in diabetic patients. [ 34 ] Megasphaera possesses urocanate reductases necessary for the production of imidazolepropionic acid, a microbial metabolite derived from the catabolism of histidine, which is associated with the pathogenesis of impaired glucose metabolism and insulin resistance. [ 35 ] Enterococcus has a mixed influence on disease; it can be a risk factor or protective agent, depending on the strain. [ 36 – 38 ] Further studies are needed to elucidate these relationships. Moreover, lactate levels, the cholesteryl ester-to-total lipid ratio in very small VLDLs, and the ratio of omega-3 fatty acids to total fatty acids were found to be risk factors for type 2 diabetes, whereas the phospholipid-to-total lipid ratio in chylomicrons and extremely large VLDLs was a protective factor, in the present study. Hyperlactatemia may contribute to the development of diabetes by inducing insulin resistance, or it could be a consequence of the diabetic state. [ 39 , 40 ] The activity of cholesterol ester transfer proteins, which are associated with insulin resistance and atherosclerosis, may explain the link between the cholesteryl ester content in VLDL and diabetes risk. [ 41 ] The role of omega-3 fatty acids in diabetes risk remains unclear, as epidemiological evidence is mixed. [ 42 ] The role of phospholipids in enhancing lipid metabolism can hasten the breakdown of free fatty acids in peripheral blood, resulting in a decrease in their levels. [ 43 ] Therefore, an increase in the phospholipid content of chylomicrons and VLDL may help prevent metabolic diseases such as obesity and diabetes. Of course, further research is also needed in this respect.

MR analysis revealed that Prevotella sp002933775, Eisenbergiella sp900066775, and Erysipelatoclostridiaceae were protective against stroke, whereas Klebsiella A was a risk factor. The absence of Prevotella can lead to cerebral artery atherosclerosis and ischemic stroke due to altered lipid metabolism. [ 44 , 45 ] Gut dysbiosis with reduced Prevotella also elevates the risk of intracerebral hemorrhage and stroke-associated pneumonia. [ 46 ] Eisenbergiella may help delay the progression of stroke, potentially through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects via the production of short-chain fatty acids. [ 47 ] However, a previous MR study linked Eisenbergiella with an increased risk of hemorrhagic stroke, necessitating further research to clarify this association. [ 48 ] Species belonging to the Erysipelatoclostridiaceae family, such as Erysipelatoclostridium ramosum , have been associated with systemic infections and systemic inflammatory response syndrome. [ 49 ] More studies are needed to understand their potential association with stroke. Klebsiella A, a significant pathogen, might increase stroke risk by inducing vascular damage and thrombosis via endotoxins and inflammation. [ 50 ] Furthermore, 22:6 docosahexaenoic acid and omega-3 fatty acids were identified as risk factors for stroke, whereas the free cholesterol-to-total lipid ratio in small HDL, phospholipids in medium HDL, and the concentration of medium HDL particles were protective. A Cochrane review of 86 randomized clinical trials revealed no impact of omega-3 supplements on stroke incidence but noted an increased risk in those with preexisting cardiovascular diseases. [ 51 ] Further research is needed given these inconsistencies. The evidence suggests a relationship between medium HDL concentrations and stroke risk, but the role of the free cholesterol-to-total lipid ratio in small HDL-C or phospholipids in medium HDL-C in stroke patients remains unclear. Several MR studies have confirmed a causal link between medium HDL and stroke. [ 52 , 53 ] The protective function of HDL in atherogenesis involves facilitating reverse cholesterol transport from macrophages. [ 54 ] Phospholipids in HDL contribute to its anti-inflammatory properties, potentially explaining our findings. [ 55 ]

In our study, Faecalibacterium sp002160895, Demequina, and Prevotella strains emerged as potential risk factors for heart failure. Faecalibacterium is known to produce butyrate, which is beneficial for mitigating heart failure by modulating the immune response. [ 56 ] However, our results show a somewhat contradictory situation. Further investigations are necessary to clarify these conflicting results. The role of Demequina and Prevotella in heart failure also needs elucidation because of limited available data. MR analysis revealed that serum acetate is a risk factor for heart failure and potentially influences cell injury by affecting cellular metabolism and oxidative stress. [ 57 ] However, supplementation with acetate in a high-fiber diet has been shown to mitigate the development of hypertension, cardiac hypertrophy, and cardiorenal fibrosis in mice, suggesting its complex role. [ 58 ] A higher triglyceride-to-total lipid ratio in chylomicrons and VLDLs seems protective, likely by transporting triglycerides to cardiomyocytes and harnessing energy through oxidative phosphorylation. [ 59 ] However, further studies are needed to explore these relationships and potential therapeutic applications.

In the present study, Atopobiaceae and Merdibacter massiliensis may be risk factors for hypertension. Most Atopobiaceae bacteria can produce beneficial lactate and short-chain fatty acids. [ 60 ] Despite these probiotic potentials, Atopobiaceae have not yet been applied practically owing to insufficient genetic and enzymatic profiling of their probiotic properties. At present, the relationship between Merdibacter massiliensis and hypertension has not been fully confirmed. Moreover, we revealed that elevated ratios of phospholipids to total lipids in VLDL and diacylglycerol to triglycerides increase the risk of hypertension. Notably, higher concentrations of VLDL particles and lower concentrations of HDL particles, as well as cholesterol, phospholipids, and total lipids within lipoproteins, were associated with an increased risk of hypertension in a UK cohort. [ 61 ] A lipidomic study revealed that diacylglycerol is associated with an increased risk of hypertension. [ 62 ] Nevertheless, additional research is needed to elucidate the mechanism of these associations.

This study explored the bidirectional causal effects of cardiometabolic diseases on the gut microbiota and metabolites. Our results indicate that type 2 diabetes has bidirectional causal effects on lactate levels, the cholesteryl ester-to-total lipid ratio in very small VLDLs, and the phospholipid-to-total lipid ratio in chylomicrons and extremely large VLDLs. Stroke has bidirectional causal effects on GCA-900199385 sp900320755, Intestinimonas massiliensis, and Megasphaera sp900066485. Studies have demonstrated that individuals with diabetes often exhibit hyperlactatemia associated with a diminished ability to perform aerobic oxidation and hindered lactate transfer processes. [ 39 ] The insufficiency of insulin secretion or insulin action present in diabetes mellitus induces derangement in lipid metabolism. [ 63 ] Furthermore, patients with stroke often exhibit intestinal microbiota disturbances manifested by an increase in opportunistic pathogens and a decrease in beneficial bacteria. [ 64 ]

Microbiota-derived metabolites are key mediators of host–microbe interactions. In the context of cardiometabolic diseases, changes in circulating metabolites reflect perturbations in the gut microbiota composition and may play a role in promoting or inhibiting disease progression. Our MR study revealed that 13 distinct circulating metabolites were significantly associated with the risk of cardiometabolic diseases, among which 4 were shown to be causally affected by the gut microbiota. We therefore speculate that these 4 circulating metabolites, including lactate levels, the ratio of omega-3 fatty acids to total fatty acids, the triglyceride-to-total lipid ratio in chylomicrons and extremely large VLDLs, and the phospholipid-to-total lipid ratio in very large VLDLs, may mediate the interaction between the gut microbiota and cardiometabolic diseases. Nevertheless, future studies are needed to further explore the precise mechanisms involved.

Our study has several advantages. First, we systematically assessed the causal role of the gut microbiota and circulating metabolites in the development of coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes, stroke, heart failure, and hypertension. Second, we utilized the latest data on the gut microbiota and circulating metabolites, enhancing the comprehensiveness and cutting-edge nature of our research. Third, we used two-sample MR methodology, which provides reliable causal associations by minimizing confounding factors and avoiding reverse causality. Fourth, the large sample size of the GWAS summary data offered statistical validity for assessing causality. Finally, the robustness and reliability of these results were further confirmed via sensitivity analysis.

However, there were also some limitations to our study. First, our study is mainly based on Europeans, limiting its generalizability to other racial populations. Future research should prioritize multi-ethnic cohorts to confirm the universality of these causal relationships. Second, we chose SNPs linked to the gut microbiota and circulating metabolites on the basis of a loose cutoff of P < 1 × 10 –5 , which exceeded the genome-wide thresholds for significance ( P < 5 × 10 –8 ). This P value threshold was used to enable a sufficient number of SNPs for our MR analysis. The use of a relaxed SNP selection threshold ( P < 1 × 10– 5 ) may increase the risk of false positives. However, sensitivity analyses (MR-Egger, MR-PRESSO) revealed no significant horizontal pleiotropy, supporting the robustness of our primary findings. Third, most of the results did not reach statistical significance after adjustment of the P value threshold for multiple testing via the Bonferroni correction. Although Bonferroni correction is a common method for multiple independent tests for 1 hypothesis, it may cause false-negative results. [ 65 ] MR analysis is hypothesis-driven and can still reveal associations supported by physiological evidence in epidemiology even if the P values do not meet the Bonferroni correction threshold. [ 66 ] For instance, the anaerobic metabolite lactate demonstrates consistent associations across cardiometabolic diseases including coronary artery disease and type 2 diabetes, aligning with its established role in insulin resistance. [ 33 , 39 ] Although the utilization of uncorrected nominal significance thresholds allows for a more thorough exploration of potential associations, we have to acknowledge the need for careful interpretation of results and the potential of false positives. Therefore, further validation and replication in future research are required.


## 5. Conclusion

In our study, we comprehensively examined the causal relationships among the gut microbiota, circulating metabolites, and cardiometabolic diseases. Our study provides genetic evidence that alterations in the intestinal microbiota could be important risk factors for the progression of cardiometabolic diseases, which are likely mediated by several circulating metabolites. To corroborate these findings, longitudinal investigations or trials can be conducted, which could facilitate the development of more precise probiotics for the prevention of cardiometabolic diseases.
