## Abstract

Gradual genome degradation and fragmentation in primary nutritional endosymbionts have required symbiont-dependent hosts periodically to replace such symbionts over evolutionary timescales, yet the processes involved in de novo emergence of endosymbiosis and symbiont replacement are challenging to ascertain. Here we show that phylogenetic relationships of two ancient vertically-transmitted bacterial endosymbionts of cicadas, Hodgkinia and Karelsulcia , mirror host phylogeny, particularly indicating a single ancestral infection of cicadas by Hodgkinia with subsequent host–symbiont codiversification before being replaced by yeast-like fungal symbionts (YLS). We demonstrate a case of co-existence of Hodgkinia with Karelsulcia and a YLS, representing an advanced ongoing symbiont replacement process. In some individuals of the cicada Chremistica ochracea , the Hodgkinia is highly degenerated but colonizes (instead of neighboring) its partner Karelsulcia . The physical fusion of these two bacterial endosymbionts yields a nested symbiosis while the new YLS is recruited, probably preserving essential metabolic pathways necessary for host nutrition and facilitating continued vertical symbiont transmission. Such fusion may have provided refuge for the degrading bacterial endosymbiont and delayed symbiont replacement. Our study sheds light on adaptive and non-adaptive evolutionary mechanisms involved in symbiont loss and replacement, offering fresh insights into endosymbiotic origins of cellular organelles.

## Introduction

Across the tree of life, many animal lineages have formed symbiotic associations with a wide variety of microbes. Endosymbioses have profoundly impacted evolution and continue to shape the ecology of a wide range of organisms 1 , 2 . Organelles such as mitochondria (originating from proteobacteria) and chloroplasts (derived from cyanobacteria) are considered relics of ancient bacterial symbionts that established endosymbioses in early eukaryotic cells 3 , 4 . Despite the many known examples of endosymbioses across the tree of life, their de novo emergence is rare and challenging to uncover in retrospect 5 – 7 . Among insects, which represent the most diverse lineage of animals, microbial symbionts have played key roles in the adaptation of their hosts to a variety of ecological niches and the exploitation of diverse food resources 8 – 12 . In many cases, particularly in sap-feeding Auchenorrhyncha (Hemiptera), the host insects have evolved specialized cells and organs called bacteriocytes and bacteriomes, respectively, to accommodate their bacterial endosymbionts 8 , 13 , 14 . Other symbionts may reside in the fat bodies adjacent to the bacteriomes or directly within the host’s hemocoel 9 . These symbionts may migrate to developing oocytes in the female body, thereby ensuring vertical symbiont transmission over generations 15 – 18 . Such obligate, vertically transmitted symbionts provide their insect hosts with essential nutrients that the insects cannot produce themselves and which are unavailable in their nutritionally poor and imbalanced plant-sap diets 8 – 11 . Stable vertical transmission has led to strict co-diversification of hosts and their obligate symbionts, resulting in the phylogenetic relationships of symbionts mirroring the host phylogeny 19 – 22 .

Gradual genome degradation often occurs, over evolutionary timescales, when microbes transition from parasitism to symbiosis. In the initial phase of association with the host cells, the genome of symbionts undergoes a tumultuous period of gene inactivation, genomic rearrangement, and genome size reduction 23 – 25 . Upon the establishment of a stable symbiotic relationship, the endosymbionts’ genomic evolution often transitions to a prolonged period of stasis 26 , 27 . However, over the longer term, some symbiont genomes have become highly reduced in size and have gradually lost metabolic functions 25 , 27 . This is driven by two main factors: relaxed natural selection acting on many symbiont genes which are unnecessary for the symbiotic lifestyle, and the accumulation of deleterious mutations due to genetic drift facilitated by strong population bottlenecks and a paucity of horizontal gene acquisition inherent in the obligate intrahost lifestyle 25 , 28 . This process achieves adaptation to the host insects by discarding non-essential genes and retaining only the essential genes required for their symbiotic functions, yet it also drives symbionts toward irreversible dependence 23 , 27 .

Approximately 340 million years ago (Ma), the ancestor of the plant sap-feeding auchenorrhynchan insects of the order Hemiptera forged an intimate symbiosis with two key bacterial partners 9 , 21 , 29 : an obligate endosymbiont of the phylum Bacteroidota, i.e., “ Candidatus Karelsulcia muelleri” (hereafter referred to as Karelsulcia , formerly named Sulcia ), and a co-obligate endosymbiont of the phylum Betaproteobacteria. These two symbionts collectively synthesize 10 essential amino acids (EAAs) and some vitamins for their host insects 30 – 32 . The genome of Karelsulcia is highly conserved, while the genomes of the co-obligate symbionts, e.g., “ Ca . Hodgkinia cicadicola” (hereafter Hodgkinia ) in cicadas, “ Ca . Zinderia insecticola” in froghoppers, “ Ca . Nasuia deltocephalinicola” in leafhoppers and “ Ca . Vidania fulgoroidea” in planthoppers, are susceptible to degradation 31 , 33 – 35 . Previous studies have shown that Hodgkinia harbored in certain cicada species has evolved into two or more complex cytologically distinct but metabolically interdependent cellular lineages with even more reduced but functionally complementary genomes 16 , 17 , 36 , 37 . In extreme cases, the genome of Hodgkinia fragments into an array of dozens of minicircles, with each minicircle possessing only a handful of genes, potentially triggering a critical phase of genomic instability 38 – 40 . Such degradation and fragmentation of symbiont lineages may present an intergenerational transmission problem for the host insects 17 , 41 .

Symbiont replacement may represent a strategy for hosts to break free from reliance on an ancient, potentially inefficient and degraded symbiont 42 , 43 . Examples of symbiont replacement and supplementation are widespread in insects, and are well documented in the sap-feeding auchenorrhynchans 19 , 42 , 44 – 46 , psyllids 47 , aphids 48 , adelgids 49 and weevils 50 . In a few auchenorrhynchan insects, the bacteriome-associated symbionts have been partially or entirely lost and apparently replaced subsequently by yeast-like fungal symbionts (YLS) of the genus Ophiocordyceps 19 , 20 , 51 , 52 . In cicadas that are associated with Karelsulcia and Hodgkinia , Karelsulcia and Hodgkinia cells are harbored in the peripheral bacteriocytes and central bacteriocytes of bacteriomes, respectively 17 , 19 , 51 . Unlike the primary bacterial endosymbionts which reside in bacteriomes, YLS usually colonize the fat bodies of their host insects, except in a few Hodgkinia -free cicada species where YLS also colonize the bacteriomes 17 , 19 , 51 . This indicates the relationships and co-evolution among related endosymbionts and Cicadidae are highly complex. However, the extent to which a symbiont can persist in the host despite degradation and fragmentation of the symbiont genome; the ultimate fate of the ancient, genome-degraded symbiont; and the evolutionary processes and mechanisms that precede symbiont replacement remain enduring puzzles.

To shed light on such questions, we reconstruct patterns and consequences of bacteriome- and fat-body-associated obligate endosymbiont loss, as well as concordant gains of YLS in 133 cicada species (see Supplementary Fig. 1 , Supplementary Tables 1 , 5 – 11 for details) using microscopy, comparative genomics and cophylogenetic analyses. We document the physical fusion of two co-primary bacterial endosymbionts, yielding a nested symbiosis that probably preserves essential metabolic pathways necessary for host nutrition and facilitates continued vertical symbiont transmission. Such fusion may have provided refuge for the highly genome-degraded bacterial endosymbiont and delayed the process of this endosymbiont being replaced by YLS. Moreover, phylogenetic analysis reveals that such fungal endosymbionts all belong to the genus Ophiocordyceps , in which cicada-associated YLS form a monophyletic clade, and that free-living Ophiocordyceps fungi tend to have more introns than YLS. The results illuminate a very late ongoing stage in the symbiont replacement, the role of symbiotic associations in driving mitogenome intron loss during evolutionary adaptation, and the complexity of co-evolution between symbionts and Cicadidae. These findings offer fresh insights into the processes underlying symbiont replacement and the endosymbiotic origins of cellular organelles.


## Results


## Co-evolution of cicadas and Karelsulcia

We newly sequenced the genomes of 17 representative cicada species (Supplementary Table 6 ) and the Karelsulcia genomes of 23 representative cicada species (Supplementary Table 7 ) from three subfamilies, and integrated them with previous data (Supplementary Tables 6 , 7 ) to conduct a comprehensive co-phylogenetic analysis of cicadas and their symbionts (Fig. 1 ). The phylogeny of the host cicadas is highly congruent with that of Karelsulcia . Tacuini is strongly supported as sister to the rest of Cicadinae in the phylogeny based on universal single-copy orthologues (USCOs) of cicadas (Fig. 1a , Supplementary Fig. 2a ) and on the phylogenetic relationships of Karelsulcia of cicadas without YLS but with Hodgkinia (Supplementary Fig. 2b ). Platypleurini is supported as sister to the rest of Cicadinae in the phylogeny of Karelsulcia of cicadas associated with YLS, but branch support is low in the Bayesian phylogeny (Fig. 1b ); thus, evidence for incongruence with the host phylogeny is weak. In addition, both the phylogenetic relationships of cicadas and those of Karelsulcia demonstrate that the tribe Leptopsaltriini is not monophyletic, requiring further taxonomic subdivision (Fig. 1 ). The results overall suggest that analysis of Karelsulcia phylogeny may contribute to resolution of contentious aspects of Cicadidae phylogeny, and the few apparent areas of incongruence suggest that Hodgkinia has been lost and replaced by YLS repeatedly in multiple independent cicada lineages (see below). ## Co-diversification of Hodgkinia with host cicadas

We BLASTed two Hodgkinia genomes (i.e., strains CHOCRA [ CP024987 , 148,642 bp] and Dsem [ CP001226 , 143,795 bp]) of the cicadas Chonosia crassipernis and Diceroprocta semicincta using NCBI’s Web BLAST blastn (optimized for somewhat similar sequences) with default parameters, including an expect threshold of 0.05, word size of 11, gap costs (existence: 5, extension: 2), and low complexity region filtering enabled. These two genomes do not exhibit genome splitting, yielding the following BLAST results: 64 matches, 13% coverage, E-value 0.0, and 81.34% identity (see Supplementary Data 1 for details). These two Hodgkinia genomes encode almost identical protein-coding genes, yet they exhibit relatively low similarity at the protein level. Even in BLAST comparisons of Hodgkinia genomes in closely related host cicada genera (e.g., strains CHOCRA [ CP024987 , 148,642 bp] and TETULN [ CP008699 , 150,297 bp]), the similarity also remained low: 73 matches, 76% coverage, E-value 0.0, and 86.51% identity (see Supplementary Data 1 for details). The divergence in nucleotide and protein sequences despite conserved gene content suggests a remarkably rapid evolutionary rate in Hodgkinia genome. Phylogenetic analysis of Hodgkinia based on 16S rRNA gene sequences indicates that the Hodgkinia of Cicadinae and Cicadettinae are not monophyletic (Supplementary Fig. 1 ), suggesting that Hodgkinia might have independently infected the ancestors of different cicada lineages on multiple occasions. However, the 16S rRNA phylogenies of Hodgkinia within the same cicada tribe form a monophyletic group, except for Chremistica ochracea , Katoa paucispina and Kosemia admirabilis (Supplementary Fig. 1 ). This apparent incongruence could also have resulted from genome splitting (which is similar to that observed after whole-genome duplication) in Hodgkinia that led to the formation of multi-copy genes, with certain cicadas retaining only the paralogs inherited from their common ancestor during subsequent evolution.

Hodgkinia is present in all investigated species of Platypleurini, and the Hodgkinia 16S rRNA gene has multiple copies in this tribe, but another gene in the same group, RNA polymerase subunit B ( rpoB ) gene, is found to retain only one copy in the assembled genomic data. Owing to excessive sequence divergence in certain regions of the full-length rpoB gene, the phylogenetic signal of its full-length sequences is compromised. Therefore, following previous studies, we analyzed the rpoB gene of more Hodgkinia strains and identified a 342-bp relatively conserved region for phylogenetic analysis. Phylogenetic analysis of this gene indicates that the Hodgkinia of cicadas from different subfamilies are monophyletic (Fig. 2a ), and their phylogeny is highly consistent with that of the host cicadas (Figs. 1 a and 2b ). Clades A–C of Hodgkinia , containing different rpoB genotypes from both host specimens within a species group, must have existed in the last common ancestor of that species group (Fig. 2a ). For example, Hodgkinia has undergone four splits in the most recent common ancestor of Chr. nigra and Chr. maculata , resulting in five clades that include at least one lineage from each of these two species (Fig. 2a, b ). A heatmap of genetic distances among rpoB genotypes reveals that the Hodgkinia rpoB genotypes, particularly the isolate EOPrepa, in cicadas of the tribe Platypleurini are more distinct from other genotypes in related cicada taxa (Fig. 2c ). These results indicate a single ancestral infection of cicadas by Hodgkinia , i.e., Hodgkinia infected the common ancestor of cicadas and co-diversified with host cicadas before being replaced by YLS. ## Origin of YLS and co-phylogeny of host cicadas and their YLS

In the present study, we obtained mitogenomes from 20 cicada-associated fungal endosymbionts and one fungal endosymbiont of the treehopper Darthula hardwickii . The genome sizes ranged from 19 kb to 144 kb, with size variation strongly correlated with the presence/absence and number of introns (Supplementary Table 8 ). The fungal symbionts of Chr. ochracea (19,249 bp) and Tibeta zenobia (23,757 bp) exhibited the smallest mitogenomes, both completely lacking introns in their protein-coding genes (PCGs), while the fungal symbiont of Dundubia terpsichore (25,794 bp) contained a single intron (Group IC2, 507 bp) interrupting the gene nad4 (Fig. 3 , Supplementary Table 8 ). The largest mitogenome (144,177 bp), with 45 and 10 introns in the PCGs and the ribosomal RNA genes (rRNAs), respectively, was identified in the fungal symbiont of Distantalna splendida , and its cox1 spans 29,326 nucleotide sites, containing 13 introns with a total exonic region of only 1584 bp (Fig. 3 , Supplementary Table 8 ). Notably, free-living Ophiocordyceps fungi tend to have more introns than YLS (Fig. 3 ). Another notable finding is that the complete mitogenome of Chr. ochracea ’s YLS contains only 17 transfer RNAs (tRNAs), lacking tRNAs for isoleucine (Ile) and tryptophan (Trp), whereas other complete YLS mitogenomes include at least 24 tRNAs, which have tRNAs for transporting 10 EAAs (Supplementary Table 8 ). Despite significant differences in mitogenome size and intron number, all YLS contained two rRNAs ( rnl , large ribosomal RNA gene, and rns , small ribosomal RNA gene), 14 standard PCGs ( atp6 , atp8 , atp9 , cob , cox1 – 3 , nad1 – 6 and nad4L ), and these 16 genes shared a consistent order and were all located on the sense strand (Fig. 3 , Supplementary Table 8 ). Phylogenetic analysis revealed that all these fungal symbionts belong to the genus Ophiocordyceps (Ascomycota: Hypocreales: Ophiocordycipitaceae), with cicada-associated YLS forming a monophyletic clade and the treehopper-associated YLS clustering in a distinct branch with other Ophiocordyceps lineages (Fig. 3 ). Phylogenetic comparison of the host cicadas and their YLS as well as allied parasitic fungi shows that the phylogeny of the host cicadas is incongruent with that of YLS (Supplementary Fig. 3 ), reflecting a complex and dynamic evolutionary history involving repeated acquisitions, losses, and replacements. Phylogenetic analyses of YLS show the YLS are close relatives of some known fungal parasites of cicadas (including O. longissima , O. yakusimensis , and O. sobolifera ) (Fig. 3 , Supplementary Fig. 3 ). The phylogenetic relationships of some YLS are locally concordant with those of host cicada lineages (Fig. 3 , Supplementary Fig. 3 ), such as Mogannia spp., Cryptotympana spp., Pomponia spp., Dundubia spp., Haphsa spp., Sonatini ( Hyalessa spp.), Polyneurini (except Formotosena spp.), Tanna spp. (except Tan. sinensis ), and Gaeanini (except Sulphogaeana spp.). This demonstrates a certain degree of host specificity, stable vertical transmission of YLS and their co-diversification with host cicadas at relatively recent timescales.

Based on the presence or absence of Hodgkinia and YLS in related host cicadas (Supplementary Fig. 3a ) and the phylogenetic relationships of YLS (Supplementary Fig. 3b ), our results show eight independent loss events of Hodgkinia , nine acquisition events of YLS and at least 17 replacement events of one YLS by another in the sampled cicada species (Supplementary Fig. 3a ). It is noteworthy that the YLS of the ancestor of the tribe Polyneurini was replaced by another YLS, and that the YLS of the two cicada species Formotosena seebohmi and Formotosena sp. 2 within the tribe were further replaced by another YLS (Supplementary Fig. 3 ).


## Cicadas associated with different facultative symbionts

Phylogenetic analyses of related facultative symbionts indicate that Arsenophonus , which are vertically transmitted within the cicada tribe Platypleurini, have been independently acquired at least four times (Supplementary Fig. 4a ). These analyses also show that the Rickettsia of Suisha formosana belongs to the spotted fever group (Supplementary Fig. 4b ); the “ Ca . Tisiphia” (hereafter Tisiphia ) of Ambragaeana ambra and Vittagaeana paviei belong to the Torix group (Supplementary Fig. 4b ); and the Wolbachia of seven cicada species belong to three different groups (A, B, and F; Supplementary Fig. 4b ).


## Endosymbionts collectively providing EAAs and B vitamins for their host cicadas

Karelsulcia genomes assembled from our metagenomic data comprise all the genes required for encoding the synthesis of eight EAAs for the host insects, except for dapE (Fig. 4a , b). Karelsulcia genomes of certain cicada species have undergone a notable reduction, as exemplified by Scieroptera splendidula with a genome size of only 218,754 bp, and some genes involved in the synthesis of EAAs having been lost or pseudogenized (Fig. 4a ). The genomes of Hodgkinia contain genes for synthesis of methionine and histidine (Fig. 4a, b ). Additionally, we have annotated genes related to the synthesis pathways of cobalamin, folate and riboflavin in the genomes of Hodgkinia . Correct structures of the YLS genomes cannot be inferred from metagenomic data due to sequence fragmentation and/or low coverage. Unfortunately, our attempts to cultivate the YLS harbored in Chr. ochracea and some other cicadas have not been successful, so the specific nutritional contributions of these YLS strains to their host cicadas remain unclear. Their potential functions are discussed in the Discussion section. Nevertheless, we reveal here that facultative endosymbionts of cicadas, including Arsenophonus and Wolbachia , possess genes encoding the synthesis of four B vitamins (biotin, folate, pyridoxine and riboflavin) and some EAAs (Fig. 4a, c ), and that Tisiphia and Rickettsia possess genes encoding the synthesis of biotin (Fig. 4a ). It is remarkable that these facultative symbionts possess the dapE gene, which is absent in the Karelsulcia genome and is essential for the synthesis of lysine (Fig. 4a, b ). In comparison to some other horizontally-acquired symbionts such as Wolbachia , Tisiphia and Rickettsia , Arsenophonus harbored in cicadas is vertically transmitted along with Karelsulcia and Hodgkinia and contains a greater number of genes involved in the biosynthesis of B vitamins (Fig. 4a , Supplementary Fig. 5 ). This suggests that the host’s reliance on the nutritional metabolism of vertically transmitted facultative symbionts is more pronounced. Wolbachia and Tisiphia of Vi. paviei are horizontally transmitted, and their B vitamin-synthesizing genes are redundant (Fig. 4a ).


## Novel symbiont distribution and a novel nested symbiosis revealed in cicadas

Four main distribution patterns of obligate symbionts in Cicadidae are corroborated through the newly revealed distribution of Karelsulcia , Hodgkinia and YLS across different cicada species in this study, as depicted in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. 6 – 10 . Pattern 1: in YLS-free cicadas, Karelsulcia and Hodgkinia are separately harbored in the peripheral and central bacteriocytes (both of which are situated within the bacteriome sheath cells) of bacteriomes, with no obligate symbionts harbored in the fat bodies (Fig. 5a–d , Supplementary Figs. 6a –d, 7a –l, 9a –d, 10a, b ). Pattern 2: in Hodgkinia -free cicadas, Karelsulcia is harbored in the bacteriocytes of bacteriomes, and YLS in the well-developed bacteriome sheath cells (which in most cases consist of a single layer of cells that form the bacteriome sheath to encapsulate bacteriocytes and do not harbor symbionts), with no obligate symbionts harbored in the fat bodies (Fig. 5e–h ). Pattern 3: in Hodgkinia -free cicadas, Karelsulcia is harbored in the bacteriocytes of bacteriomes, and YLS in both the bacteriome sheath cells and the bacteriocytes of fat bodies (Fig. 5i–l ). Pattern 4: in Hodgkinia -free cicadas, Karelsulcia is harbored in the bacteriocytes of bacteriomes, and YLS in the bacteriocytes of fat bodies (Fig. 5m–t , Supplementary Figs. 6e –x, 7m –x, 8 ). ## Discussion

The present tissue distributions and genomic organizations of the endosymbionts represent only snapshots in the evolutionary history of such associations. The evolutionary process of endosymbiont replacements in Cicadidae is seemingly much more complex and dynamic than observed so far in other insects. This may reflect the unusual lifestyle of cicadas, with juveniles (nymphs) feeding underground on root xylem fluids for multiple years, but adults usually active aboveground for only a few weeks 54 . Thus, juvenile cicadas are constantly exposed to microbe-rich soil, which may facilitate repeated infection by fungi and other soil-borne microorganisms 19 , 20 , 55 . Based on our present study, we attempt to further reconstruct the evolutionary scenarios of Karelsulcia , Hodgkinia and YLS with their host insects here.


## Symbioses at a crossroads: bacterial symbiont decline and fungal symbiont rise

Considerable diversity has been observed in the genomes of Hodgkinia among different cicada species. The single Hodgkinia genome has undergone extreme reduction (<0.15 Mb), and the average guanine + cytosine (GC) content of Hodgkinia assemblies from different host cicadas varies considerably, ranging from 28% to 58% on average 36 , 38 , 40 . Moreover, there is a coding reassignment phenomenon, in which UGA is recoded from stop to tryptophan in the Hodgkinia genome 56 . Previous studies have shown that the genomic evolution process (e.g., the genome splitting and degradation) of Hodgkinia has no adaptive significance in periodical cicadas 38 , 39 . We revealed that the Hodgkinia detected in Chr. ochracea forms a clade with the Hodgkinia of the cicada genus Tettigetta (Supplementary Fig. 1 ), which also exhibit severe genome fragmentation 17 , despite the two host cicadas being phylogenetically distant. In the genomes of Hodgkinia and those of other severely reduced symbionts, genes involved in synthesizing lipopolysaccharide, peptidoglycan, phospholipids and other components of the bacterial cell envelope are almost completely lost 57 – 59 . These results suggest that the Hodgkinia in Chr. ochracea exhibits very bizarre evolution, and the colonization of Karelsulcia ’s cytoplasm by Hodgkinia may represent a very late stage in the symbiont replacement process. Because the process we document is occurring at the population level within an individual cicada species, we can make a strong argument that it represents an ongoing endosymbiont replacement case that has been discovered and confirmed to date in cicadas. Moreover, the highly degraded genome of Hodgkinia in Chr. ochracea has proven challenging to assemble most probably due to its high level of degeneration and fragmentation. The exact role of this symbiont in provisioning its host with nutrients remains unclear and will need to be elucidated through implementation of more advanced sequencing or assembly methods than those currently available to us.

The vast majority of Karelsulcia genomes comprise a single circular chromosome and exhibit conserved collinearity with one another 19 , 32 , 52 , 60 . Among the 21 complete Karelsulcia genomes analyzed in this study, large segmental inversions were identified in the Karelsulcia genomes of both Chr. ochracea (spanning 104,250–188,931 bp) and Kat. taibaiensis (spanning 70,473–241,592 bp) (Supplementary Fig. 12a ). Notably, the Karelsulcia genomes of Auritibicen and Cryptotympana exhibited inversions in regions homologous to those identified in Chr. ochracea ’s Karelsulcia genome 19 , suggesting a single ancestral large genomic inversion event within the Karelsulcia of tribe Tacuini. Intriguingly, a recent investigation revealed that the Karelsulcia genome of the planthopper Trypetimorpha occidentalis comprises two circular chromosomes, both exhibiting extensive rearrangements compared to other characterized genomes 60 . However, despite the high sequencing coverage for our samples, the Karelsulcia genomes of Chr. maculata and Chr. nigra assembled here remain fragmented, with recovered contigs lacking circularity. Comparative analysis further shows that the rRNAs of these two genomes contain a high density of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) compared to the Karelsulcia ’s rRNAs of Chr. ochracea , indicating that their genes are undergoing active rearrangement (Supplementary Fig. 12b–d ). These findings suggest that the Karelsulcia symbionts associated with Chr. maculata and Chr. nigra may have entered a new phase of genomic instability compared to Karelsulcia in other cicadas. Future investigations using long-read sequencing and comprehensive genomic analyses of Karelsulcia within Chremistica may further elucidate these intriguing genomic mysteries.

The ability of Karelsulcia to produce EAAs varies across major lineages of Auchenorrhyncha, e.g., Karelsulcia symbionts synthesize eight EAAs in cicadas, leafhoppers and treehoppers, seven in spittlebugs, and only three in planthoppers, with the latter exhibiting the smallest genome size (<0.16 Mb) 30 , 34 , 35 . Notably, a recent study revealed that the Karelsulcia symbiont of the treehopper Da. hardwickii has lost the ability to provide the EAA Trp for its host, retaining functionality for only seven EAAs 52 . Additionally, Karelsulcia has been completely replaced by fungal symbionts in numerous planthopper lineages 45 , 46 . These findings suggest an evolutionary transition in host-microbe symbiosis involving shifts from bacterial to fungal partnerships across these insect lineages.

Metabolically interdependent symbionts that exchange metabolites are usually housed within the same organ, whereas symbionts that do not share biosynthetic pathways are typically localized in different organs 61 . The synthesis of methionine necessitates the cooperation of Karelsulcia and Hodgkinia (Fig. 4b ), but the highly degenerated Hodgkinia genome may have lost related genes 39 , suggesting cicadas initially recruited YLS as a compensatory measure to offset the gradual loss of Hodgkinia gene function 19 . The fragmentation process of Hodgkinia is deleterious, especially at its more advanced stages, and the host cicadas may face growing disadvantages compared to ecologically similar organisms that have more efficient symbioses 37 – 39 . Recruitment of an Ophiocordyceps fungus as a symbiont may have served to alleviate the dependency of host cicadas on Hodgkinia although, as we show here, Hodgkinia cells with genes retaining functional parts (metabolic pathways) may merge with Karelsulcia cells, forming a nested symbiosis that may preserve such pathways despite the presence of newly recruited YLS.

To date, only the YLS of the cicada Meimuna opalifera has been successfully cultivated 19 . In their pioneering work, Matsuura et al. sequenced and assembled the pure-cultured YLS of Mei. opalifera , yielding a draft genome of 25.1 Mb assembled into 31 contigs, along with a single mitogenome contig spanning 170 kb (accession number: PNEP01) 19 . Genomic analysis of this fungal symbiont has unveiled its remarkable metabolic adaptability, with the potential to synthesize a comprehensive range of amino acids, vitamins, and other essential metabolites 19 . However, examination of the 170 kb mitogenome revealed an assembly error: a 40 kb sequence was duplicated. After removing the duplicated region, its size (129,780 bp) exactly matches the assembly obtained in our present study. Intriguingly, the mitogenome of Mei. opalifera ’ YLS retains more introns than those of other YLS except for those associated with Maua quadrituberculata and Dis. splendida . Co-phylogenetic analysis indicates that the YLS of these three cicada species were recently acquired through the replacement of one fungal symbiont by another (Fig. 7 , Supplementary Fig. 3 ). The YLS of Mei. opalifera is culturable 19 , so the culturability of the YLS from the other two cicada species merits further investigation. In our present study we revealed that the genome sizes of YLS and related Ophiocordyceps fungi are strongly correlated with the presence/absence and number of introns (i.e., free-living Ophiocordyceps fungi tend to have more introns than YLS). Our novel findings suggest that symbiotic associations drive intron loss in fungal mitogenomes during evolutionary adaptation, while intron abundance and genome size are strongly linked to fungal lifestyle transitions between free-living states and symbiotic dependency.


## Nested symbiosis in insects

Previous research on microbial nested symbiosis in insects has mainly focused on the Tremblaya – Moranella symbiotic system within mealybugs 62 – 66 . Tremblaya ’s genome is highly reduced (138,927 bp), but its cytoplasm contains another symbiont, Moranella , which has a much larger genome (538,294 bp) comprising more protein-coding genes. These two symbionts are metabolically complementary, collaborating to synthesize 10 EAAs for their hosts 63 . Mealybugs have repeatedly recruited intra- Tremblaya symbionts 65 . However, this system is organized quite differently from the nested symbiosis unraveled in our present study. In some individuals of Chr. ochracea , it is the highly degenerated Hodgkinia that transitioned from coexistence with Karelsulcia in the bacteriomes to nesting inside cells of the latter during recruitment of YLS by the host cicada.

Two alternative hypotheses have been presented to explain why the initial γ-proteobacterial endosymbiont of mealybugs ended up inside Tremblaya 65 . The first is that utilizing the existing transport system between the insect cells and Tremblaya is more feasible than evolving a new system; and the second is that Tremblaya cells are likely not targeted by the insect immune system, making Tremblaya ’s cytoplasm an ideal refuge for a newly arrived symbiont.

In contrast, three alternative explanations for why Hodgkinia colonized the cytoplasm of Karelsulcia in cicadas are proposed here. The first is suggested by adaptative evolution of the vertical transmission of related endosymbionts. An unusual transmission strategy has been adopted by one of the endosymbionts of the planthopper Tryp. occidentalis , in which an Acetobacteraceae facultative endosymbiont inhabits deep invaginations of the cellular membranes of Karelsulcia and this close physical association facilitates transovarial transmission 60 . Fragmentation of Hodgkinia may be compensated for to some extent by an increased number of cells transmitted transovarially, but this does not guarantee all lineages will be transmitted to each egg 17 , 41 . The greater the fragmentation of Hodgkinia , the higher the cost of Hodgkinia transmission to the next generation of host cicadas will be. Thus, colonization of Karelsulcia by Hodgkinia may increase efficiency of the transmission process. Such colonization may also represent an adaptive strategy favoring survival of Hodgkinia following non-adaptive genome degradation.

A second possible explanation for the colonization of Karelsulcia cells by Hodgkinia relates to retention of gene product exchange between symbionts. In extreme cases of endosymbiotic genome reduction, genes that are essential for the synthesis of the cell envelope, including those involved in other fundamental biological processes, are likely to be lost, requiring the symbiont to rely on the host to provide metabolic products for survival 57 – 59 . As Hodgkinia split into distinct cellular lineages in certain host cicadas, the genomes of each newly formed lineage contained at least two genes not present in the other lineage, requiring gene products to be shared between lineages 37 . Given that some EAAs and vitamins are collectively synthesized by two endosymbionts 30 – 32 , 35 , the collaborative synthesis of EAAs (e.g., methionine which has the highest energy cost) between Karelsulcia and Hodgkinia may have been destabilized as Hodgkinia split into distinct cellular lineages 61 . Thus, immigration of Hodgkinia into the cytoplasm of Karelsulcia may facilitate the exchange of gene products while provide Hodgkinia with access the cellular products of Karelsulcia during the gradual decoupling of symbiosis between Hodgkinia and its host cicada.

A third potential explanation is that this novel nutritional symbiotic system may be driven by stochastic processes, such as random mutations and genetic drift. Hodgkinia genome degradation has proceeded down different paths in different Magicicada species, supporting a model of genomic degradation that is stochastic in outcome and likely nonadaptive for the host 39 . In Chremistica species, the 16S rRNA of Hodgkinia in Chr. ochracea differs significantly from that of the other species of the genus, e.g., Chr. maculata and Chr. nigra (Supplementary Fig. 1 ). This suggests that degradation of the Hodgkinia genome in Chr. ochracea may have been driven by stochastic processes, and the genome-fragmented Hodgkinia then colonize the cytoplasm of Karelsulcia to seek refuge. The physical fusion of these two bacterial endosymbionts yields a nested symbiosis—probably preserving essential metabolic pathways necessary for host nutrition and facilitating continued vertical symbiont transmission. Such fusion may have delayed the gradual decoupling of symbiosis between Hodgkinia and its host cicada and the replacement of this endosymbiont by the newly recruited YLS.

In the Tremblaya – Moranella symbiotic system of mealybugs, Moranella within the cytoplasm of Tremblaya provides many metabolic products to Tremblaya 64 and collaborates with the host for peptidoglycan synthesis, thus functioning like an organelle 66 . The possible benefits Hodgkinia provides to Karelsulcia and other collaborative processes between these symbionts and their host cicadas beyond energy metabolism require further study, and this symbiotic system may provide fresh insights into the endosymbiotic origins of cellular organelles.

Additionally, a few operational taxonomic units (OTUs) isolated from the bacteriomes of a minority of individuals of the cicada Subpsaltria yangi were identified to be Hodgkinia in a previous study 67 , in which Karelsulcia was also harbored in the bacteriomes and YLS in the fat bodies 67 . Whether Hodgkinia is distributed, as in the cicada Chr. ochracea , within the cytoplasm of Karelsulcia merits further investigation. Notably, the 16S rRNA sequences of Hodgkinia from certain cicada species (marked with asterisks in Supplementary Fig. 1 ), particularly that of Chr. ochracea (where it is deeply nested within a clade of Tettigetta symbionts), exhibit unusual phylogenetic placement. However, the two cicada species group have different phenological periods and geographical distributions (Supplementary Table 1 ). Moreover, the Hodgkinia 16S rRNA sequence from Chr. ochracea was obtained prior to the collection of Tettigetta samples through plasmid-based cloning experiments (the restriction banding patterns clones and the raw sequencing files of positive clones have been deposited in the OMIX database, China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences ( https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/omix : accession number OMIX009590 )). Transmission electron microscopy revealed the presence of Hodgkinia within the cytoplasm of Karelsulcia cells, and the fluorescent probes to rRNA-targeted sites confirmed the authenticity of this sequence as well as the distribution of Hodgkinia (Fig. 6 ). Thus, this unusual phylogenetic placement might represent a case of convergent evolution of Hodgkinia .


## Evolutionary histories of cicadas and their three obligate endosymbionts within symbiotic organs

Divergence time of Cicadidae lineages and the phylogeny of Cicadidae along with their symbiont distribution patterns, symbiont loss and replacement events, and the apparent evolutionary trend of such patterns are shown in Fig. 7 to provide a more intuitive understanding of the extraordinarily complicated co-evolutionary histories of this insect group and associated microorganisms. With the fossils constraining the applicable crown nodes, the age of the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) of the Cicadidae lineages is estimated to be ~96.38 Ma (95% HPD: 84.33–108.03 Ma) (Fig. 7 ). The origins of Derotettiginae, Tibicininae and Cicadettinae were estimated at ~88.36 Ma (95% HPD: 76.76–100.08 Ma), ~75.44 Ma (95% HPD: 66.81–84.92 Ma), and ~67.11 Ma (95% HPD: 58.61–75.32 Ma), respectively. While the divergence between Tettigomyiinae and Cicadinae occurred at ~63.99 Ma (95% HPD: 55.16–72.12 Ma) (Fig. 7 ). Based on the divergence time estimation of Cicadidae lineages and co-phylogenetic analysis between host cicadas and their YLS, the earliest detectable symbiont replacement event was inferred to have occurred at least 42.22 Ma (95% HPD: 37.11–47.33 Ma) (Fig. 7 ).

The highly conserved Karelsulcia is present in all investigated species of the five subfamilies of Cicadidae, but its partner Hodgkinia is only present in a few lineages, and YLS has replaced Hodgkinia in the remaining lineages except in Chr. ochracea (Fig. 7 ). The ancestor of cicadas apparently harbored Karelsulcia and Hodgkinia independently in different parts of the bacteriomes, i.e., the peripheral and central bacteriocytes, respectively (Category 1 in Fig. 7 ). The genome-degraded Hodgkinia subsequently split into distinct cellular lineages, with many functions being lost in some host cicadas 36 – 40 . Such cicadas then recruited a new symbiont, particularly an Ophiocordyceps fungus residing in the soil that had long been in contact with the subterranean cicada nymphs 19 , 20 . The fungus was initially harbored in the bacteriome sheath cells or the fat bodies, with its pathogenicity decreasing due to purifying selection. The fungus gradually shifted from being parasitic to symbiotic, becoming a YLS. Establishment of the new cicada– Karelsulcia –YLS symbiosis gradually disrupted the original cicada– Karelsulcia – Hodgkinia symbiotic system, but in some cases at least, Hodgkinia persisted by migrating into cytoplasm of Karelsulcia (Category 4 in Fig. 7 ). Eventually, Hodgkinia was completely lost from cicadas that continued to harbor YLS (Category 5 in Fig. 7 ). An alternative evolutionary scenario probably occurred in some cicada lineages, where YLS, originally only harbored in the bacteriomes (Category 2 in Fig. 7 ), partially migrated to the fat bodies (Category 3 in Fig. 7 ). The temporary endpoint of the ancestral co-evolutionary relationship between cicadas, Karelsulcia and Hodgkinia is characterized by the complete disappearance of Hodgkinia , with Karelsulcia being harbored in the bacteriomes and YLS in the fat bodies (Category 5 in Fig. 7 ). This pattern is currently the most common in Cicadidae.

Discovery of the co-existence of Karelsulcia , Hodgkinia and YLS, together with nested symbiosis between the highly genome-degraded Hodgkinia and the highly genome-conserved Karelsulcia , probably demonstrates a very late ongoing step in the symbiont replacement process and highlights the complexity of co-evolution between nutritional endosymbionts and Cicadidae. This is, so far, the only ongoing symbiont replacement case documented by both ultrastructural and genomic evidence. Our results shed light on an enduring puzzle in the life sciences: how host animals can escape from their dependence on an ancient vertically transmitted symbiont and what adaptative or nonadaptive evolutionary processes may occur during the process of replacement of a bacterial symbiont by a novel fungal symbiont. The results improve our understanding of co-evolutionary relationships of sap-feeding insects with endosymbionts and the endosymbiotic origins of cellular organelles. Further studies are needed to clarify details of the population dynamics, genomic fragmentation patterns and nutrient supplementation in co-occurring genome-eroded Hodgkinia, Karelsulcia and YLS in cicadas in which Hodgkinia nesting in Karelsulcia is in the process of being replaced by YLS.


## Methods


## Sample collection and tissue dissection of cicadas

Sixty-five cicada species (a total of 236 individuals used in this study) were collected during the adult emergence periods between [YEAR] and [YEAR] through daytime netting and nighttime light trapping methods, with detailed specimen information documented in Supplementary Table 1 . Species identification was performed using morphological characteristics and DNA barcoding ( cox1 gene). Some of the collected individuals were dissected to obtain fresh tissues, while the remaining individuals were either individually preserved in anhydrous ethanol or prepared as dry specimens following standard entomological protocols. Before dissection, cicadas were anesthetized at 0 °C for 5 min (anesthesia was omitted when conditions did not permit), externally sterilized with 75% ethanol, and then rinsed with sterile water 5 times. Dissection was performed under sterile conditions using a stereoscopic zoom microscope (Motic SMZ168, China) with 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.2). Sterile forceps were used to isolate intact bacteriomes, fat bodies, testes and ovaries from adult cicadas. Bacteriomes and fat bodies were observed using a stereomicroscope (Olympus SZX10, Japan) and photographed with a digital camera (Canon 550D, Japan). The dissected tissues were preserved in the corresponding reagents (2.5% glutaraldehyde fixative for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) sample preparation; 4% paraformaldehyde or absolute ethanol for fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH); absolute ethanol for DNA extraction) at 4 °C for subsequent TEM, FISH or molecular analyses.


## Embryo collection and fixation

During the sexual maturity period of Chr. ochracea , observations of adults’ courtship, mating, and oviposition behaviors were conducted at Maoming, Guangdong Province, China. After female adults laid eggs on the host plant branches, the branches were immediately cut off and brought back to the laboratory. The eggs were then peeled off and transferred to petri dishes lined with moist absorbent cotton and filter paper, and cultivated at a condition of 25 °C, with regular sampling of some eggs (at least 15 each time) for dissection and fixation. The dissections of eggs at different developmental stages were carried out in Ephrussi–Beadle’s physiological saline (0.75% NaCl + 0.035% KCl + 0.021% CaCl 2 ), and the dissected samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde fixative or 4.0% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C until use.


## DNA extraction, PCR and sequencing

Genomic DNA of cicada legs, bacteriomes and fat bodies was separately extracted using TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (DP304; Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For fungal DNA, we also extracted it using the E.Z.N.A. HP Fungal DNA Kit (D3195-01; Omega Bio-tek, Inc., USA) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Standard PCR methods were used to amplify partial sequences of three genes of host cicadas [cytochrome c oxidase subunit I ( cox1 ), cytochrome c oxidase subunit II ( cox2 ), and cytochrome b ( cob )], two genes of Karelsulcia [16S ribosomal RNA ( 16S ), 23S ribosomal RNA ( 23S )], one gene of Hodgkinia [16S ribosomal RNA ( 16S )], six genes of YLS [18S ribosomal RNA ( 18S ), internal transcribed spacer ( ITS ), 28S ribosomal RNA ( 28S ), DNA-dependent RNA polymerase II largest subunit ( RPB1 ), DNA-dependent RNA polymerase II second largest subunit ( RPB2 ), and the translation elongation factor 1-alpha ( EF-1α )]. PCR primer sequences and annealing temperature are listed in Supplementary Table 2 , and amplifications were conducted in a total volume of 25 μL using the following thermal cycling conditions: an initial denaturing step at 94 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of denaturing at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at sequence-specific temperatures for 1 min, and an extension at 72 °C for 45 s; and a final extension step of 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products were electrophoresed in a 1.0% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide to verify target fragment sizes. DNA products were subsequently sequenced by Beijing Tsingke Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Based on diagnostic PCR amplification, we obtained mitochondrial gene sequences from 59 cicada species, Karelsulcia gene sequences from 58 cicada species, and YLS gene sequences from 41 cicada species (see Supplementary Table 10 for the accession numbers and details).


## Detection of Hodgkinia in cicada bacteriomes by RFLP-based cloning

PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene was performed using the universal primers (see Supplementary Table 2 for detalis). The PCR products were purified with a Universal DNA Purification Kit (Tiangen Inc.), cloned into pMD TM 19-T Vector (Takara Inc.) and subsequently transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α Competent Cells (Takara Inc.). For each biological replicate, ~200 positive clones were selected randomly from a white-blue selection system containing ampicillin and X-gal, and then grown overnight in 900 μL liquid lysogeny broth (LB) medium at 37 °C. Extracted plasmid DNA was used as the template for subsequent PCR amplification with M13 vector primers [M13F: 5’-(CGCCAGGGTTTTCCCAGTCACGAC)−3’, M13R: 5’-(AGCGGATAACAATTTCACACAGGA)−3’] to check positive clones. PCR products of the expected size (~1.5 kb) were digested with restriction endonuclease Hha I at 37 °C for 3 h according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Takara Inc.). For details on the manufacturers and catalog numbers of the relevant reagents, see Supplementary Data 2 . The restriction fragments were separated by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis and analyzed under UV light. Representative clones for each restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) profile were randomly selected for sequencing at Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The 16S rRNA sequences of bacterial symbionts obtained RFLP analysis were used as queries in BLAST searches against the NCBI Nucleotide database, Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) and Silva Database. Based on RFLP-based cloning, we obtained Hodgkinia sequences from 14 species cicadas (see Supplementary Table 10 for details).


## Histological and ultrastructural microscopic observation

First, samples previously fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde were rinsed five times with 0.1 M PBS (pH 7.2). Then, the samples were placed in 0.1 M PBS containing 1.0% osmium tetroxide (OsO 4 ) for postfixation treatment at 4 °C for 1.5 h. After postfixation, the samples were thoroughly rinsed six times with PBS. Next, the samples were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95% for 15 min each, and 100% for 30 min twice). Following this, the samples were infiltrated with a graded mixture of ethanol and LR White Resin (3:1 for 1.5 h, 1:1 for 4 h, and 1:3 for 12 h), subsequently with pure LR White Resin for 24 h twice, and eventually embedded in pure LR White Resin and polymerized at 60 °C for 48 h. Semithin sections (1 μm) were cut with a glass knife, then stained with 1% methylene blue, and photographed using the Leica DM6 B light microscope (Leica, Germany). Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were cut with a diamond knife on the ultramicrotome, followed by double-staining with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and finally examined under the FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit Bio TWIN transmission electron microscope (FEI, Czech Republic).


## Fluorescence in situ hybridization

The dissected bacteriomes, fat bodies and embryos were fixed in 4.0% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in melted paraffin. Paraffin blocks were sectioned into 4 μm sections for histological or fluorescence microscopy. Histological sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and photographed using the Leica DM6 B light microscope (Leica, Germany). FISH was conducted to visualize the distribution of related symbionts in the fat bodies, bacteriomes and embryos of different cicada species 68 , 69 . The probe and helper sequences used in this study were listed in Supplementary Table 3 . Briefly, hybridization was performed on each slide in a final volume of 25 μL hybridization buffer, which contained 2.5xSSC buffer, 10% dextran sulfate (DS), 0.25% bovine serum albumin (BSA), helper oligonucleotides (2 μM), and fluorescently labeled probes (200 nM). For details on the manufacturers and catalog numbers of the relevant reagents, see Supplementary Data 2 . Hybridization was conducted in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for ~10 h. In addition, negative control was done using no probe staining and only one symbiont-targeted probe to check the specificity of hybridization. Slides were observed and imaged under an Olympus FV 3000 IX inverted laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, Japan).


## Genome sequencing and analysis

The dissected bacteriomes, fat bodies and legs of cicadas were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground with a pestle in a plastic tube, mixed with the lysis buffer of the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Cat. No. 51304; QIAGEN GmbH, Germany). Subsequently, proteinase K was added to the lysate, and the DNA extraction was performed following the manufacturer’s Kit protocol. Sequencing libraries were generated using the TruSeq DNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) and the Template Prep Kit (Pacific Biosciences, USA) with an average insert size of ~400 bp following the manufacturer’s protocol. The genome sequencing was then performed by Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) using the Illumina Novaseq platform. An average of 10 Gb of raw reads (paired-end, 150 bp each) was produced from each DNA sample through metagenomic sequencing (see Supplementary Table 5 for details), whereas 30 Gb (~5x coverage) was generated through whole-genome resequencing (see Supplementary Table 6 for details).

Data assembly was performed after adapter contamination removing and data filtering using AdapterRemoval v2.3.1 70 and SOAPec v2.01 71 . The filtered reads were assembled by SPAdes v3.7.1 72 or Megahit v1.2.9 73 to construct contigs. We identified symbiont contigs using blastn 2.15.0+ and tblastn 2.15.0+ searches against a custom database containing genomes of multiple known insect symbionts, verifying the identifications using coverage and GC content information (computed using BBTools v38.78). Then, for Karelsulcia contigs, we confirmed their circularity and contiguity by read mapping and visualization on Tablet v1.20.12.24 74 and the presence of overlapping ends. We rearranged the circularized genomes to ensure the same orientation and start position as in those published previously. Hodgkinia , Arsenophonus , Wolbachia , Tisiphia and Rickettsia genomes were represented by multiple contigs, and we did not attempt to close them (except in the Hodgkinia genome of Chr. nigra ). We found 11 scaffolds assembled from these combined data that contained recognizable Hodgkinia sequences of Chr. nigra and totaled 588,504 bp (47.68% GC) in length. Of these 11, we were able to verify that 2 scaffolds were circular-mapping genomes by PCR and Sanger sequencing (accession numbers: CP178246 and CP178247 ). All symbiont genomes have been deposited in the NCBI Genome database (see Supplementary Tables 5 , 7 and 9 for details). We only selected a subset of these symbionts to illustrate their contribution to the nutritional metabolic pathways of their cicada hosts. The genomes of these symbionts were annotated using Prokka v1.14.6 75 , with the default parameters. The pseudogenes were detected using Pseudofinder v1.1.0 76 .

The representative mitogenomes of fungi in the order Hypocreales were retrieved and used as references to map raw sequencing reads. Subsequent de novo assembly was performed using the mapped reads with SPAdes v3.7.1 72 . We successfully assembled mitogenomes from 20 cicada-associated fungal symbionts and one mitogenome from the fungal symbiont of the treehopper Da. hardwickii using raw reads from both our sequencing data (15 samples) and those (6 samples) published by Matsuura et al. 19 (see Supplementary Table 8 for details). Additionally, we assembled the mitochondrial genomes of 38 cicada species from metagenomic sequencing data and whole-genome resequencing data using NOVOPlasty v4.3.1 77 (see Supplementary Table 10 for details).


## Molecular phylogenetic analysis

The gene and genome sequences of 65 cicada species and their symbionts were obtained in this study (see Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 5 – 10 for details). The remaining molecular sequences were downloaded from NCBI database (see Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Tables 6 – 9 , 11 for details). USCOs were extracted from genomes using BUSCO v5.4.7 78 from dataset “hemiptera_odb10” (for cicadas; Supplementary Table 6 ), dataset “flavobacteriales_odb10” (for Karelsulcia ; Supplementary Table 7 ), dataset “enterobacterales_odb10” (for Arsenophonus ; Supplementary Table 9 ), and dataset “rickettsiales_odb10” (for Wolbachia , Rickettsia , and Tisiphia ; Supplementary Table 9 ). Multiple sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.450 79 . To reduce compositional heterogeneity, the gappy columns were trimmed using TrimAl v1.4.1 80 . Trimmed alignments were concatenated using FASConCAT-g v1.04 81 . For the USCOs of cicadas and Karelsulcia , we conducted normalized Relative Compositional Frequency Variation (nRCFV 82 ) detection by removing sequences greater than 0.0016. The USCO matrices (USCO50, USCO60, USCO80 and USCO90) of 50%, 60%, 80% and 90% completeness were generated using PhyKIT v1.0.0 83 . Partitioning schemes and substitution models were selected based on the best­fit partition model and the BIC criterion using ModelFinder v2.2.2 84 as implemented in IQ-TREE v1.6.8 85 and included in PhyloSuite v1.2.3 86 . Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using maximum-likelihood (ML) by IQ-TREE v1.6.8 85 and Bayesian inference (BI) by Mrbayes v3.2.6 87 . The ML tree was inferred under an edge-linked partition model for 5000 ultrafast bootstraps and 1000 SH-aLRT replicates. For the BI analysis, Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) settings were as follows: 2 independent runs, 4 Markov chains, run for 2 × 10 6 generations with sampling every 100 generations and the initial 25% of sampled data were discarded as burn-in. When the standard deviation of clade frequencies fell below 0.01, the analyses terminated. Finally, the con-tree files were visualized and refined using iTOL v6 88 .


## Divergence time estimation of Cicadidae

Divergence times were estimated for the phylogeny resulting from a Bayesian estimation based on three genes ( cox1 , cox2 and cob ) using MCMCTree (implemented in paml v4.10.7 89 ) under a HKY85 model. Seven fossils from three different fossil faunas, ranging in age from Late Cretaceous (~98.2 MYA) to Miocene (~5.3 MYA), were used to calibrate nodes on the phylogeny (see Supplementary Table 4 for detalis). To provide a lower bound for the root node of Cicadidae, we used Burmacicada , the oldest known cicada fossil, dated at 98.2 MYA, to constrain the minimum age of the ingroup. Two priors for the rgene gamma and sigma2 gamma were set to (2, 20) and (1, 10), respectively. An alpha value of 0.5 and an independent rates model were employed. MCMC sampling involved 20,000 estimates sampled every 50 cycles after a burn-in of 200,000 cycles. The stationarity and convergence of chains were checked in Tracer v1.7.2 90 , ensuring the effective sample sizes (ESS) > 200 for all parameters. Finally, a maximum credibility tree, annotated with mean node ages and the 95% highest posterior density (HPD) intervals, was visualized using FigTree v1.4.4 (Available from: http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/ ).
