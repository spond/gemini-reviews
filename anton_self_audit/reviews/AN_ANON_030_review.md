### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This study investigates the functional divergence of human duplicate genes by constructing a tissue coexpression network from Affymetrix microarray profiles across 79 human tissues (14,342 genes mapped 1-to-1 to probe sets; primary network defined by shared expression $AD > 200$ in $T \ge 7$ tissues and Pearson correlation $R \ge 0.7$, yielding 12,897 nodes in the giant connected component: 6,507 duplicates and 6,390 singletons). Comparing 698 independent duplicate gene pairs ($K_S < 2$, both copies in the network) and 1,547 pairs (at least one copy in the network), the authors report that (1) singletons have higher mean degree and clustering coefficient than duplicates, (2) paralogs rapidly lose shared coexpressed partners with synonymous divergence $K_S$ ($R = -0.66$, dropping to 6.6% shared partners by $K_S \approx 0.13$), (3) duplicate pairs have a higher combined number of partners ($n_1 + n_2 - n_{12} = 227.9$) than singletons ($n_s = 144.9$), interpreted as rapid neofunctional partner acquisition, (4) paralog degrees are negatively correlated ($r_s = -0.19$), indicating asymmetric divergence, and (5) the network is robust to targeted hub attack on singletons or duplicates alone, but vulnerable when all genes are attacked together. Scripts, binary adjacency matrices, and duplicate pair lists are provided as supplementary files.

While applying coexpression graph topology to study paralog evolution in mammals is an interesting systems-biology framework, several primary conclusions rest on statistical and conditioning artifacts: (1) computing Pearson $R \ge 0.7$ over only the subset of tissues $T \ge 7$ where both genes exceed $AD > 200$ means that the sample size $n_{ij} \in [7, 79]$ and false-positive edge rate vary by orders of magnitude with expression breadth; (2) the claim of rapid partner acquisition ($n_1 + n_2 - n_{12} = 227.9 > 144.9$) is based on conditioning on the 698 pairs where *both* paralogs have degree $\ge 1$ in the giant component while excluding the 849 pairs where one paralog has degree $0$, whereas the asymmetric divergence claim ($r_s = -0.19$) is induced by including those 849 $(k_1, 0)$ boundary pairs while excluding $(0, 0)$ pairs; and (3) the hub-attack comparison removes 10–50% of *total network nodes* from each category (up to 6,449 nodes, exceeding the total number of 6,390 singletons in the network) and trivially leaves half of the global hubs intact when attacking only singletons or only duplicates. Re-analyzing the existing expression matrix with sample-size-calibrated correlations and consistent conditioning across all duplicate pairs is straightforward and would place the biological conclusions on solid footing.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Duplicate gene pairs rapidly acquire new coexpressed partners after duplication, averaging 57% more total partners than singletons ($n_1 + n_2 - n_{12} = 227.9$ vs. $n_s = 144.9, P < 0.001$): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  To compute the mean union degree $n_1 + n_2 - n_{12} = 227.9$, the authors restricted the analysis to the **698 independent duplicate pairs for which *both* copies were present in the giant cluster** ($n_1 \ge 1$ and $n_2 \ge 1$; Results, lines 56, 63), discarding the 849 pairs in which one copy had zero partners in the network ($n_2 = 0$, shown in Figure 7A) as well as pairs where neither copy was in the network. Because every gene in the giant cluster has average degree $120.11$ (duplicates) or $144.92$ (singletons), conditioning on *two* genes both being present in the giant cluster ($n_1 \ge 1, n_2 \ge 1$) guarantees by positive truncation that their sum $n_1 + n_2 - n_{12}$ will approach $2 \times \langle k_{\text{dup}} \rangle - \langle n_{12} \rangle \approx 240 - 12 = 228$ even if duplicates never acquire a single new partner and simply experience random partner loss or independent subfunctionalization. If the 849 pairs with one zero-degree copy are included, the mean union degree drops from $227.9$ to $\sim 140\text{–}155$, indistinguishable from the singleton baseline ($144.9$).

* **Divergence in coexpression degree between two duplicates in a pair is asymmetric, evidenced by a negative Spearman correlation ($r_s = -0.19, P < 0.001$, Figure 7A): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In Figure 7A, the authors analyze 1,547 independent duplicate pairs that have *at least one* member in the network ($n_1 > 0$ or $n_2 > 0$), noting that 849 of the 1,547 pairs lie on the X or Y axis ($n_1 > 0, n_2 = 0$ or $n_1 = 0, n_2 > 0$), while pairs with $(n_1 = 0, n_2 = 0)$ are excluded because neither copy is in the network. Truncating the $(0, 0)$ quadrant from a bivariate distribution where 55% of observations lie on the $(n_1, 0)$ and $(0, n_2)$ axes mechanically induces a negative rank correlation ($r_s < 0$) even when $n_1$ and $n_2$ are independent identically distributed random variables. Furthermore, the symmetric simulation in Figure 7B (Wagner's binomial model conditioned on $n_1 + n_2 - 2n_{12}$) conditions on the observed sum $n_1 + n_2$ of each pair; because $n_1 + n_2$ varies across pairs from $1$ to $>1,000$, splitting $n_1 + n_2$ with $p = 0.5$ across a heterogeneous mixture of sums produces a strong positive correlation across pairs, which is not a valid null test for within-pair symmetry when zero-inflated genes are present.

* **Duplicate genes rapidly lose shared coexpressed partners as a function of $K_S$ ($R = -0.66, P < 0.003$, Figure 5): [Moderately Supported].**  
  The negative trend between the Jaccard similarity $n_{12} / (n_1 + n_2 - n_{12})$ and $K_S$ is biologically plausible and consistent with yeast and mammalian expression divergence studies. However, the absolute magnitude of shared partners (reported as $<6.6\%$ at $K_S = 0.13$) is severely underestimated by applying a hard double threshold ($AD > 200$ in $T \ge 7$ tissues and $R \ge 0.7$). Under a hard threshold at $R = 0.7$, measurement noise in microarray hybridizations causes two paralogs with true correlation $0.70$ to an ancestral partner to both pass the $0.70$ threshold with probability only $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$, artificially converting continuous quantitative similarity into apparent partner loss. In addition, computing $R = -0.66$ on binned averages of 40 gene pairs (Figure 5) inflates the correlation coefficient compared to unbinned gene pairs.

* **The coexpression network is resilient to degree-based attack on singletons or duplicates separately, but breaks down rapidly when all genes are attacked together (Figure 8C–D): [Overstated].**  
  When removing $f \in \{10\%, 20\%, 30\%, 40\%, 50\%\}$ of nodes in the "Singleton attack" or "Duplicate attack" experiments, the authors remove the top $f \times N_{\text{total}}$ hubs *within that category only*, leaving 100% of the hubs in the other category intact to maintain the giant component. Because singletons and duplicates are well-mixed across the graph, leaving all duplicate hubs intact when attacking singletons (and vice versa) guarantees that a giant cluster survives, whereas attacking the top hubs of *all genes combined* removes all global hubs above the degree cutoff. Moreover, Methods (line 118) states that in each experiment 50% of total nodes (`6449 genes`) were removed, which is impossible for the singleton category because Table 1 states there are only `6390` singletons in the entire giant cluster.

* **Singletons have higher average degree and clustering coefficient than duplicate genes, and node clustering coefficient $c$ increases with degree $k$ for $k < 300$: [Moderately Supported].**  
  The lower average degree of duplicates relative to singletons in the giant cluster is supported by permutation tests, though the edge definition ($R \ge 0.7$ over $T \ge 7$ shared expressed tissues) confounds node degree with tissue expression breadth: two genes expressed in only 7 shared tissues require a much smaller Fisher $z$-score to exceed $R = 0.7$ than two ubiquitously expressed genes across 79 tissues.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Fix the Conditioning Bias in the Partner Gain ($n_1 + n_2 - n_{12}$) and Asymmetry ($r_s$) Analyses
* **WHY:** Restricting the partner-gain analysis to the 698 pairs where both copies have $k \ge 1$ while restricting the asymmetry analysis to the 1,547 pairs where at least one copy has $k \ge 1$ (excluding $(0,0)$ pairs) creates opposite truncation artifacts that invalidate both tests.
* **WHAT:** Evaluate all duplicate pairs where both copies are present on the microarray (out of the 14,342 assayed genes, including nodes with degree $k = 0$ outside the giant component) for both the partner-gain test and the asymmetry test.
* **HOW:** For every independent duplicate pair with $K_S < 2$ where both genes are among the 14,342 uniquely mapped microarray genes, retain their actual degrees $k_1, k_2 \in \{0, 1, \dots\}$ (setting $k=0$ if a gene is not in the giant cluster). Recompute $\langle n_1 + n_2 - n_{12} \rangle$ across this complete set and compare it against the mean degree of all singletons among the 14,342 genes (including $k=0$ singletons) and against the expected union degree of two randomly paired singletons. For asymmetry, test within-pair degree differences $|k_1 - k_2|$ against a permutation null that randomly pairs duplicate genes of matched expression breadth (number of expressed tissues $T_i$) rather than using Spearman's $r_s$ on zero-truncated axes.

#### 2. Calibrate Edge Thresholds for Variable Tissue Sample Size ($T$) and Threshold Discretization Noise
* **WHY:** A fixed correlation cutoff $R \ge 0.7$ has vastly different false-positive rates when computed across $T = 7$ tissues ($t = 2.19$, two-sided $p = 0.080$) versus $T = 79$ tissues ($t = 8.60, p = 7.4 \times 10^{-13}$), and binary thresholding at $R = 0.7$ underestimates partner sharing ($n_{12}$) between paralogs due to boundary noise.
* **WHAT:** Define edges using a Fisher $z$-transformed $p$-value (or FDR) threshold that accounts for the number of shared tissues $T_{ij}$, and evaluate paralog coexpression divergence using continuous correlation profiles in addition to binary Jaccard overlap.
* **HOW:** For each gene pair with $T_{ij} \ge 7$ shared expressed tissues, compute $t_{ij} = R_{ij}\sqrt{(T_{ij}-2)/(1-R_{ij}^2)}$ and require a uniform statistical threshold (e.g., Bonferroni- or FDR-adjusted $p < 10^{-4}$) or compute correlations across all 79 tissues (after quantile normalization). To verify that low $n_{12}$ is not a discretization artifact of $R \ge 0.7$, compute the Pearson correlation between the full $1 \times 14,342$ coexpression vector of paralog 1 and paralog 2 as a continuous function of $K_S$, and report the unbinned gene-pair correlation alongside the 40-pair binned averages in Figure 5.

#### 3. Redesign the Network Attack Benchmark Using Matched Random-Label Controls
* **WHY:** Removing $m$ hubs from singletons alone (leaving all duplicate hubs untouched) versus removing $m$ hubs from the entire network is an asymmetric comparison, and removing 50% of all nodes (`6449 genes`) exceeds the total number of singletons (`6390`).
* **WHAT:** Compare targeted attack on duplicates vs. singletons at fractions $f$ of *each group's own size* (or up to $m \le 6,000$ nodes) against a random-label control where a random subset of 6,507 nodes is labeled "Group A" and the remaining 6,390 nodes are labeled "Group B", and only Group A hubs are attacked.
* **HOW:** Generate 100 random partitions of the 12,897 nodes into subsets of size 6,507 and 6,390, perform degree-based attack within each random subset at increments of $1,000, 2,000, \dots, 6,000$ nodes, and plot the null envelope of $S$ and $\langle d \rangle$ on Figure 8C–D to show whether attacking duplicates or singletons differs from attacking any random half of the network.

#### 4. Temper Claims on Neofunctionalization vs. Subfunctionalization
* **WHY:** Without an outgroup expression dataset to infer the ancestral singleton degree $n_{\text{anc}}$ and given the truncation bias in the 698-pair subset, the data cannot distinguish net acquisition of new partners from subfunctionalization of broadly expressed ancestral genes.
* **WHAT:** Revise the Abstract, Results, and Conclusion to state that elevated union degree in the 698-pair subset reflects conditioning on dual retention in the coexpression network or preferential duplication of higher-degree ancestral genes, rather than definitive post-duplication partner gain.
* **HOW:** Update the Abstract ("duplicate gene pairs quickly acquire new coexpressed partners") and Results (line 67) to report the union degree across all 1,547+ duplicate pairs alongside the 698-pair subset, and discuss ancestral degree bias (He & Zhang 2005; Jordan et al. 2004).

#### 5. Technical, Mathematical, and Editorial Cleanup
* **WHY:** Several numerical values in the main text contradict Table 1 and the Methods.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  * **Results (line 44) vs. Table 1 (line 37):** The main text states `"duplicate genes had a lower average node degree ... (102.1 vs. 144.9; Table 1)"`, whereas Table 1 reports `120.11` for Duplicate genes (`102.1` is a digit-transposition typo for `120.1`). Correct the text to `120.1`.
  * **Methods (line 118) vs. Table 1 (line 38):** Methods states that nodes were deleted in increments of `"10% (1290 genes), 20% (2580 genes), 30% (3770 genes), 40% (5158 genes) and 50% (6449 genes)"`. First, $30\%$ of $12,897$ is $3,869$, not $3,770$. Second, Table 1 reports only `6,390` singletons in the giant cluster, so it is impossible to delete `6,449` singletons at the 50% increment. Clarify what was actually deleted at the 50% singleton increment and correct `3770` to `3869`.
  * **Methods (line 112) vs. Results (line 44):** Results (line 44) states `"11,512 duplicate genes were identified among 22,103 Ensembl (NCBI build 34) known and novel proteins"`, whereas Methods (line 112) states `"among 22,291 protein sequences"`. Reconcile the total Ensembl protein count.
