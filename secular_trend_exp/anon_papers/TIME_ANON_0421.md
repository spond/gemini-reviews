## Abstract

Neolamarckia macrophylla is one of two species in genus Neolamarckia of the Rubiaceae family and distributed only in Indonesia. The two species possess several common properties, including fast-growing, superiority of stem form for wood industry and potential medicinal value. However, compared with N. cadamba that has been extensively explored for industrial and pharmacological applications, little is known about N . macrophylla . Here we sequenced the mitochondrial genome of N. macrophylla , which was 289,793 bp in length and assembled into a single circular structure. The genome contained 36 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 19 tRNA and 3 rRNA genes, and was characterized by a high AT content (55.03%), low coding-site per site (13.39%), typical C-to-U RNA editing, and codon usage bias. Homologous sequences accounted for 39.13% of its length in alignment with the nuclear genome of N. cadamba , and 1.2% in alignment with the chloroplast genome of N. macrophylla . A comparative analysis indicated that N. macrophylla substantially differed from N. cadamba , with an alternative conformation besides a smaller mitochondrial genome size, higher gene density per site, and less protein- and tRNA-coding genes. Strong purifying selection was present in all PCGs between the two species. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the two species were divergent at about 6.757 Mya. This study provides the first complete mitochondrial genome sequence of N. macrophylla , enriching the genomic resource of the Neolamarckia genus for future study. The overall findings confirmed divergent structure of mitochondrial genomes between the two species in genus Neolamarckia .

## Introduction

The Neolamarckia genus is composed of two species, N. cadamba and N. macrophylla , and belongs to the Cinchonoidae subfamily, Naucleeae tribe of the Rubiaceae family [ 1 , 2 ]. The genus is naturally distributed in tropical regions, including Southeast Asia, South Asia, and Australia [ 2 , 3 ]. The two species have unequal sizes of natural geographical distribution where N. cadamba in South Asia is distributed in Vietnam, Malaysia, Myanmar, India, Sri Lanka and southern China while N. macrophylla is only distributed in Indonesia [ 4 ]. N. macrophylla exhibits a disjunct distribution, being endemic to eastern Indonesia (including Sulawesi and the Maluku Islands) [ 5 ], and morphologically differs from N. cadamba by its more robust and rose-coloured leaves [ 6 ]. Both species are pioneer species and grow in high humidity (1200–2400 mm for annual precipitation), high temperature (average 20–24 °C) and fertile soils. Both species are fast-growing, aka a miraculous tree, and can attain 45 m in height and 1.6 m in trunk diameter [ 7 , 8 ]. The two species can be planted in watershed areas for wetland ecological restoration and have high capability in absorbing and holding water. The rapid growth rate and adaptability to critical habitats have established the two species as an important industrial and ecological plant in tropical regions.

Two distinct features of the Neolamarckia genus make this plant an important botanical resource, valued for its industrial and medicinal applications. One feature is concerned with the physical properties of wood property traits in both N. macrophylla and N. cadamba [ 9 – 11 ], such as a low lignin content and lengthy wood fibers comparable to conifer trees [ 12 ]. Both species have straight and cylindrical trunk suitable for raw material for wood industry. The high-quality timber is extensively utilized in manufacturing plywood, dimensional lumber, engineered wood products, and paper pulp [ 13 ]. The second feature is concerned with pharmacologically active compounds and its potential for developing new drugs and disease treatments [ 14 , 15 ]. N. macrophylla demonstrates a substantially pharmacological potential due to its diverse secondary metabolite profiles. Traditional medicinal systems have employed this species for treating febrile conditions, vomiting, infections, and inflammation [ 16 – 18 ]. While pharmacological investigations remain preliminary, studies of its congener N. cadamba have revealed promising bioactive properties, including anticancer activity mediated through apoptosis induction and cell cycle modulation [ 19 ]. Notably, the phenolic-rich leaf extracts exhibit significant antimicrobial and antioxidant capacities [ 20 ], highlighting their therapeutic potentials.

Previous genetic studies mainly concentrated on N. cadamba , such as provenance trials [ 21 ], transcriptomic analysis [ 22 ], gene discovery and expression in xylem tissues [ 23 , 24 ], and population structure assayed with molecular markers in Malaysia [ 25 ] and China [ 26 ]. Existing genetic research on N. macrophylla is less recorded in literature. Only a few reports are available, such as analysis on wood traits based on provenance trails [ 8 , 27 ], industrial application and propagation techniques [ 28 ], preliminary population genetic diversity [ 8 ], progeny test [ 29 ], mutation breeding [ 13 , 30 ], and seed germination [ 31 ]. Both species are monoecious with both stamen and pistil in a single flower. N. macrophylla was reported to have a mixed mating system, with a selfing rate of about 23.1% [ 32 ]. The genetic relationship between the two species has rarely been explored. Only a couple of studies have examined their relationship, such as the analyses based on a single cellulose synthase gene [ 33 ] and the chloroplast genome [ 34 ], showing that N. macrophylla and N. cadamba were genetically closely related.

In this study, we sequenced the mitochondrial genome of N. macrophylla for the first time and assembled the genome. Structure of mitochondrial genome of N. macrophylla was then characterized, including whose genome organization, repetitive sequences, codon usage, the homologous sequences with the chloroplast genome [ 34 ] of N. macrophylla and the nuclear genome of N. cadamba [ 35 ]. Through comparative genomic analyses, we assessed the evolutionary divergence in mitochondrial genome sequences between N. macrophylla and N. cadamba [ 1 ] and examined evolutionary process underlying their divergences. Since plant mitochondrial genomes possess several evolutionarily informative features [ 1 ], such as uniparental inheritance, lower mutation rates compared with the chloroplast genome, intra-chromosomal recombination, and larger genetic drift effects, their structural divergence would provide alternative contexts of the genetic relationship between N. macrophylla and N. cadamba. This research enriches genome resources to the existing genomic data in the Neolamarckia genus and provides a basis for future genetic and biotechnological applications with mitochondrial genomes.


## Materials and methods


## Sample collection and DNA extraction

Fresh leaves were collected from an ex-situ N. macrophylla tree planted at the Herb Garden of INTI International University, which is originated from a plantation in Kuala Muda District, Kedah State of Malaysia (Geographic coordinates: E100°28′49.95", N5°47′44.72"). The sampled tree was identified by SYL, SHL and SS. Voucher specimens were deposited in both the Biotechnology Laboratory at INTI International University and the Herbarium of South China Agricultural University (CANT) (Accession number: 32221). Genomic DNA was extracted using QIAGEN Genomic-tips at the Biotechnology Laboratory of INTI International University by following standard protocols [ 1 ]. The extracted DNA sample was checked by (1) 0.8% agarose electrophoresis to detect DNA samples for degradation and impurity, and to estimate the DNA concentration; (2) UV–Vis Nano Spectrophotometer (Nabi, Korea) spectrophotometer to detect the concentration and purity of samples; and (3) Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer (Life Technologies, USA) to detect the concentration of samples.


## Library construction and high-throughput sequencing

Both the second- and third-generation sequencings were applied to sequence mitochondrial genome of N. macrophylla . For PacBio sequencing, library preparation was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol of SMRTbell® Express Template Prep Kit 2.0 (Pacific Biosciences, PN 101–853-100). Briefly, quality-checked genomic DNA was sheared using a 26G needle, and fragments greater than 10 kb were size-selected using BluePippin. Subsequent steps included end-repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation to construct the SMRTbell library. Following quality control assessment, qualified libraries were sequenced on the PacBio Revio system (Pacific Biosciences, CA, USA) with appropriate loading concentrations determined by both library quality metrics and desired data output requirements. The PacBio sequencing platform was used to sequence and produced high-quality sequencing reads (polymerase reads) of single molecules. Each polymerase read sequence was divided into one or more subsequences, called subreads. The generated subreads were processed by software and converted into CCS data. The CCS data with a quality value greater than or equal to Q20 were extracted to obtain the final high-quality HiFi data.

For the second-generation sequencing, a double ended sequencing library with an inserted fragment size of 350 bp was constructed according to the Illumina DNA library construction standard process using qualified DNA samples. The Prep Kit for Illumina V3 (Vazyme, ND607) for universal DNA library was followed. After the completion of library construction, qPCR method and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) were used to control quality. The qualified DNA libraries were sequenced using the Illumina Novaseq6000 (Illumina, USA) high-throughput sequencing platform with the PE150 (Pair-End 150) sequencing strategy. Raw sequencing data were filtered with fastp v.000.23.2 [ 31 ] (-q 20 -l 150) to remove low-quality reads and adapters.


## Genome assembly

The mitochondrial genome of N. macrophylla was sequenced using PacBio long-read technology. To ensure data quality, raw Illumina high-throughput sequencing reads were processed to remove low-quality sequences, generating high-quality clean reads using fastp v0.23.2 [ 36 ]. The PacBio sequencing data were assembled into contigs using Canu v2.2. To identify mitochondrial genome sequences from the whole-genome assembly, we conducted BLASTn (v2.15.0 +) search against the NCBI nt database. Contigs with significant homology to known mitochondrial sequences were extracted for further analysis. Since the initial Canu assembly typically produced linear contigs, we performed manual curation to circularize the mitochondrial genome. This involved examining overlapping regions between contigs and validating connections using paired-end Illumina reads. The final circular mitochondrial genome was annotated and visualized using OGDRAW v1.3.1 [ 37 ].

Bowtie2 v2.4.2 [ 38 ] was applied to align sequencing data obtained from Illumina Novaseq6000 (Illumina, USA) high-throughput sequencing platform (second-generation sequencing) to mitochondrial genome. The resulting alignments were processed using SAMtools v1.8 [ 39 ], where reads were sorted to generate a BAM file. To enhance mapping precision, we further filtered the alignments using SAMtools v1.14 with the -bF 12 parameter to retain only properly paired reads for subsequent analyses of coverage and depth. Sequencing depth at each mitochondrial genomic position was calculated using the depth function in SAMtools. The depth profiles were then visualized following the methodology described by Wei et al. [ 40 ] with default parameters applied to generate comprehensive coverage and depth distribution plots.


## Genome annotations and repetitive sequences

We annotated the mitochondrial genome by combining Geseq [ 41 ] and ORFFinder [ 42 ] ( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/ ). To ensure annotation accuracy, we performed rigorous validation through comparative analysis: protein-coding genes (PCGs) were verified using BLASTp (v2.15.0 +) against known mitochondrial proteins from related species, and rRNA sequences were validated through BLASTn v2.15.0 + alignments with a few published mitochondrial genomes in the Rubiaceae family, including Damnacanthus indicus var. indicus ( MZ285073.1 ), D. indicus var. microphyllus ( MZ285076.1 ), D. indicus var. indicus ( MZ285071.1 ), N. cadamba ( MT364442.1 ), Uncaria rhynchophylla ( OR051900.1 ), and in the Solanaceae family, including Lycium barbarum ( PQ638952.1 ) and L. ruthenicum ( PQ613844.1 ). During this process, we carefully examined and adjusted gene features including start/stop codons and intron–exon boundaries based on conserved characteristics of plant mitochondrial genomes. For tRNA annotation, we employed a dual-validation strategy. The initial predictions from ARWEN v1.2 were cross-verified using tRNAscan-SE 2.0. This approach allowed us to identify and discard tRNAs with abnormal length or incomplete structure and generate accurate secondary structure predictions.

A total of 128 simple repeats (SSRs) were identified using MISA-web [ 43 ]. The minimum number of repeats for mononucleotide, dinucleotide, trinucleotide, tetranucleotide, pentanucleotide, and hexanucleotide was set to 8, 5, 4, 4, and 4, respectively. In addition, we analyzed long repetitive sequences (minimum repeat length = 30 bp) using REPuter [ 44 ]. This enabled identification of all significant repeat sequences while maintaining high specificity in repeat detection.


## Codon usage

The relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) was estimated using MEGA 7.0.26 to quantify codon preference strength with default parameters. The resulting data were visualized using ggplot2 v3.1.1 [ 45 ]. RSCU values deviating from unity indicate codon usage bias, while values equaling 1.0 suggest neutral selection with no preferential usage. Besides, nucleotide skewness was estimated by the following formulae [ 4 ]: GC-skew = (G%—C%)/(G% + C%) and AT-skew = (A%—T%)/(A% + T%), where A%, T%, G% and C% represent the percentages of A, T, G and C in a focal sequence, respectively.


## Identification of RNA-editing

All protein-coding genes (PCGs) in the mitochondrial genome were used as input text files to predict editing sites based on a convolutional neural network model through Deepred-mt [ 46 ]. The results were the preserved probability values greater than 0.9.


## Phylogenetic and collinearity analysis

To investigate the evolutionary relationships between N. macrophylla and its close relatives, we performed a phylogenetic analysis using single-copy homologous genes. We selected four tree or arbor species in the Rubiaceae family, including Coffea arabica , Scyphiphora hydrophyllacea , Cinchona calisaya , and N. cadamba . The complete mitochondrial genome sequences of these species were downloaded from NCBI database. Shared 19 protein-coding genes across all taxa were identified and extracted for phylogenetic reconstruction. MUSCLE v.3.8.31 [ 47 ] ( http://www.drive5.com/muscle/ ) software was used to compare the amino acids of individual genes among multiple species, and the differentiation time of each species was estimated by BEAST2 (v2.7.1) [ 48 ] software. The divergent times between selected species were provided from TimeTree ( https://timetree.org ; access on November 3, [YEAR]). They were 30.2–37.4 Mya (95% highest posterior density, HPD) between C. arabica and S. hydrophyllacea , 38.0–51.3Mya between C. calisaya and N. cadamba , and 55.4–72.2 Mya among the four species. These divergent times were used to estimate and correct the divergent times of N. macrophylla with other species.

The analytical pipeline comprised the following steps: First, individual gene sequences were aligned using MUSCLE v3.8.31 [ 49 ] with default parameters. The resulting alignments were then concatenated to generate species-specific protein-coding gene sets. Sequence alignments were refined using Gblocks v0.91b [ 50 ] (with stringent parameters (-b5 = h) to eliminate poorly aligned regions and positions with excessive variability. The curated concatenated alignments were subjected to substitution model selection using ModelFinder [ 51 ] implemented in IQ-TREE2, which identified the JTT + F + I + G4 model as optimal based on Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Maximum likelihood phylogenies were inferred with 1000 bootstrap replicates under the following parameters: -m MFP -B 1000 -alrt 1000, providing robust nodal support estimates.

To elucidate genome synteny patterns in N. macrophylla , we performed comparative genomic analyses through pairwise BLASTN alignments between: (i) mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes [ 34 ], and (ii) mitochondrial and nuclear genomes [ 35 ]. The alignment results were processed using local blast to identify homologous genomic regions with E -value of 10 –5 . The genomic regions with the homologous rate less than 95% were filtered out. The subsequent visualization of syntenic relationships was accomplished through Circos v.0.67 software, generating high-resolution synteny maps. To analyze the collinearity relationship of the mitochondrial genome of the genus Neolamarckia , we downloaded the mitochondrial genomes of N. cadamba and A. thaliana from the NCBI database, with accession numbers MT320890.1 , MT364442.1 , and Y08501.2 , respectively. We compared the mitochondrial genome using BLASTn v.2.11.0, identified homologous genes and conserved regions, and identified collinear blocks using MCScanX v.1.0.0. The collinearity was visualized using Multiple Synteny plot function of TBtools V.2.326.


## Test of natural selection

To assess the evolutionary process underlying mutant genes in N. macrophylla compared with N. cadamba , we estimated the non-synonymous ( and synonymous ( ) substitution rates and their ratios ( ) for all orthologous PCGs. Pairwise orthologous PCGs are based for calculating the ratio [ 52 ]. Specific steps are detailed as follows: (1) counting the numbers of synonymous and nonsynonymous sites in each gene sequence and their sums; (2) counting the numbers of synonymous and nonsynonymous mutations between two gene sequences; (3) calculating the proportions of synonymous substitutions per synonymous site ( ) and nonsynonymous substitutions per nonsynonymous site ( ; and (4) estimating ratio. KaKs_Calculator 3.0 [ 53 ] was applied to calculate this ratio. When the ratio is equal to unity ( =1), the focal gene is under neutrality. When the ratio is significantly greater than 1 ( >1), the focal gene is under positive selection; when the ratio is significantly less than 1 ( <1), the focal gene is under purifying selection.


## Results

The sequences from Illumina Novaseq6000 platform generated a total of 5,493,724 reads and 2,324,058,600 bp, and Q20 greater than 97.91 and Q30 greater than 94.05. The mitochondrial genome was assembled in 289,793 bp in length, with average depth 97.24X (34 ~ 193X) and 100% coverage. Base composition analysis revealed an AT-biased feature (55.03%), with individual nucleotide frequencies of 27.69% (A), 27.34% (T), 22.72% (G), and 22.25% (C). Tandem repeat analysis detected 355,791 repetitive elements (microsatellites, minisatellites, and satellites), accounting for 3.59% of the total genome sequence-a basis for potentially contributing to structural plasticity and evolutionary adaptation. The mitochondrial PCGs were predominantly initiated by the canonical ATG start codon, with a subset utilizing ACG as an alternative initiation codon (Table 1 ). Termination codons were primarily TAA, though TGA and TAG were also observed in certain genes, reflecting conserved mitochondrial translation mechanisms. As shown in Fig. 2 , most genes were located on the heavy strand (H-strand), with a minority distributed on the light strand (L-strand). While AT- and GC-skew values varied among genes, the majority exhibited near-zero skewness, indicating balanced nucleotide composition across the genome. A notable feature of the N. macrophylla mitochondrial genome was the absence of * tRNA -Thr*, * tRNA- Val* and * tRNA- Arg* (The “*” symbol refers to this amino acid coded by two or more synonymous codons).

## Codon usage

Codon usage bias analysis revealed significant preference for specific codons in the mitochondrial genome of N. macrophylla (Fig. 3 ). Among high-preference codons, the stop codon TAA exhibited the highest usage frequency (0.20%, RSCU = 1.75). Preference was observed for TTA (frequency 2.62%, RSCU = 1.49) and CTT in leucine (Leu) and for TTT in phenylalanine (Phe), which appeared the most frequently (390 times). Conversely, among low-preference codons, the usage frequency of CGC in arginine (Arg) was the lowest (RSCU = 0.62), while GCG in alanine (Ala) also showed low frequency (RSCU = 0.46). In glutamine (Gln), CAG had the lowest usage frequency (RSCU = 0.46). Additionally, methionine (Met) and tryptophan (Trp) were represented by only one codon ATG/TGG, with RSCU = 1.0. The stop codon TAG was rarely utilized, occurring only 4 times.


> **Fig. 3:** The RSCU values for each amino acid encoded by distinct genetic codes. The x-axis lists the amino acids from alanine (Ala) to valine (Val), while the y-axis represents the RSCU values, which quantify the usage frequency of each codon relative to its synonymous counterparts


## Repetitive sequences

A total of 128 SSR sites were identified by MISA-web, including multiple SSR clusters, of which 98 were mononucleotide repeats, accounting for 76.6%, 24 dinucleotide repeats, accounting for 18.8%, and 6 trinucleotide repeats, accounting for 4.7%. Notably, the pentanucleotide and hexanucleotide repeats were missing. The SSR type showed significant base preference, with A/T accounting for 89.8% of the total number of mononucleotides and only 10 (10.2%) of the C/G repeats. Among the dinucleotide repeats, AG/CT had the highest frequency, with a total of 14, accounting for 58.3% of the total number of dinucleotides, followed by AT/AT, accounting for 37.5%. The number of trinucleotides was small, and AAG/CTT (4) and AAT/ATT (2) were the main types. The long repeats were concentrated in the A/T mononucleotide type, with a total of 88 repeats of length ≥ 8, among which 3 repeats were of length ≥ 12.

We conducted REPuter [ 44 ] for repeat sequences analysis. We identified a total of 69 repetitive sequences of more than 30 bp in length, collectively spanning 13,846 bp. These repeats ranged in size from 30 bp to 7,589 bp, with a mean length of 220 bp. The longest repeat (7,589 bp) was located between positions 97,617 and 282,205. Twenty-eight inverted repeats (IRs) accounted for 40.5% of all repeats, representing a significantly higher proportion of IRs than observed in N. cadamba .


## Transfer RNA and ribosomal RNA genes

The mitochondrial genome contained 19 tRNA genes spanning 371 bp in total (Table 1 , Supplementary Materials Table S1). Individual tRNA genes ranged in length from 65 bp ( tRNA- Ala, tRNA- Leu) to 87 bp ( tRNA- Ser), with 15 tRNAs (78.9%) mapped to the heavy (H) strand and only four (21.1%) to the light (L) strand, indicating predominant H-strand localization. The mean GC content of tRNAs was 49.6%, slightly higher than the genomic average (44.5%). All tRNAs exhibited canonical cloverleaf secondary structures, with notable structural variations including an extended 15-nt variable loop in tRNA- Tyr, a truncated dihydrouridine (D) arm in tRNA- Ala (retaining only a 3-bp stem), and a shortened TΨC arm in tRNA- Leu (4-bp stem). Non-canonical base pairing: an A-A mismatch in the D-loop of tRNA- Ile and a C–C mismatch in the anticodon stem of tRNA- Asp. Additionally, three rRNA genes ( rrn 26, rrn 18, rrn 5) were identified in the N. macrophylla mitochondrial genome, ranging from 116 bp to 3,428 bp. All rRNA genes resided exclusively on the H-strand. Comparative analysis with N. cadamba revealed high conservation of rrn 26 length (< 0.5% divergence) and fixed positioning of rrn 5 upstream of ccm C.


## Gene editing

We employed Deepred-mt [ 46 ] to identify RNA editing events in the 36 PCGs of N. macrophylla (Supplementary Materials Table S2), and observed a total of 486 potential RNA editing sites with a cutoff value of 0.9, all of which were 'C to U' base edits. The nad 4 gene exhibited 39 potential RNA editing sites, making it the most edited among all mitochondrial genes. This was followed by ccm B and mtt B, which contained 36 and 35 RNA editing events, respectively. In contrast, rps 7 and rps 10 had the lowest frequency of editing sites, with only 2 potential editing sites. High-probability editing sites were typically located at the second position of codons, leading to changes in amino acids. The vast majority of editing events resulted in amino acid changes, with 12 distinct amino acids undergoing alterations: Ser → Phe, Ser → Leu, Pro → Leu, His → Tyr, Pro → Ser, Arg → Trp, Pro → Phe, Arg → Cys, Ala → Val, Leu → Phe, Thr → Ile, and Thr → Met. The most common changes included proline to leucine, serine to phenylalanine, and serine to leucine.


## Gene rearrangement

We compared the mitochondrial genome structure between N. macrophylla and N. cadamba (Fig. 4 ). It was observed that the core functional genes such as ATP synthase, cytochrome c biogenesis, NADH dehydrogenase and ribosomal RNAs were arranged in essentially the same order in the three chromosomes. The ccm C-5srRNA-18srRNA gene fragment underwent rearrangement between N. cadamba 1 and N. macrophylla , whereas the trn W- trn A- rpl 12, sdh 4- cox 3- atp 8, and trn G- trn Q- trn L fragments were completely reversed in N. cadamba 1 and N. macrophylla . Only the nad 5- trn C- trn N- trn Y and trn S2- atp 4- nad 4L gene fragments were completely conserved. The rearrangement events were concentrated in the chromosomal segments where the duplication genes were located, and their positions changed in N. macrophylla and N. cadamba .


> **Fig. 4:** This figure illustrates patterns of gene rearrangement across mitochondrial genes between N. macrophylla and N. cadamba

The sequence of trn A- trn R- trn N- trn S- trn E- trn F gene clusters showed significant dynamics in the transport RNA functional module. In N. macrophylla , the relative positions of trn W and trn C were reversed, and the cluster was located downstream of the NAD 2 gene, and the 18S and 26S rRNA genes changed from the traditional tight tandem arrangement to spaced distribution. In N. cadamba , the trn R and trn N genes were inverted in order and formed a new arrangement pattern of trn A -trn N -trn R -trn S -trn E -trn F. The atp 8 gene was transposed from the typical downstream position of nad 3 to the upstream of the nad 2 gene, forming a new colocalization module with trn D.


## Phylogenetic and synteny analyses

Based on the sequences of 19 PCGs and the priorly selected Yule model, the best nucleotide substitution model was the HKY model, and the molecular clock model chosen was the strict molecular clock model. The species divergence times were calculated using calibration nodes based on the divergence times obtained from Timetree ( http://www.timetree.org/ ). The MCMC method was employed with a chain length of 2 × 10 7 , sampling every 1,000 runs and discarding the last 10% of the aging chain (effective sample size > 200). The maximum clade credibility tree was generated using TreeAnnotator v2.7.1 [ 54 ], and the estimated results were annotated using FigTree v1.4.4 [ 55 ] software. Fig. 5 shows that N. macrophylla exhibited high genetic similarity with N. cadamba , with their phylogenetic node supported by a 100% bootstrap value. The divergent time between N. macrophylla and N. cadamba was estimated as 6.757 (2.472–12.107) Mya. As expected, the congeneric species N. macrophylla and N. cadamba formed a sister clade with full nodal support (100%).


> **Fig. 5:** The phylogenetic tree was constructed based on 19 protein-coding genes. The estimated divergence times are provided at each branch node, with the 95% HPD intervals in parentheses. All terminal branches display 100% bootstrap support values

Figure 6 presents the synteny map generated through MCScanX v.1.0.0 analysis, in which genes were arranged according to their chromosomal positions and connected by color-coded arcs representing syntenic gene pairs. In Fig. 6 A, extensive collinear blocks between the mitochondrial genome of N. macrophylla and the nuclear genome of N. cadamba demonstrated multiple regions of homology, and the length of homologous sequences accounted for 39.13% of the mitochondrial genome. Fig. 6 B shows homologous sequences between mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes of N. macrophylla , and only a small proportion of genome was homologous, accounting for 1.2% of the mitochondrial genome.

The collinearity analysis of the mitochondrial genomes supported the phylogenetic relationships among species in the Neolamarckia genus (Fig. 7 ), with A. thaliana used as the reference species. It is noteworthy that the arrangement of collinear segments was different among the three species, especially between N. macrophylla and N. cadamba , indicating a substantial occurrence of gene rearrangements between them.

## Evolutionary processes underlying mutant genes

Estimates of values indicated that all PCGs were under purifying selection between N. macrophylla and N. cadamba . There were 15 PCGs whose values were not equal to zero, showing the range from 0.001 to 0.9756. About 2.5%−99.9% (1- ) of deleterious mutant alleles were eliminated by natural selection. Specifically, several genes were under very strong purifying selection ( =0.001), including genes related to ATP energy metabolism ( atp 1, atp 6, and atp 8), respiratory chain ( cox 1, cox 3), dehydrogenase coenzyme ( nad 2, nad 3, nad 4, and nad 9), and ribosomal protein L gene complex ( rpl 5). There were a few genes under relatively stronger purifying selection, including rps 3 ( =0.385), ccm C (0.0783), cox 2 (0.1295), and mat R (0.3458). Notably, only one gene, rpl 2, whose K a /K s value was close to 1 (0.9756), was almost neutral or under weak purifying selection. All the remaining 22 genes did not have any substitution ( = =0) and exhibited an extremely conserved feature.


## Discussion

This study provides the first complete mitochondrial genome sequence of N. macrophylla , and offers a genomic resource for this economically and ecologically important species. Several specific conclusions can be drawn from the comparison of genomic structure between N. macrophylla and its close relative N. cadamba (Table 2 ). One is that the mitochondrial genome size of N. cadamba is more than 1.4 times larger than the mitochondrial genome size of N. macrophylla despite a genetically close relationship between them, evidencing the divergent evolution at the genome scale. The mitochondrial genome of N. macrophylla retained more non-coding regions and tRNA sequences after speciation from common ancestors. The second conclusion is that the number of genes per unit genome size is lower in N. macrophylla than in N. cadamba . The non-coding regions contribute to mostly interspecific variation in genome size, but their roles remain to be explored in adaptation to climatic and environmental changes. The third conclusion is that PCGs are highly evolutionary conservative. The analysis provides direct evidence of selection pressures on mitochondrial genes, offering valuable insights into both functional conservation and adaptive changes. The fourth conclusion is concerned with alternative conformations that were evident from genome assembly, also implying significant evolutionary divergence between the two species.

Mechanisms for maintaining the larger mitochondrial genome size in N. cadamba remain unknown. It is conventionally hypothesized that organelle genomes are highly reduced because their genes had been transferred to the nuclear genomes of their hosts after endocytosis of bacteria [ 56 ]. Synteny analysis showed extensive collinear regions between mitochondrial and nuclear genomes and suggests potential functional gene transfer events. The extra PCGs and tRNA genes imply that such a process of gene transference was slower in N. cadamba than in N . macrophylla . This was likely the same situation for the non-coding regions in the mitochondrial genome of N. cadamba . However, it differs from the pattern of chloroplast genome where genome sizes are comparable between N. macrophylla (155,406 bp) [ 34 ] and N. cadamba (154,999 bp) [ 57 ]. The intracellular horizontal gene transfer is far more active for chloroplast genomes compared with mitochondrial and nuclear genomes [ 58 ]. The observed homology patterns between mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes align with documented chloroplast-to-mitochondrion gene transfer phenomena [ 58 , 59 ], supporting the relative independence between mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes in N. macrophylla [ 60 , 61 ]. The contrasting patterns imply that unequal rates of transference occurred from chloroplast and mitochondrial genes to host genomes.

Maintenance of more PCGs and non-coding regions in the mitochondrial genome could enhance plant adaptation to diverse habitats. Loss of several ORF genes could limit plant growth and responses to environmental stress [ 62 ]. Also, loss of other genes, including cox 2, ccm C, nad 3, rps 12, cob , and rpl 5 and multiple tRNA genes (Table 2 ), affects energy metabolism and cellular respiration [ 63 ]. Roles of non-coding regions, accounting for 86.61% of the mitochondrial genome for N. macrophylla and 89.00% for N. cadamba , could be important in regulating gene expression, as implied previous studies in A. thaliana [ 64 , 65 ]. Both N. macrophylla and N. cadamba are sensitive to environmental conditions, especially to soil humidity and temperature [ 66 ]. Limitation in geographical distribution due to the loss of important mitochondrial genes is also reported in plant species, such as Silene [ 67 ] and Nicotiana sylvestris [ 68 ]. The result from comparison of mitochondrial genomes is probably related to the more restrictive distribution of N. macrophylla compared with that of N. cadamba.

Strong purifying selection against all orthologous PCGs effectively impeded evolutionary divergence between N. macrophylla and N. cadamba , which is probably a common phenomenon between closely related species, such as between T. ciliata and T. sinensis [ 69 ] or between Morus atropurpurea and M. cathayana [ 70 ] . Deleterious mutations limit the adaptation of N. macrophylla to diverse habitats, which is a threatened species in Indonesia. Presence of codon usage bias in all codons except for the codon for tryptophan (Trp) implies natural selection occurring at the codon level in the mitochondrial genome of N. macrophylla . This is a similar case to the mitochondrial genome of N. cadamba where only Trp and Met do not have preferences. Both species exhibited comparable AT-biased patterns (55.03% in N. macrophylla vs. 54.82% in N. cadamba ) in their mitochondrial genomes. These comparisons imply highly conservative features in PCGs during the evolutionary processes, but deleterious mutations shape the adaptative evolution of N. macrophylla .

Since strong purifying selection impedes sequence evolution of PCGs in mitochondrial genome, the complex and dynamic mixture of conformations arises from non-coding sequences. One common feature of plant mitochondrial genomes is that their genomes exhibit substantially greater structural complexity than those of animals or fungi [ 71 – 73 ]. N. macrophylla exhibits a significantly higher proportion of coding regions than N. cadamba (13.39% vs 11.00%), suggesting loss of non-coding sequences in N. macrophylla besides the loss of some PCGs. Compared with N. macrophylla , the mitochondrial genome of N. cadamba exhibits a double-circular structure. The mechanism underlying this phenomenon could arise from homologous recombination mediated by repetitive sequences [ 74 ]. Homology-based ectopic recombination between either short or long repetitive sequences within mitochondrial genome can lead to chromosome rearrangements and deletion [ 75 ]. N. cadamba has significantly more abundant short and long repetitive sequences than N. macrophylla. Similar cases were also reported in other plant species, such as Lactuca species [ 75 ], T. macrocarpa with a long repeat sequence [ 69 ], and Cucumis sativus where three circular chromosomes (155 bp, 684 bp and 45 kb) were constructed [ 76 ]. Note that the long repeats exceeding 1,000 bp enhance the probability of gene rearrangements and compromise genomic structural stability. The short repeats (microsatellites and minisatellites), which typically constitute minor genomic fractions, also contribute to variation between the sizes of N. macrophylla and N. cadamba mitochondrial genomes. Furthermore, while the distribution and functional roles of these repeats warrant further investigation, they exhibit potential utility as molecular markers for population genetic studies.

It remains unknown whether the multipartite structure of plant mitochondrial genome has advantage over alternative structure in species adaptation. It is likely that the bipartite configuration in N. cadamba may enhance genomic plasticity, providing a basis for adaption to complex habitats. N. cadamba has a substantially larger mitochondrial genome and contains more genes than N. macrophylla (Table 2 ). Critically, respiratory chain genes are predominantly retained in the main master circle (100% retention), ensuring their functionality. In contrast, some genes are amplified within the subgenomic circle and could help selective expression under environmental stress. This phenomenon was potentially associated with the adaptive advantages of N. cadamba .

Plant mitochondrial genomes are used to construct the phylogeny of older land plant clades due to lower mutation rates compared with nuclear and chloroplast genome [ 71 ]. Our phylogenetic analysis indicates that the divergence time between the two species is approximately 6.757 Mya, with a 95% HPD interval of 2.472–12.107 Mya. This finding supports previous results based on chloroplast genome phylogenies [ 29 ], and confirmed their close relationship as a highly supported (100% nodal support), monophyletic sister group. Both species are more distantly related to Toona genus ( T. ciliata ) and members of the Salicaceae ( Populus ). Since their separation (~ 6.757 Mya), the mitochondrial genomes of the two species could undergo divergent evolutionary trajectories. N. cadamba likely retained the ancestral genomic complexity or acquired genes from nuclear genomes, whereas N. macrophylla underwent drastic genomic reduction. The small genome size in N. macrophylla , concordant with the general pattern of mitochondrial gene losses observed in angiosperms, could reflect an adaptive restriction to relatively narrow geographical habitats and a less adaptive potential. The mitochondrial genome degeneration in N. macrophylla and structural complexity retention in N. cadamba could collectively correlate with their natural distribution ranges.

As the cellular powerhouse involves in numerous vital physiological processes [ 77 – 79 ], plant mitochondria play important roles in development and environmental adaptation. Our findings confirmed that mitochondrial genome exhibited variation in both structure and organization between the two relative species. The partial tRNA deficiency warrants further investigation in species adaptation or gene regulatory mechanism.


## Conclusion

N. macrophylla is a fast-growing species belonging to the Neolamarckia genus of the Rubiaceae family and distributed only in Indonesia. It is one of two species in the Neolamarckia genus, and the other species is N. cadamba with much wider geographical distribution. N. cadamba was recognized as a miraculous tree owing to its multiple properties in wood quality, forage for livestock and material for medicinal purposes. However, little is known about N. macrophylla . In this study, we sequenced the mitochondrial genome of N. macrophylla for the first time and assembled it into one circular structure. The genome was 289,793 bp in length and contained 36 PCGs, 19 tRNA and 3 rRNA genes, with the GC content of 44.97%. There were 246 C-to-U RNA editing across 36 PCGs, with high editing sites occurred in genes nad 5, nad 4, nad 7, nad 2, ccm C and ccm B. The repetitive sequences were dominated by mononucleotide repeats, followed by dinucleotide repeats. Comparative genomic analysis indicated that, compared with N. cdamba , N. macrophylla had a smaller genome size, higher gene density per site, less protein- and tRNA-coding genes, and an alternative conformation. Homologous sequences accounted for 39.13% of its length in alignment with the nuclear genome of N. cadamba , and 1.2% in alignment with the chloroplast genome of N. macrophylla . The two species were divergent at about 6.757(2.472–12.107) Mya, indicating their close genetic relationship. Strong purifying selection against all PCGs ( =0 ~ 0.9756) were detected, which impeded evolutionary divergence between these two species. The overall results enrich the genomic resources of the Neolamarckia genus in the Rubiaceae family and confirmed divergent structure of mitochondrial genomes between the two species in genus Neolamarckia .
