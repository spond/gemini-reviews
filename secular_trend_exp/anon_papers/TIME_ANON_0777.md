## Abstract

Hypercholesterolemia is a leading cause of various cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-Coenzyme A reductase (Hmgcr) is the rate-limiting enzyme in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway. Previous studies investigated the regulation of Hmgcr expression under various pathophysiological conditions. However, its expression under hypoxia, an important player in the pathogenesis of CVD, is poorly understood. The present study investigated the hepatic expression of Hmgcr and Hif-1α in Spontaneously hypertensive rats [SHR] and its normotensive control, Wistar Kyoto [WKY] rats. Interestingly, hepatic Hmgcr expression was diminished while Hif-1α expression was elevated in SHR. In cultured rat liver cells, the Hmgcr promoter activity/transcript/protein and intracellular cholesterol levels were diminished after hypoxia treatment. Further, while Hif-1α /Runx3 transcript and protein levels were enhanced, Srebf levels decreased upon hypoxia. Knock-down of Hif-1α abrogated the hypoxia-mediated effect on Hmgcr, intracellular cholesterol levels, and the expression of Srebf and Runx3. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays showed binding of Hif-1α to both endogenous SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr/Srebf/Runx3 domains with similar promoter occupancies. However, differential binding of Hif-1α to Srebf and Runx3 promoter domains were observed from ChIP assay. While HIF-1α showed a negative correlation with HMGCR / SREBF transcript levels, it correlated positively with RUNX3 . Additionally, ChIP assays demonstrated differential binding of Srebf and Runx3 to SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter domains upon hypoxic stress. Taken together, this study elucidates the regulatory role of Hif-1α in modulating Hmgcr expression and intracellular cholesterol levels via Srebf and Runx3 under hypoxic stress. These findings provide new mechanistic insights underlying cholesterol homeostasis under hypoxia.

## Introduction

Corticosteroids play a crucial role in the regulation of blood pressure (BP) [ 1 ]. As cholesterol is the precursor for corticosteroid synthesis, cholesterol homeostasis may be essential in BP regulation. Liver is the major player in maintaining cholesterol homeostasis in the body [ 2 ] since it is involved in the synthesis of cholesterol and high-density lipoprotein particles, clearance of low-density lipoprotein particles and chylomicrons, and plays a role in the reverse cholesterol transport [ 3 ]. Plasma cholesterol concentration is an important determinant for the onset and progression of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), as dyslipidemia may lead to numerous disease states such as atherosclerosis, coronary artery disease, hypertension, obesity, and type 2 diabetes [ 4 – 6 ]. 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-Coenzyme A reductase (Hmgcr), the rate-limiting enzyme in the mevalonate or cholesterol biosynthesis pathway is indispensable in dyslipidemia and thus in the pathogenesis of CVDs [ 7 , 8 ].

Hmgcr, an endoplasmic reticulum (ER) trans-membrane glycoprotein, is expressed ubiquitously [ 9 ]. Hmgcr gene expression is regulated at transcriptional and post-translational levels by sterols and non-sterols (end-products of the mevalonate metabolic pathway) via feedback control mechanisms. Insulin-induced genes (Insig1 and Insig2), sterol regulatory element binding proteins (SREBPs) and SREBP cleavage-activating protein (SCAP) [ 10 ] are critical factors involved in the feedback control regulation of Hmgcr . Our previous study showed that transcription factors, Srebf and Runx3 regulated rat Hmgcr expression under both basal and cholesterol-modulated conditions [ 11 ]. Similarly, transcription factors c-Fos, n-Myc and Max regulate mouse Hmgcr promoter activity in various cultured cells [ 12 ]. Additionally, c-Fos, c-Jun, NF-κB and HSP-70 regulate Hmgcr expression in mouse models subjected to renal ischemia [ 13 ]. Furthermore, hypoxia stimulates the degradation of Hmgcr through hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-mediated induction of Insigs and accumulation of lanosterol [ 14 ]. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms of transcriptional regulation of Hmgcr under conditions of hypoxia are poorly understood.

Hypoxia is a pathological condition associated with various diseases, including CVDs, atherosclerosis and cancer [ 15 – 17 ]. Furthermore, hypoxia plays a role in regulating the enzymes involved in the cholesterol synthesis pathway for maintaining cholesterol homeostasis, the perturbation of which contributes to the onset of various diseases [ 14 , 18 ]. Hypoxia stimulates several adaptive changes, which are mediated transcriptionally by a family of HIFs [ 19 ]. The cellular and developmental response to hypoxia is regulated by HIF-1 [ 20 ]. HIF-1 is a heterodimer transcription factor consisting of two subunits: a HIF-1α subunit which is regulated based on oxygen levels and a HIF-1β subunit which is expressed constitutively [ 21 ]. Under normoxic conditions, HIF-1α undergoes ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation upon recognition by the Von Hippel-Lindau tumor suppressor protein. However, under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α is stable and dimerizes with HIF-1β to form a heterodimer complex that binds to hypoxia-response elements (HREs) within the promoter regions of target genes, thereby activating transcription. Several studies report that HIF-1α regulates several genes involved in vascularization and energy metabolism [ 22 ]. Recent reports indicate that during hypoxia, HIF-1α modifies lipid metabolism by enhancing lipogenesis and endocytosis of lipoproteins, while inhibiting β-oxidation contributing to metabolic disorders. A few of the genes that are modulated in this process are PPARγ, LRP1, VLDLR, GLS2, FASN, and PGC-1α [ 23 – 25 ]. Interestingly, HIF-1α was shown to play an indirect role in regulating most of the genes that are involved in lipid metabolism under hypoxia [ 19 ]. Likewise, HIF-1α has been reported to play an indirect role in the degradation of Hmgcr by activating the insulin-induced genes Insig1 and Insig2 [ 26 ]. However, the molecular mechanisms involving HIF-1α-mediated Hmgcr expression remain unknown.

In the present study, we report that differential interactions of Srebf and Runx3 with the Hmgcr promoter domain diminished the expression of Hmgcr under hypoxia. Furthermore, we also demonstrate that Hif-1α regulates the transcription factors, Srebf and Runx3, which in turn can modulate hepatic Hmgcr gene expression and intracellular cholesterol level. To the best of our knowledge, this study for the first time, unravels the role of Srebf and Runx3 in the regulation of Hmgcr under hypoxia.


## Materials and methods


## Tissue samples from rat models of cardiometabolic disease

The Institutional Animal Ethics Committee approved all animal procedures at the Indian Institute of Technology Madras (Approval No. 1007/C/06/CPCSEA/IITM/BT/19/NRM-[YEAR]). Spontaneously hypertensive rat (SHR) is a widely recognized model for cardiometabolic disease [ 27 – 31 ]. In 1963, Okamoto and Aoki mated a male Wistar rat with high blood pressure (150–175 mmHg) and a female with marginally elevated blood pressure (130–140 mmHg) to create a hypertensive strain. After repeated breeding selections, they developed SHR strains which displayed consistently elevated blood pressure [ 32 ]. Liver tissues were collected from 6–8 weeks old male SHR and WKY rats ( n = 9 each) at Central Drug Research Institute (CDRI, Lucknow, India). To minimize the effect on gene expression by age-related confounding factors, tissues were isolated from young rats. Liver tissues from six SHR and six WKY animals were collected in RNAlater solution and stored at −80 °C until isolation of DNA, RNA and proteins. The remaining liver tissues (from three SHR and three WKY animals) were snap-frozen for endogenous chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays.


## Generation of rat Hmgcr (r Hmgcr ) promoter-firefly luciferase reporter plasmids

Rat genomic DNA was isolated from the liver tissues obtained from CDRI, Lucknow, India. The SHR and WKY Hmgcr promoter regions were amplified by PCR using Phusion® High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Finnzymes, USA) and cloned into pGL3-basic vector as described previously [ 11 ]. The authenticity of the promoter-reporter plasmids was confirmed by PCR and DNA sequencing. Three variations [an 11-bp insertion (−398_−388insTGCGGTCCTCC), A-405G and C-62 T] between the SHR and WKY promoters were detected [ 11 ].


## Data mining from GTEx, and EMBL-EBI expression databases for correlation analysis

HIF-1α , HMGCR , RUNX3, and SREBF expression data for various human tissues were retrieved from the GTEx portal ( https://gtexportal.org/home/ ), and expression data for both human and rat tissues were obtained from the EMBL-EBI expression atlas ( http://www.ebi.ac.uk/gxa ). Only those tissues which showed at least 10% of HIF-1α expression compared to that of the esophagus (with the highest TPM = 158) were selected for analysis from the GTEx portal and TPM cut-off of at least 1 for HIF-1α , HMGCR , RUNX3, and SREBF were selected from

the EMBL-EBI expression atlas for analysis. In the case of rat expression data, tissues that showed at least 10% of HIF-1α expression compared to the thymus (with the highest TPM = 114) were used for correlation analysis. The details of samples are provided in Table S1 , S2 , and S3 .


## In silico analysis of SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter domains for putative Hif-1α binding sites

Six online prediction tools viz., ConSite [ 33 ], LASAGNA [ 34 ], PROMO [ 35 ], TF-BIND [ 36 ], P-MATCH [ 37 ], and MATCH [ 38 ] were used to identify the hypoxia-response element (HRE) sites of Hif-1α on the SHR/WKY- Hmgcr/Srebf/Runx3 promoter. HREs predicted by atleast three programs were shortlisted for experimental validation. Primers were designed for the selected HREs and analyzed.


## Cell culture

Buffalo rat liver fibroblasts (BRL 3A) and Human hepatoma (HuH-7) cell lines were procured from the National Center for Cell Sciences, Pune, India. The cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagles Medium (DMEM) containing high Glucose, Sodium Pyruvate, 4.0 mM L-Glutamine (HyClone, USA), supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS), streptomycin sulfate (100 mg/ml) and penicillin G (100 U/ml) (Invitrogen, USA) in 25 cm 2 tissue culture flasks (Tarsons, India) in a humidified incubator (Thermo Scientific, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO 2 . For maintenance and sub-culturing of the cells, the monolayer of cells was washed with 1X PBS (phosphate buffer saline) (Invitrogen, USA) and trypsinized for 2 min. DMEM + 10% FBS medium was added to neutralize trypsin, and cells were pelleted at [YEAR] rpm for 2 min. The cells were re-suspended in fresh DMEM + 10% FBS and were either dispensed on 60 mm dishes or cultured in 12-well plates (Tarsons, India) for different experiments.


## Transient transfections and luciferase-reporter assays

For transient transfections, BRL 3A cells were grown up to 60% confluency in 12-well plates, and co-transfections were carried out in triplicates with 500 ng/well of SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter-reporter plasmids and 300 ng/well of Renilla luciferase expression plasmid (pRL-TK plasmid; Promega, USA) using Targefect F1 transfection reagent (Targeting Systems, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The ratio of DNA: F1 was 1:2 [ 11 ]. Renilla luciferase expression plasmid was employed as an internal control to assess the transfection efficiency. The cells were lysed with 200 µl of cell lysis buffer (100 mM Phosphate buffer pH 7.8, 1 mM DTT, 0.1% Triton X-100) after six hours of hypoxic treatment (95% argon plus 5% CO2) [ 39 ]. Luciferase assay was carried out with some modifications of a previously described method [ 40 ]. In brief, 70 µl of cell lysates were taken in RIA vials, and luciferase assay was performed using 100 µl luciferase assay buffer (100 mM Tris–acetate pH 7.8, 1 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 10 mM magnesium acetate, 3 mM ATP and 100 µM beetle luciferin potassium salt) per sample; RLTK assay was carried out using 100 µl RLTK assay buffer (1.1 M sodium chloride, 2.2 mM EDTA, 0.22 M dihydrogen potassium phosphate buffer pH 5.1, 0.44 mg/ml BSA, 1.43 µM coelentrazine) per sample. The intensity of light produced was measured using AutoLumat Plus LB953 luminometer (Berthold, Germany) for 10 s. Bradford (Bio-Rad, USA) assays were carried out with 10 µl of cell lysates to analyze the total protein content. The luciferase activities were expressed as firefly luciferase activity/ Renilla luciferase activity or luciferase activity/total protein.

For the siRNA treatments, BRL 3A cells were seeded in 12-well plates and co-transfected with SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter reporter construct (500 ng/well) and 10 nM of Runx3 siRNA or Srebf siRNA (IDT, USA) or 10 nM Negative control (Sigma Aldrich, USA) [ 11 ]. The cells were subjected to hypoxic treatment for 6 h and lysed for measuring firefly and Renilla luciferase activities and performing Bradford assay. The luciferase activities were expressed as firefly luciferase activity/ Renilla luciferase activity or luciferase activity/total protein.

To investigate the role of Hif-1α in regulating Hmgcr gene expression, HIF-1α shRNA expression plasmids were co-transfected in BRL 3A cells in a dose-dependent manner (0, 250, 500 and 1000 ng/well) along with SHR and WKY promoter-reporter plasmids (500 ng/well) using Targefect F1 [ 39 ]. The total amount of plasmid DNA was balanced with an empty vector plasmid pcDNA3.0. The cells were revived 5 h after transfection and lysed 24 h post-transfection for measuring firefly and Renilla luciferase activities and performing Bradford assay. The luciferase activities were expressed as firefly luciferase activity/ Renilla luciferase activity or luciferase activity/total protein.


## Isolation of RNA and real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated from liver tissues of 6-8 weeks old SHR and WKY rats using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and the isolated RNA was purified using Nucleospin columns (Machery-Nagel, Germany). RNA (~ 2 µg) was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using a High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Takara Bio Inc, Japan) and random hexamers. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed by using Hif-1α gene-specific primers; FP: 5'-GTTTACTAAAGGACAAGTCACC-3'and RP: 5'-TTCTGTTTGTTGAAGGGAG-3'and Hmgcr gene-specific primers; FP: 5'-ACCTGCTGCCATAAACTGGAT-3'and RP: 5'-ACCACCTTGGCTGGAATGAC-3' with DyNAmo™ HS SYBR® Green qPCR kit (Thermo Fisher, USA) in Quantstudio7 real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA). To normalize Hif-1α and Hmgcr expression, Vinculin abundance was measured using FP: 5’-CGCTGGCGTCCATAGACTC-3'and RP: 5'- CCTAGCATTTTGCAGGTTCCTA-3'and β-actin abundance was measured using FP: 5’-GCTGTGCTATGTTGCCCTAG-3'and RP: 5'- CGCTCATTGCCGATAGTG-3'. Hif-1α and Hmgcr expression in SHR and WKY tissues were normalized to Vinculin or β-actin abundance and the fold difference was calculated by 2 −ΔΔCt method as described previously [ 39 ].

Total RNA was also isolated from BRL 3A cells subjected to different transfection/treatment conditions using TRIzol. 250 µl of TRIzol was added to each sample and incubated at room temperature for 5 min, followed by the addition of 50 µl chloroform, vortexed, and incubated at room temperature for 3 min. Following incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 12000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The colorless aqueous phase was transferred into a fresh tube and 500 µl of isopropanol was added to precipitate RNA, followed by incubation at room temperature for 10 min. The samples were centrifuged at 12000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was rinsed using 75% ethanol by centrifugation at 7500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting RNA pellet was air-dried for 10 min at room temperature. The RNA pellet was re-suspended in RNase-free water (Himedia, India) and the concentration of RNA was measured by spectrophotometer (BioPhotometer Plus, Germany).

Gene-specific primers (Table 1 ) and DyNAmo™ HS SYBR® Green qPCR kit were used to quantify the transcript levels of various genes by real-time PCR analysis. ## Total protein isolation from cell lines

After hypoxic treatment or siRNA/co-transfection treatment, BRL 3A cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed with ice-cold lysis buffer (100 mM Phosphate buffer pH 7.8, 1 mM DTT, 0.1% Triton X-100) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (PIC) (Sigma Aldrich, USA) and phenyl methyl sulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). Adherent cells were scraped off from the dish using a cold plastic cell scraper and the cell suspension was then gently transferred into a pre-cooled centrifuge tube. The samples were left in ice for 5–10 min for lysing the cells, sonicated at an amplitude of 30 Hz for 1 min with 10 s on and off cycle and spun for 20 min at 15,000 rpm at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected without disturbing the pellet and stored at −80 °C until further use.


## Protein isolation from the liver of SHR and WKY rats

About 40 mg of liver tissues from SHR and WKY rats were washed twice with 1X PBS (supplemented with PIC) and homogenized in 1.0 ml of Radioimmunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer [50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP40/Triton-X 100, 1% sodium-deoxycholate, 1 mM EDTA, 0.1% SDS] supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and PIC using Dounce Homogenizer (glass-Teflon homogenizer). To obtain uniform suspension of proteins, samples were then sonicated on ice using Labsonic-M Ultrasonic Homogenizer (Sartorius, Germany) with 30 s on and off cycle for at least 5 min. Homogenized samples were centrifuged for 30 min at 4 °C at a speed of 15,000 rpm and the supernatants were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until further use. Bradford assays were performed to estimate the protein concentration.


## Western blot analysis

Expression of Hmgcr, Hif-1α, Srebf, and Runx3 proteins in BRL 3A cells post hypoxia or siRNA treatment was determined by western blotting. For detecting Hmgcr expression equal amounts of protein samples (~ 30–50 μg) were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min; for analyzing Hif-1α, Srebf, and Runx3 expression, the samples were denatured at 99 °C for 5 min. The protein samples were electrophoresed by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membrane (Pall Life Sciences, USA). The membrane was then blocked using 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 2 h and incubated with a specific primary antibody, i.e., either Hmgcr (ab174830) at 1:2500 dilution or Hif-1α (ab2185) at 1:1000 dilution or Runx3 (ab11905) at 1:1000 dilution from Abcam, UK or Srebf (sc-13551, Santa Cruz Biotechnologies, USA) at 1:2500 dilution or Vinculin (v9131, Sigma Aldrich, USA) at 1:10,000 dilution, overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking. After washing with 1X Tris Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (1X TBST), the membrane was incubated with secondary antibody with HRP-conjugate specific for either rabbit (Jackson, USA; Immunoresearch No. 111–035-003 at 1:3000 dilution for Runx3, and Hmgcr) or mouse (Jackson Immunoresearch No. 115–035-003 at 1:3000 dilution for Hif-1α and Srebf or 1:5000 dilution for Vinculin) for 1 h at room temperature. The blots were incubated with Ponceau solution for 5 min and were visualized in a ChemiDoc Gel imaging system iBright CL1000 (Invitrogen, USA) to image the total proteins in each lane. The proteins of interest were detected by using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Bio-Rad, USA), and ChemiDoc Gel imaging system. The relative abundance of each of these proteins was normalized with total proteins and Vinculin and quantified using NIH ImageJ software (ImageJ, USA).


## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

To test the interactions of Srebf and Runx3 with the SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoters under conditions of hypoxia, ChIP assays were performed as described previously [ 41 ]. Briefly, HuH-7 cells were grown in 100 mm dishes, transfected with SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter constructs (10 µg) using Targefect F1 transfection reagent and then subjected to hypoxia treatment for 6 h. The cells were cross-linked using 1% formaldehyde in 1X PBS, which was terminated after a stipulated time using 0.125 M of glycine. After cell and nuclear lysis, chromatin was isolated and sheared to generate chromatin fragments of approximately 200–500 base pairs by repeating 10 s on and 20 s off cycles 25 times at 30 Hz amplitude using Ultrasonic Homogenizer (Sartorius, Germany). The sheared chromatin was precleared with 60 µl of Rec protein G-Sepharose beads (Invitrogen, USA) before using it for precipitation with specific antibodies. Overnight immunoprecipitation reactions were performed using 5 µg of ChIP-grade antibodies against Srebf (sc-13551X) or Runx3 (sc-30197X) and non-immune IgG mouse (Sigma-Aldrich, #15831) was used as a negative control. After various washing steps, immunoprecipitated chromatin and input DNA were reverse cross-linked, treated with RNase A/proteinase K, washed with ethanol, air dried, and stored at −20 °C until use. Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out using immunoprecipitated chromatin as a template and the following specific primers: FP1: 5'-ACTACTTCCCCGCAGATCTC-3'; RP1: 5’-CCCCGAACTCAGAATTTGACC-3'. Input DNA and water were used as positive and negative controls in the PCR reactions. Real-time PCR amplification (157 bp) was carried out by using DyNAmo™ HS SYBR® Green qPCR kit. The fold enrichment method was used to calculate the enrichment of the promoter domain in the immunoprecipitated chromatin. C t values were normalized to their corresponding IgG control, which accounted for the background/non-specific signal. To ascertain the specificity of the primers used for targeting the specific locus of the transcription factors binding site, we designed primers FP2: 5'-ACCCGACTAGCCATTGGTTG-3'; RP2: 5'- GGCCGCCAATAAGGAAGGAT-3'amplifying the region which contains the negative control locus.

To study the interaction of Hif-1α with the SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr/Srebf/Runx3 promoter in liver tissues, ChIP assays were performed as described previously with few modifications [ 42 ]. Approximately, 25 mg of liver tissue was homogenized and cross-linked in 1% formaldehyde for 15 min. To arrest fixation, 150 mM glycine was added to the cross-linked tissues and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. The fixed tissues were washed with ice-cold PBS and homogenized in cold ChIP cell lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 140 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 0.5% NP-40, 0.25% Triton X-100 and protease inhibitor) using a Dounce homogenizer followed by incubation on ice for 20 min. Further, nuclei were pelleted and re-suspended in nuclear lysis buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl at pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 EGTA mM, and 0.2% SDS). Nuclear chromatin was fragmented by sonication at 30 Hz amplitude with 30 s on and off pulse cycle for 1 h. Sheared DNA (10 μg) was incubated with 5 μg of ChIP grade antibodies against Hif-1α (mouse monoclonal, ab2185) and non-immune anti-mouse IgG (Sigma-Aldrich, #I5831) overnight at 4 °C. Protein A Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher, USA, # 10001D) were added to the sheared DNA-antibody mix and placed on a rotator for 1 h followed by multiple washes with ChIP Wash buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 500 mM LiCl, 0.1% NP-40 and 1% deoxycholic acid) at 4 °C. Immunoprecipitated chromatin and input were reverse cross-linked, treated with RNase A and proteinase K, washed with ethanol, air dried, and stored at −20 °C until use. Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out using immunoprecipitated chromatin as template and HRE-specific and negative control primers (Table 2 ). ## Visualization of intracellular cholesterol in BRL 3A cells by filipin staining

For visualizing intracellular cholesterol levels, BRL 3A cells were subjected to filipin staining as per our standardized protocol [ 43 ]. In brief, those cells were exposed to hypoxic stress for 6 h or transfected with HIF-1α shRNA (1000 ng/2 ml medium) or transfected with 10 nM control/Srebf/Runx3 siRNA followed by hypoxia treatment for 6 h. The cells were washed with 1X PBS for three times and then fixed with 3% (v/v) formaldehyde (in 1X PBS) for 15 min at room temperature followed by quenching using glycine (1.5 mg/ml in 1X PBS). Subsequently, the cells were washed thrice with 1X PBS, stained with 50 µg/ml (in 1X PBS) Filipin III ready-made solution (SAE0087, Sigma Aldrich, USA) and incubated in dark at room temperature for 1 h. The stained cells were imaged using an Olympus IX83 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). Quantification for total corrected fluorescence per cell was carried out using ImageJ (ImageJ, USA).


## Data presentation and statistics

All the transient transfections using promoter-reporter constructs or co-transfection assays were carried out at least 3 times, and results were expressed as mean ± S.E of triplicates. The significance level was determined by student's t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Newman-Keuls multiple comparisons post-test as appropriate using the Prism 7 program (GraphPad Software, USA).


## Results


## Hif-1α and Hmgcr are expressed differentially in the liver tissues of SHR and WKY animals

In view of the previous reports on the enhanced expression of Hif-1α levels in brain and heart tissues of SHR when compared to WKY rats [ 44 , 45 ], we investigated the expression of Hif-1α in the liver tissues of SHR and WKY. qPCR analysis revealed that the transcript levels of Hif-1α were elevated in liver tissues of SHR (~ 1.7-fold, p < 0.0001 and ~ 2.4-fold, p < 0.0001 normalized to Vinculin and β-actin, respectively; Fig. 1 A and S1 A) as compared to WKY. In corroboration, the Hif-1α protein level was also higher in SHR liver tissues when compared to WKY (~ 2.9-fold, p < 0.0001; Fig. 1 B and C). Consistent with our previous results, we observed a reduction in the expression of Hmgcr in the liver tissues of SHR at the mRNA (~ 2.7-fold, p < 0.0001 and ~ 2.9-fold, p < 0.0001 normalized to Vinculin and β-actin, respectively; Fig. 1 D and S1 B) and protein levels (~ 2.1-fold, p < 0.0001; Fig. 1 E and F) when compared to WKY rats. ## Effect of hypoxic stress on Hmgcr expression

To test if hypoxic stress influences the SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter activity, BRL 3A cells were subjected to hypoxia. Previous studies have observed the effect of hypoxia/ischemia at various time points like 1, 6, 12, 24 h in different experimental models [ 46 – 51 ]. However, based on the conclusions drawn from our time course studies, we decided to choose a 6 h time-point for the subsequent experiments. Interestingly, BRL 3 A cells transfected with SHR/WKY -Hmgcr promoter constructs showed reduced Hmgcr promoter activities upon hypoxic stress. The treatment diminished both the SHR- (~ 1.5-fold, p < 0.001; Fig. 2 A) and WKY- Hmgcr promoter activities (~ 1.3-fold, p < 0.01; Fig. 2 A) when the firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity. Similar results were observed when the SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter luciferase activities were normalized with the total proteins (Fig. S2 A). In corroboration, intracellular cholesterol levels were also diminished upon hypoxic stress and was confirmed by filipin staining (Fig. 2 B and C). Western blot analysis revealed that the Hif-1α expression was elevated (~ 2.2-fold, p < 0.0001), whereas the endogenous Hmgcr protein level was decreased (~ 1.9-fold, p < 0.0001) upon hypoxic treatment. Furthermore, as transcriptional factors Srebf and Runx3 were found to play a role in regulating Hmgcr promoter activity, we also checked the endogenous level of these proteins upon hypoxic stress. Interestingly, the Runx3 protein level was elevated (~ 1.8-fold, p < 0.0001), while the Srebf level was diminished (~ 2.7-fold, p < 0.0001) upon hypoxic treatment (Fig. 2 D and E). Normalization of the proteins of interests with total proteins showed directionally-concordant results (Fig. S2 B). Consistently, the transcript levels of Hmgcr , Runx3 and Srebf showed similar trends upon hypoxia treatment. Upon hypoxia, Hif-1α and Runx3 transcript levels were elevated by ~ 2.1-fold, p < 0.0001 and ~ 2.2-fold, p < 0.0001, respectively, whereas the Hmgcr and Srebf transcript levels were diminished by ~ 2.7-fold, p < 0.0001 and ~ 2-fold, p < 0.0001, respectively when normalized to Vinculin (Fig. 2 F). Similar trends in the transcript levels of Hif-1α , Hmgcr , Runx3 and Srebf were observed when normalized to β-actin as well (Fig. S2 C). It is interesting to adduce that there is a regulatory control over the above-mentioned transcription factors (viz. Runx3 and Srebf) upon hypoxic stress even at the transcriptional level. ## Correlation between HIF-1α and HMGCR /SREBF/RUNX3 in human and rat tissues

Since the initial findings point to an inverse relationship between Hif-1α and Hmgcr expression, we performed a correlation analysis between Hmgcr and Hif-1α across various human and rat tissues. Furthermore, as transcription factors, Runx3 and Srebf were found to play a role in regulating Hmgcr promoter activity, we analyzed whether there is a correlation between Hif-1α and Runx3 / Srebf expression. Correlation analysis was performed using the transcriptome data of various human tissues mined from the GTEx portal and EMBL-EBI expression atlas. The transcript level of HMGCR showed a significantly negative correlation with HIF-1α expression in human tissues from GTEx portal (Pearson r = −0.4429, p = 0.0183; Fig. 3 A), EMBL-EBI expression atlas (Pearson r = −0.3755, p = 0.0447; Fig. 3 B) as well as in rat tissues from EMBL-EBI expression atlas (Pearson r = −0.7318, p = 0.0161; Fig. 3 C). On the other hand, correlation analysis between transcript levels of HIF-1α and SREBF revealed a significantly negative correlation in human tissues from GTEx portal (Pearson r = −0.2814, p = 0.0477; Fig. 3 D), EMBL-EBI expression atlas (Pearson r = −0.3345, p = 0.0430; Fig. 3 E) as well as in rat tissues from EMBL-EBI expression atlas (Pearson r = −0.6664, p = 0.0354; Fig. 3 F). However, a positive correlation was observed between the transcript level of HIF-1α and RUNX3 in human tissues from GTEx portal (Pearson r = 0.4155, p = 0.0348; Fig. 3 G), EMBL-EBI expression atlas (Pearson r = 0.7478, p = 0.0013; Fig. 3 H) as well as in rat tissues from EMBL-EBI expression atlas (Pearson r = 0.7737, p = 0.0413; Fig. 3 I). ## Role of Hif-1α in the regulation of Hmgcr

HIF-1α is significantly up-regulated under hypoxia and it modulates the transcription of many genes [ 52 ]. To test whether Hif-1α has a role in Hmgcr gene regulation, we performed co-transfection experiments in BRL 3A cells. Knock-down of Hif-1α augmented the SHR- Hmgcr promoter activity (~ 1.9-fold, p < 0.0001) and WKY- Hmgcr promoter activity (~ 1.7-fold, p < 0.0001) in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4 A). Similar results were observed when the SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter luciferase activities were normalized with the total protein levels (Fig. S3 A). Consistently, the endogenous Hmgcr protein level showed a dose-dependent enhancement upon Hif-1α knock-down (~ 2.8-fold, p < 0.0001 for SHR- Hmgcr promoter and ~ 2.2-fold, p < 0.0001 for WKY- Hmgcr promoter; Fig. 4 B). The down-regulation of Hif-1α level was confirmed by western blotting. We observed that the Hif-1α protein level was diminished up to ~ 2.6-fold ( p < 0.0001) and ~ 2.3-fold ( p < 0.0001) in cells transfected with SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr promoter, respectively (Fig. 4 C). Interestingly, the Runx3 protein level was also reduced up to ~ 2.8-fold ( p < 0.0001) and ~ 2.6-fold, ( p < 0.0001) for SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr promoter transfected cells, respectively (Fig. 4 D); on the other hand, the Srebf level was increased up to ~ 2.8-fold ( p < 0.0001) and ~ 3.6-fold ( p < 0.0001) for SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr promoter, respectively (Fig. 4 E). Normalization of the proteins of interests with total proteins showed directionally-concordant results (Fig. S3 B). In corroboration, the intracellular cholesterol level also increased upon Hif-1α knock-down and was confirmed by filipin staining (Fig. 4 F and G). To assess the extent of interactions of Hif-1α with the SHR and WKY- Hmgcr promoters, in - silico analysis was performed to predict the hypoxia-response elements (HRE) on SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter domains. We used six independent prediction programs: ConSite, LASAGNA, PROMO, TF-BIND, P-MATCH and MATCH. The analysis revealed a total of 4 HRE at positions −182 bp (HRE 1), −452 bp (HRE 2), −545 bp (HRE 3) and −922 bp (HRE 4). However, HRE 1 was predicted by only one tool and was excluded from further analysis. All six tools predicted HRE 2, while three programs predicted HRE 3 and HRE 4 and so HRE 2, HRE 3 and HRE 4 were considered for further experimental validation (Fig. 5 A). Primers were designed for each of the HRE’s and a negative control which was not harboring any HRE (Fig. 5 B); however, there was no amplification for HRE 3. qPCR analysis using chromatin from SHR and WKY liver tissues for HRE 2 demonstrated significant enrichment of the endogenous SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr domain up to ~ 5.3-fold, p < 0.0001 and ~ 5.1-fold, p < 0.0001, respectively with respect to IgG. Similarly, for HRE 4, there was significant enrichment of the endogenous SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr domain up to ~ 3.1-fold, p < 0.0001 and ~ 3-fold, p < 0.0001, respectively with respect to IgG. However, no differential binding of Hif-1α at HRE 2 and HRE 4 was observed between SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr domains (Fig. 5 C and D). We designed primers that were not predicted to harbor HRE to prove the specificity of the primers used for targeting the binding sites. There was no fold-enrichment of the SHR/WKY- Hmgcr domain compared to IgG (Fig. 5 E). These findings suggest that Hif-1α regulates Hmgcr expression but it may not contribute to the differential expression of Hmgcr between SHR and WKY. ## Role of Srebf/Runx3 in the regulation of Hmgcr under hypoxic stress

To test whether the Hmgcr activity under hypoxia is regulated by Srebf or Runx3 in BRL 3A cells, the SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter activity was assessed after siRNA-mediated down-regulation of Srebf or Runx3. We did co-transfection experiments with different concentrations of Srebf/Runx3 siRNA (2.5, 5, 10, and 20 nM) to identify the concentration with the most significant knock-down effect. 10 and 20 nM of siRNA gave significant results and hence we used 10 nM siRNA concentration for further experiments (Figs. S4 A and B). Consistent with our previous observations, Srebf down-regulation showed significant reduction of SHR/WKY- Hmgcr activity (~ 1.3-fold, p < 0.01 and ~ 1.5-fold, p < 0.01 for SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr promoter, respectively; Fig. 6 A). In contrast, Runx3 down-regulation showed significant enhancement of SHR/WKY- Hmgcr activity (~ 1.4-fold, p < 0.01 and ~ 1.6-fold, p < 0.01 for SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr promoter, respectively; Fig. 6 B). Hypoxia treatment further diminished the promoter activity of SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr by ~ 1.1-fold, p < 0.01 and ~ 1.3-fold, p < 0.01, respectively for Srebf siRNA co-transfection (Fig. 6 A) and by ~ 1.2-fold, p < 0.01 and ~ 1.3-fold, p < 0.01, respectively for Runx3 siRNA co-transfection (Fig. 6 B). Further, upon co-transfection with Srebf/Runx3 siRNA, the cells exhibited reduced or enhanced SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr promoter activities after normalizing the luciferase activities with the total protein content. Similarly, a reduction in promoter activity was observed after hypoxia treatment when the luciferase activities were normalized to total proteins (Figs. S5 A and B). The knock-down of endogenous Srebf/Runx3 level and hypoxia treatment, validated by western blot analysis, altered the endogenous Hmgcr protein level (Fig. 6 A and B). Of note, directionally-concordant results were observed when the proteins of interests were normalized with respect to the total proteins (Figs. S5 C and D). In corroboration, intracellular cholesterol levels were elevated/diminished upon Runx3/Srebf siRNA treatment, respectively (Fig. S6 ). ## Discussion


## Overview

Cholesterol is crucial for the normal functioning of cells by being a component of cell membranes and a precursor of several steroid hormones, bile acids, and oxysterols [ 53 ]. The synthesis of one cholesterol molecule consumes 11 molecules of oxygen, thus making it an oxygen-intensive process [ 54 ]. Cells exhibit different adaptive mechanisms to counteract hypoxic stress. Some of these adaptations require metabolic modifications, including modulation of the expression of genes involved in cholesterol biosynthesis [ 55 , 56 ]. The prominent among the list is Hmgcr , the rate-limiting enzyme of the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway, which is responsible for the synthesis of sterols and non-sterol isoprenoid compounds [ 8 ]. In the current study, we have tried to understand the molecular mechanisms involved in the transcriptional regulation of Hmgcr under hypoxia.


## Elevation of Hif-1α levels in various SHR tissues

HIF-1 is a crucial transcription factor that regulates the transcription of various genes involved in biological processes that are susceptible to hypoxia [ 57 ]. HIF-1 is the active transcription factor which consists of HIF-1α and HIF-1β, wherein active HIF is named after its alpha subunit [ 58 ]. In the current study, we checked for the expression levels of Hif-1α. The expression of Hif-1α was found to be elevated in liver tissues of SHR when compared to WKY at both RNA and protein levels (Fig. 1 ). In line with our findings, increased expression of Hif-1α was observed in the hepatic tissues of SHR rats subjected to high-fat diet-induced NAFLD [ 59 ]. Pertinently, studies have shown that apart from the liver, several other tissues also express elevated levels of Hif-1α. Francesco et al. reported an up-regulation of Hif-1α at both RNA and protein levels in SHR hearts compared to WKY [ 45 ]. Along similar lines, increased Hif-1α levels were also observed in the brain, prostate and retina of SHR compared to WKY [ 44 , 60 , 61 ]. The up-regulation of Hif-1α may be considered an adaptive response to combat the increased stress caused due to hypertension in SHR. In addition, SHR strains exhibited intrinsic oxygen deficiency, resulting in chronic hypoxia in various organs. This hypoxic environment was associated with elevated levels of Hif-1α [ 39 , 45 , 59 , 60 , 62 , 63 ].


## Hif-1α regulates Hmgcr expression and intracellular cholesterol under hypoxic conditions

Cholesterol synthesis pathway is highly oxygen-sensitive process and hypoxia can limit cholesterol synthesis through HIF-1α-dependent mechanism. Upon hypoxia, there is an up-regulation of HIF-1α which in turn can activate the Insulin-induced genes (Insig) [ 26 ]. Lanosterol, a cholesterol precursor, facilitates the degradation of Hmgcr by promoting its binding with the Insig proteins in the endoplasmic reticulum. Following binding, Insig-associated ubiquitin ligases facilitate the ubiquitination of Hmgcr, thereby leading to its proteasomal degradation and completion of proteasome-mediated ERAD pathway [ 64 – 67 ]. Insigs are also involved in the modulation of the proteolytic activation of Srebf by binding to SCAP, an escort protein for Srebf. So, when Insigs bind to SCAP in the ER, they trap Srebf, thereby precluding its export to the golgi apparatus and activating the transcription of Hmgcr [ 68 , 69 ]. Together, hypoxia stimulates these Insig-mediated reactions (sterol-accelerated ERAD of Hmgcr and sterol-induced ER retention of SCAP-Srebf), which leads to the degradation of Hmgcr [ 26 ].

In this study, a reduction in the SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter activity was observed after 6 h of hypoxia treatment. Further, we also observed that the endogenous transcript and protein levels of Hmgcr were decreased upon acute hypoxic stress. Additionally, the intracellular cholesterol levels were also diminished upon hypoxia. On the other hand, enhanced Hif-1α transcript and protein levels were observed after hypoxia. (Fig. 2 ). Interestingly, we also found a strong negative correlation between HIF-1α and HMGCR (Fig. 3 A, B and C). In view of the inverse correlation between HIF-1α and HMGCR , we sought to investigate whether HIF-1α plays a role in modulating the expression of Hmgcr , as various genes were reported to be indirectly regulated by HIF-1α [ 19 ]. Consistent with our computational predictions, the knock-down of Hif-1α increased the SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter activity and the endogenous Hmgcr protein level (Fig. 4 A and B). In corroboration, silencing of Hif-1α was shown to increase intracellular cholesterol levels in the BRL 3A cells (Fig. 4 F and G). Our findings indicate the potential role for Hif-1α as a transcriptional repressor of Hmgcr gene expression. Existing literature also supports similar findings on the regulatory role of Hif-1α as a transcriptional repressor [ 70 – 74 ]. However, Hif-1α can also act as a transcriptional activator [ 75 – 80 ] suggesting that Hif-1α-mediated regulation of gene transcription is context-dependent.

Next, we leveraged in-silico tools (ConSite, LASAGNA, PROMO, TF-BIND, P-MATCH and MATCH) to predict Hif -binding sites in the Hmgcr promoter domains. Our in-silico analysis revealed four hypoxia-response elements (HREs) on the SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter domain. To identify which HREs are functional, we performed ChIP assays to evaluate the extent of interaction of Hif-1α with the promoter domains. However, none of the HREs had significant differential binding between SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr domains (Fig. 5 ). Hence, the findings suggested that although Hif-1α regulated Hmgcr expression in SHR and WKY liver tissues it did not contribute to the differential expression of Hmgcr in these animal models of cardiometabolic disease.


## Srebf and Runx3 regulate Hmgcr expression and intracellular cholesterol under hypoxic conditions

Sterol regulatory element-binding transcription factor (Srebf) is a membrane-bound transcription factor that plays a major role in regulating hepatic cholesterol and fatty acid synthesis [ 81 ]. It has been shown that sterols accumulate upon hypoxia and inhibit Srebf, leading to diminished Hmgcr expression and exerting feedback control of cholesterol synthesis [ 82 ]. Runt-related transcription factor 3 (Runx3) is a transcription factor that may either activate or repress transcription and can also interact with other transcription factors. It is a tumor suppressor protein in various cancers [ 83 ]. Runx3 is expressed in epithelial, hematopoietic, and neuronal stem cells [ 84 ] and is essential to the development of TrkC-dependant dorsal root ganglion neurons [ 85 ] and CD8-lineage T cells [ 86 ]. It is also involved in the TGF-β signaling pathway [ 87 ]. Lee et al. revealed that the expression of Runx3 is modulated under hypoxic conditions by histone deacetylation and histone methylation [ 88 ]. We had previously reported that Srebf and Runx3 bind differentially to the SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr promoter and play key roles in regulating Hmgcr expression [ 11 ]. Since Srebf and Runx3 are involved in the differential expression of SHR and WKY- Hmgcr promoters under basal conditions, we wanted to know whether they play a role in regulating Hmgcr under hypoxia.

Since Srebf and Runx3 regulate transcription of rat Hmgcr and an inverse correlation was observed between HIF-1α and HMGCR we examined the correlation between HIF-1α and SREBF / RUNX3 in human tissues. Indeed, the HIF-1α transcript levels had a significantly negative correlation with SREBF transcript levels across various tissues of human origin retrieved from the GTEx portal and EMBL (Fig. 3 D and E). On the contrary, RUNX3 transcript levels tend to correlate positively with HIF-1α transcript levels (Fig. 3 G and H). Also, in our previous study, we reported that while SREBF had a positive correlation with HMGCR , RUNX3 had a negative correlation [ 11 ]. These results suggest that HIF-1α could regulate SREBF and RUNX3, which in turn might modulate the HMGCR expression upon hypoxic stress.

To validate this hypothesis in vitro , we assessed the levels of Srebf and Runx3 after acute hypoxic stress in BRL 3A cells. We observed that while the transcript/protein level of Srebf was reduced upon hypoxic stress, the transcript/protein level of Runx3 was elevated (Fig. 2 ). We also checked whether there would be any change in the SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter activity after siRNA-mediated down-regulation of Srebf/Runx3 prior to hypoxic treatment. Consistent with our previous results, we observed Srebf siRNA-mediated reduction and Runx3 siRNA-mediated enhancement of SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter activities (Fig. 6 ). In tandem, the intracellular cholesterol levels were also modulated after Srebf or Runx3 knock-down in both normoxic and hypoxic conditions. In line with our in-silico predictions, we observed that the endogenous Srebf and Runx3 proteins were enhanced and diminished respectively upon Hif-1α knock-down (Fig. 4 ). Observed changes in the protein levels of Srebf and Runx3 levels upon Hif-1α silencing suggest its involvement in both repression and activation of these transcription factors, respectively. The Hypoxic treatment enhanced Runx3 protein levels and increased the Runx3-mediated repression of SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter activity. In corroboration, the hypoxic treatment caused diminished activation of Srebf-mediated SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter activities. We performed ChIP assays to assess the extent of interactions between Srebf/Runx3 with the SHR- and WKY- Hmgcr promoter domains in vivo under hypoxic conditions. We observed that while Srebf showed diminished binding to the SHR/WKY- Hmgcr promoter domains, Runx3 exhibited augmented binding under hypoxic stress (Fig. 7 ). Furthermore, ChIP-seq data analysis suggested binding of Hif-1α to both Srebf and Runx3 promoters in both rodent and human models, supporting the hypothesis of its regulatory role (ChIP-Atlas, https://chip-atlas.org ).

Our in-silico analysis revealed two hypoxia-response elements (HREs) on the SHR/WKY- Srebf promoter domain and three HREs on the SHR/WKY- Runx3 promoter domain. The analysis showed that Hif-1α binding scores were higher in the case of Runx3 promoter as compared to the Srebf promoter (Fig. S7 ). Consistently, ChIP analysis revealed that HREs bound with higher affinity to SHR/WKY- Runx3 promoter domains as compared to SHR/WKY- Srebf promoter domains (Fig. 8 ). Thus, Hif-1α may enhance the level of Runx3 (that acts as a repressor for Hmgcr ) to a greater extent as compared to Srebf (that acts as an activator for Hmgcr ). Such concomitant regulation of Runx3 and Srebf by Hif-1α leads to overall decrease in the Hmgcr expression upon hypoxic stress.


## Conclusions

This study identified two transcription factors, Srebf and Runx3, which can regulate Hmgcr expression under hypoxia. To the best of our knowledge, the present study unravelled, for the first time, the role of Srebf and Runx3 in regulating Hmgcr expression under conditions of hypoxia. Upon acute hypoxic stress, there was decreased Srebf expression and increased Runx3 expression, thus altering the binding affinity of Srebf and Runx3 to the Hmgcr promoter domains, resulting in the modulation of Hmgcr gene expression and intracellular cholesterol levels.
