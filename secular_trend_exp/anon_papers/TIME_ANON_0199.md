## Abstract

Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) is a double-stranded DNA virus that establishes lifelong latency and can cause fatal herpes encephalitis. This study aimed to identify key clock genes regulating HSV-1 infection and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Transcriptomic analyses of the brainstem and hippocampus in mice were conducted to identify clock genes potentially involved in HSV-1 regulation. A serum shock cell model was employed to assess the impact of temporal variations in NECTIN-1 expression on HSV-1 infection. Luciferase reporter assays and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) were used to investigate the transcriptional regulatory mechanisms.

NECTIN-1, a primary receptor mediating HSV-1 entry into neurons, exhibited rhythmic expression in both the brainstem and hippocampus of mice. In vitro assays further demonstrated that HSV-1 DNA copy numbers fluctuated in near synchrony with NECTIN-1 expression levels. Overexpression of the core clock factors BMAL1 and CLOCK significantly increased NECTIN-1 expression. Mechanistically, BMAL1 and CLOCK bind directly to E-box-like motifs in the NECTIN-1 promoter region, thereby enhancing its transcriptional activity. Furthermore, pharmacological inhibition of CLOCK using CLK8 markedly suppressed HSV-1 infection.

Together, our findings reveal a novel mechanism by which the circadian clock modulates HSV-1 entry through rhythmic regulation of the NECTIN-1 receptor. BMAL1 and CLOCK emerge as potential therapeutic targets for circadian-based antiviral strategies.

## Introduction

HSV-1 is a neurotropic virus classified within the α-herpesvirus subfamily [ 1 – 3 ]. Epidemiological studies have shown that 50–90% of the global population is seropositive for HSV-1 [ 4 ]. Primary infection typically occurs in the oral cavity or respiratory tract upon initial exposure [ 5 ]. Following entry, HSV-1 travels retrogradely along axons to the neuronal cell body, where it establishes latency in the trigeminal ganglia [ 6 ]. However, psychological and physiological stressors, such as fever, ultraviolet radiation, or immunosuppression, can reactivate latent HSV-1, resulting in herpes simplex keratitis or encephalitis [ 7 ].

HSV-1 initiates infection by fusing its envelope with the host cell membrane. The HSV-1 virion encodes up to 16 envelope proteins, among which four glycoproteins—gB, gH, gL, and gD—are indispensable for viral entry. gD directly interacts with cellular surface receptors to facilitate viral entry into host cells [ 8 ]. Three cellular receptors for gD have been identified: herpesvirus entry mediator (HVEM), heparan sulfate (HS) modified by 3-O-sulfotransferases (3-OSTs) and NECTIN-1 [ 9 – 11 ]. HVEM, the first identified receptor for HSV-1, is highly expressed in leukemia tissues [ 12 ]. HS constitutes a broadly distributed family of cell-surface molecules on nearly all cell types [ 13 ]. NECTIN-1, a primary HSV-1 receptor, is expressed in neurons, keratinocytes and epithelial cells [ 9 ]. As a member of the Nectin/Necl family, NECTIN-1 primarily mediates cell-cell adhesion [ 14 ]. Its high expression in neurons highlights its crucial role in the pathogenesis of herpes simplex encephalitis.

The circadian clock system is an intrinsic regulatory mechanism that has evolved as a long-term adaptation to the Earth’s 24-hour environmental cycles. In mammals, this system comprises a central clock located in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) and peripheral clocks distributed throughout various organs and tissues [ 15 – 17 ]. At the molecular level, the core circadian oscillator consists of interlinked transcription-translation feedback loops regulated by transcription factors. In the primary feedback loop, BMAL1 forms a heterodimer with CLOCK, which activates the transcription of target genes by binding to E-box elements in their promoters. Among the target genes are their own repressors, Period (Per) and Cryptochrome (Cry), which, upon expression, inhibit BMAL1-CLOCK activity and thus close the feedback loop. Additionally, RORα and REV-ERBα regulate BMAL1 expression by binding to RORE elements in its promoter, with RORα functioning as an activator and REV-ERBα as a repressor. The transcription factors DBP and E4BP4 further influence circadian gene expression, including that of Per, via the D-box element [ 18 ]. The precise coordination of these clock-controlled genes ensures the maintenance of 24-hour circadian oscillations.

The circadian clock system is ubiquitous in nearly all cells and tissues, orchestrating a broad range of physiological processes, including sleep-wake cycles, metabolism, hormone secretion and immune responses. Notably, it also plays a pivotal role in regulating the life cycles of various viruses. For instance, overexpression of PER2 in permissive hepatocyte-derived cells reduces HCV RNA replication [ 19 ]. Deletion of the core clock gene Bmal1 or disruption of circadian rhythms, exacerbates acute viral bronchiolitis induced by Sendai virus and influenza A virus in mice [ 20 , 21 ]. Additionally, silencing Bmal1 or treating lung epithelial cells with the REV-ERB agonist SR9009 reduces ACE2 expression, thereby inhibiting SARS-CoV-2 entry and replication [ 22 ].

Despite these insights, it remains unclear whether the circadian clock modulates HSV-1 infection through the regulation of NECTIN-1, a key neuronal entry receptor. In this study, we aim to investigate whether NECTIN-1 expression is under circadian control and how core clock components such as BMAL1 and CLOCK influence HSV-1 infectivity. Uncovering this mechanism will not only enhance our understanding of host–virus interactions within the context of circadian biology but may also identify novel targets for circadian-based antiviral strategies.


## Materials and methods


## Cells, viruses and antibodies

Vero (CCL-81) and SH-SY5Y (CRL-2266) cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, USA). The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (GIBCO, Grand Island, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (ExCell Bio, Shanghai, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (GIBCO, Grand Island, USA). HSV-1 strain F was propagated and titrated in Vero cells using a standard plaque assay. Anti-NECTIN-1 (sc-21722), Anti-HSV-1 ICP5 (sc-56989) and anti-HSV-1 ICP0 (sc-53070) antibodies were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA, USA).


## RNA abundances analysis

Microarray data used in this study were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (accession number GSE54652 ; www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo ). The data were analyzed according to a previous study [ 23 ]. Briefly, the data were analyzed using the JTK_CYCLE package in R, with parameters configured to detect 24-hour periodic waveforms characteristic of circadian rhythms. Significance was bounded by P < 0.05.


## Cell culture and serum shock

SH-SY5Y were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured until reaching 70–80% confluence. Cells were subsequently incubated in fresh serum-free medium for 24 h. A serum shock was induced by exposing the cells to medium containing 50% fetal bovine serum for 2 h. Following the serum shock, the medium was replaced with serum-free medium. Cells were harvested every 4 h to detect the gene expression levels of BMAL1 , NR1D1 and NECTIN-1 . For HSV-1 infection experiments, cells were challenged with the virus at 2 h, 6 h, 10 h, 14 h, 18 h and 22 h post serum shock, and viral DNA was extracted at 24 h post-infection.


## Viral DNA extraction

Viral DNA was extracted using a viral DNA extraction kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 200 µL of supernatant was mixed with 500 µL of Buffer VL for lysis, transferred to FastPure ® RNA columns, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm. The filtrate was discarded. Next, 600 µL of Buffer RW was added, and the column was centrifuged to remove the filtrate, yielding purified DNA. Finally, RNase-free ddH₂O was used to elute DNA from the columns.


## Viral DNA copy number measurement

Plasmid gB was first constructed by inserting the glycoprotein B encoding sequence into a plasmid vector (pMD 19-T Vector). Serial dilutions of the plasmid were used as templates for qPCR. A standard curve was generated by plotting the Ct values against the logarithm of plasmid concentrations and performing linear regression.

Total DNA from the samples was extracted and used as a template for qPCR amplification. qPCR reactions were performed using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The amplification protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing and extension at 60 °C for 30 s. Melt curve was conducted from 65 °C to 95 °C, with readings taken every 0.5 °C. The reaction was completed with a 5-second plate read. Viral load was calculated based on a standard curve generated by plotting Ct values against serial dilutions of a standard plasmid. Primer sequences are provided in Table 1 .

## Transfection of plasmid

SH-SY5Y cells were seeded in 12-well plates and cultured until reaching 70–80% confluence. Transfection was then performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cleveland, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each well, 0.5 µg of plasmid DNA was diluted in 25 µL of Opti-MEM medium and mixed with 0.5 µL of P3000 reagent. In a separate tube, 1 µL of Lipofectamine 3000 was diluted in 25 µL of Opti-MEM. After a 5-minute incubation at room temperature, the DNA mixture was combined with the Lipofectamine solution and incubated for an additional 15 min to allow complex formation. The resulting transfection complexes were added dropwise to the cells. At 6 h post-transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete growth medium. Cells were harvested 48 h later for subsequent analysis. The sequences of the overexpression plasmids are provided in Supplementary Table 1 .


## Western blotting

The cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fuoride (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA reagent kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Total proteins were separated by 8%−15% gradient SDS-PAGE (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. After blocking with 5% skim milk, the membrane was incubated with a primary antibody (anti-ICP0, anti-ICP5 or anti-NECTIN-1) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody. Protein bands were imaged by using the Bio-Rad imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA).


## Luciferase reporter assay

HEK293T cells were seeded into 48-well plates and cultured until reaching 70–80% confluence. Cells were then co-transfected with pRL-TK renilla luciferase control reporter vectors and clock-related gene expression plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). After 24 h, the cells were lysed, and incubated with the appropriate luciferase substrates. Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were subsequently measured using a dual-luciferase assay system. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity, and the results were presented as relative luciferase units (RLU).


## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

ChIP assays were performed using the SimpleChIP Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA). Briefly, SH-SY5Y cells were infected with HSV-1 for 12 h or without infection, then the cells were fixed with 37% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. The cross-linking reaction was quenched by adding glycine to a final concentration of 0.125 M for 5 min. Cells were then resuspended and lysed in lysis buffer, followed by sonication to solubilize and shear the cross-linked chromatin. The sheared chromatin was immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against BMAL1, CLOCK, Histone H3 (positive control) and normal IgG (negative control). Immunocomplexes were eluted in elution buffer for 30 min at 65 °C. Cross-links were reversed by incubation with 5 M NaCl and proteinase K at 65 °C for 2 h. The purified DNA was subsequently analyzed by qPCR using specific primers listed in Table 2 .

## Statistics

Data were presented as mean ± SD of the results from at least 3 independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**) or p < 0.001 (***).


## Results


## Nectin-1 was rhythmically expressed in mouse brains

Herpes simplex encephalitis, a severe neurological disorder caused by HSV-1, primarily affects the temporal lobes and limbic system, leading to extensive brain damage and neuronal death [ 24 , 25 ]. To investigate the potential influence of the circadian clock system on the outcomes of central nervous system infection caused by HSV-1. We analyzed rhythmically expressed protein-coding genes in brainstem and hippocampus, with the raw data obtained from previously published studies [ 23 ]. Using JTK_CYCLE analysis, a total of 1,670 genes were found to exhibit rhythmic expression in the brainstem, and 2,106 genes in the hippocampus, with 559 rhythmic genes overlapping between the two regions (Figure S1A, Fig. 1 A). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified six first-level and 43 s-level KEGG pathways (Fig. 1 B). Among the second-level pathways, those related to viral infectious diseases were selected for further analysis. Ten genes were involved in the herpes simplex virus 1 infection, including Nectin-1 , Bcl2l1 , Calr , Nxf1 , Pdia3 , Ppp1cc , Srpk1 , Zfp442 , Zfp773 and Zfp983 (Figure S1B, Fig. 1 C). Given that HSV-1 primarily targets neurons in the brain and Nectin-1 is a principal cellular receptor mediating HSV-1 entry into neurons, we speculated that the circadian expression pattern of Nectin-1 may modulate susceptibility to HSV-1 neuroinvasion. As shown in Fig. 2 A-B and Figure S2A-C, SH-SY5Y cells exhibited temporal variations in the mRNA levels of the BMAL1, NR1D1 , and NECTIN-1 . To further investigate whether the oscillation of NECTIN-1 affects the outcome of HSV-1 infection, we challenged the cells with HSV-1 at various time points following serum shock treatment (Fig. 2 C). As shown in Fig. 2 D and Figure S2D, the HSV-1 DNA copy numbers fluctuated in near synchrony with the oscillatory pattern of NECTIN-1 expression. These findings suggest that the outcome of HSV-1 infection in nerve cells is associated with circadian expression of NECTIN-1 . ## Core clock proteins BMAL1 and CLOCK positively regulate NECTIN-1 expression

In mammals, more than 10 core clock factors constitute a sophisticated gene regulatory network comprising multiple positive and negative feedback loops. To elucidate the regulatory mechanisms underlying NECTIN-1 circadian expression, we evaluated the effects of core clock factors on NECTIN-1 expression. BMAL1, CLOCK, NR1D1, NR1D2 and RORα were overexpressed in SH-SY5Y cells via plasmid transfection (Figure S3), followed by measurement of NECTIN-1 gene expression. Overexpression of BMAL1 and CLOCK significantly increased NECTIN-1 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3 A and B). In contrast, overexpression of NR1D1, NR1D2 and RORα had no significant effect on NECTIN-1 mRNA expression (Fig. 3 A). Consistent with these findings, BMAL1 and CLOCK overexpression enhanced HSV-1 entry into cells (Fig. 3 C). ## CLOCK and BMAL1 directly bind to the NECTIN-1 promoter to regulate its transcription

As shown, overexpression of BMAL1 and CLOCK significantly increased the luciferase activity of the NECTIN-1 promoter (Fig. 4 A). Subsequently, we found NECTIN-1 promoter contains an E-box-like motif (Fig. 4 B), and further explored the physical association of BMAL1 and CLOCK with the promoter of NECTIN-1 by performing ChIP assay. The result showed that CLOCK and BMAL1 directly bind to the NECTIN-1 promoter to regulate its transcription, both in the presence and absence of HSV-1 (Fig. 4 C). ## Pharmacological inhibition of CLOCK suppresses HSV-1 infection

To further assess whether CLOCK and BMAL1 serve as potential targets for regulating HSV-1 entry. We employed CLK8, a CLOCK/BMAL1 binding inhibitor, to evaluate its effects on NECTIN-1 expression and antiviral activity. To confirm the activity of CLK8, we first measured the mRNA expression of PER2 and CRY1, two canonical BMAL1/CLOCK target genes, and found that CLK8 treatment significantly reduced their expression (Figure S4). Moreover, CLK8 treatment reduced NECTIN-1 protein expression in a dose-dependent manner regardless of HSV-1 presence (Fig. 5 A). We further examined the anti-HSV-1 activity of CLK8 using a viral plaque assay and found that the inhibitory rate against the virus of 20 µM CLK8 reached 52% (Fig. 5 B). CLK8 also significantly reduced the DNA copy numbers of viral genes (Fig. 5 C). Furthermore, CLK8 significantly decreased the protein levels of HSV-1 early protein ICP0 and late protein ICP5 (Fig. 5 D). ## Discussion

Viruses utilize various mechanisms to enter host cells, among which receptor mediated entry via cell surface receptors being one of the most critical pathways [ 26 ]. NECTIN-1 is a critical cellular receptor that mediates HSV-1 entry into host cells [ 9 ]. Previous studies have demonstrated a qualitative and quantitative correlation between NECTIN-1 expression levels and susceptibility to HSV-1 infection. Petermann et al. reported that HSV-1 entry was significantly reduced in nectin-1-deficient epidermis and nearly absent in nectin-1-deficient keratinocytes cultured in vitro [ 27 ]. Krummenacher et al. further showed a direct relationship between viral entry and the average number of NECTIN-1 receptors per cell [ 28 ]. Moreover, cell lines with varying NECTIN-1 expression displayed differential sensitivity to oncolytic HSV therapy in vivo, suggesting that NECTIN-1 may serve as a predictive biomarker for the sensitivity of a tumor to herpes oncolytic therapy [ 29 ]. In this study, we observed that the Nectin-1 gene exhibits rhythmic expression in the brainstem and hippocampus of mice. Notably, previous studies have shown that HSV-2, a virus primarily responsible for genital herpes, causes less severe skin infections during the rest phase compared to the active phase, a phenomenon attributed to the rhythmic expression of NECTIN-1 in skin cells [ 30 ]. In addition to NECTIN-1, other viral entry receptors, such as CD81, claudin-1 and occludin for HCV, and ACE2 for SARS-CoV-2, are also regulated by circadian clock genes [ 22 , 31 ]. Collectively, these findings underscore the broader implication that components of the circadian clock may represent promising targets for the prevention and treatment of infectious diseases. Except for Nectin-1, data analysis revealed that other genes including Bcl2l1, Calr, Nxf1, Pdia3, Ppp1cc, Srpk1, Zfp983, Zfp773, and Zfp442 exhibit rhythmic expression in both the brainstem and hippocampus. These genes are implicated in biological processes such as viral replication, immune evasion, and latency. Further research is needed to elucidate the impact of their rhythmic expression on herpesvirus infection.

We observed that the overexpression of BMAL1 and CLOCK upregulates both NECTIN-1 gene and protein expression in SH-SY5Y cells. Mechanistically, CLOCK and BMAL1 directly bind to the NECTIN-1 promoter to positively regulate its transcription. However, Edgar et al. reported acute HSV-1 infection was significantly enhanced of Bmal1 −/− mice and cells, and this study did not address specific mechanism [ 21 ]. We speculate that this discrepancy may be due to cell-type or context-dependent regulatory mechanisms, or that the absence of BMAL1 may lead to compensatory pathways that favor viral replication independently of NECTIN-1 expression. Beyond HSV-1, the core circadian transcription factors BMAL1 and CLOCK play diverse roles in the infection processes of various viruses. Recent studies have shown that loss of BMAL1 enhances the replication of influenza virus, respiratory syncytial virus and parainfluenza virus type 3 [ 21 , 32 ], whereas it suppresses the replication of HCV, dengue virus (DENV) and Zika virus (ZIKV) [ 31 ]. Additionally, BMAL1 and CLOCK have been reported to drive HIV transcription by binding to E-box elements within the LTR promoter [ 33 ]. Other circadian regulators are also implicated in antiviral responses: PER2 inhibits HCV replication [ 19 ], while REV-ERBα suppresses HIV promoter activity [ 34 ]. Notably, the relationship between the circadian clock and viral infection is bidirectional, the circadian clock can influence viral pathogenesis, viruses can in turn modulate circadian pathways. For instance, overexpression of the HBV X protein disrupts the expression of circadian genes [ 35 ]; the HIV Tat protein dampens circadian rhythm amplitude [ 36 ]; and influenza A virus advances the expression of Bmal1, Clock and Rev-erbβ, while enhancing Per2 amplitude [ 37 ]. Although numerous studies highlight the interplay between circadian regulation and viral pathogenesis, the inherent complexity of the circadian system and the diversity of viral mechanisms necessitate further in-depth investigation to fully elucidate these interactions.

Multiple small molecule compounds including SR9009, SR8278, SR9011, GSK4112, GSK1362 and CLK8 have been identified to target circadian clock components [ 38 ]. Among these, several compounds have exhibited notable antiviral properties. For example, SR9009, a REV-ERB-specific agonist, has been shown to significantly suppress infections caused by influenza virus, SARS-CoV-2, DENV and ZIKV. In the present study, we observed that CLK8 inhibits HSV-1 DNA replication and reduces the expression of viral proteins in a concentration dependent manner. Although the antiviral activity of CLK8 has not been previously reported, its function as a CLOCK inhibitor suggests that it may influence viruses whose replication is modulated by CLOCK-dependent mechanisms.

In summary, we observed that the Nectin-1 gene exhibits circadian rhythmic expression in brainstem and hippocampus of mice. We further demonstrated that oscillations in Nectin-1 expression significantly impact the outcome of HSV-1 infection in SH-SY5Y cells. Mechanistically, the core circadian transcription factors BMAL1 and CLOCK directly bind to the promoter region of Nectin-1, thereby regulating its expression in nerve cells. Notably, we identified CLK8, a small molecule inhibitor that disrupts BMAL1/CLOCK binding, thereby suppressing NECTIN-1 expression and effectively inhibiting HSV-1 infection. Collectively, our findings provide mechanistic insights into the circadian regulation of HSV-1 entry via the NECTIN-1 receptor and suggest that BMAL1 and CLOCK may serve as potential antiviral therapeutic targets.
