## Abstract

In the absence of treatment, detection of pathogenic RNA viruses and quarantine of infected individuals are critical for controlling the infection spread. Conventional antibody‐based methods often lack the required accuracy and sensitivity, due to the need for a substantial amount of viral antigens. Similarly, traditional reverse transcription real‐time polymerase chain reaction methods face challenges in providing timely results due to their multiple thermal nucleic acid amplification steps. To overcome these limitations, a new method based on imaging of virus‐specific DNA markers is developed. This approach employs specially designed single‐stranded circular DNA probes that capture virus‐derived RNA fragments generated by RNase digestion of the viral genome. These fragments serve as primers for a subsequent single‐step DNA filling reaction, producing double‐stranded virus‐specific marker molecules. These individual markers are recognized through fluorescence imaging following linearization by enzyme cleavage and subsequent fluorescence staining. This method can detect viruses at a genome equivalent level of 14 within 40 min. In addition, the molecule‐level imaging‐based method effectively detects human immunodeficiency virus‐1 in clinical samples. This diagnostic approach does not require sophisticated thermal controls nor extensive nucleic acid amplifications, allowing for accurate, sensitive, and rapid detection without the need for large equipment, offering substantial potential for point‐of‐care applications.

## Introduction

RNA viruses, characterized by their ribonucleic acid (RNA) genomes, utilize their own RNA polymerases for replication. [ 1 ] These error‐prone enzymes often introduce mutations into the viral genomes, resulting in the emergence of variant progenies and new viruses such as human immunodeficiency virus‐1 (HIV‐1), severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS‐CoV), and Middle East respiratory syndrome coronavirus (MERS‐CoV). [ 2 ]

The inherent characteristics of RNA viruses complicate the development of effective antiviral treatments. [ 3 ] First, RNA viruses, like other viruses, primarily reside inside host cells, making them difficult to target effectively. Second, their high mutation rates lead to frequent alterations in the viral proteins and nucleic acids, diminishing the efficacy of antiviral drug candidates. The development of vaccines is similarly complex and time‐consuming, as vaccines must not only be effective but also safe, typically requiring extensive tests and regulatory approval processes. In the absence of effective antiviral therapy and vaccines, accurate, sensitive, and rapid diagnostic capabilities, coupled with the timely quarantine of infected individuals, are crucial for mitigating viral transmission within communities.

Conventional antibody‐based virus detection methods can quickly provide diagnostic results within 20 min, but often compromise on accuracy and sensitivity, primarily due to the need for a substantial amount of viral antigens. In contrast, RT‐qPCR is widely regarded as the standard for the diagnosis of viruses due to its high accuracy and sensitivity. [ 4 ] However, the RT‐qPCR process involves highly repetitive heating and cooling cycles for nucleic acid amplification (Figure S1 , Supporting Information), requiring large and complex equipment that is challenging to miniaturize for field use. [ 5 ] Moreover, the time‐consuming nature of these thermal cycles poses a critical disadvantage for rapid diagnostics. [ 6 ]

Isothermal nucleic acid amplification techniques, such as recombinase polymerase amplification and loop‐mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), [ 7 ] offer potential solutions to some of the limitations associated with RT‐qPCR, particularly the dependence on multiple thermal cycles. [ 7h,j ] However, a significant drawback of these isothermal methods is their propensity to generate false positives due to non‐specific nucleic acid amplifications, particularly at low working temperatures. [ 7h,j ] Moreover, LAMP typically employs four to six primers for a single target virus, further elevating the likelihood of non‐specific interactions during nucleic acid amplifications, which can lead to erroneous results. [ 7i ]

To overcome the limitations associated with the existing methods for RNA virus detection, we have developed a new technique based on single‐molecule imaging that enables direct detection of virus‐derived DNA molecules without requiring a nucleic acid amplification step. This method utilizes sensitive imaging technology to visualize individual DNA molecules and employs novel single‐stranded circular DNA (sscDNA) probes designed to encompass substantial portions of the viral genome sequence. By leveraging these probes, the method captures viral RNAs as primers and subsequently generates virus‐specific marker molecules via quick enzymatic conversion of the single‐stranded probe to double‐stranded DNA (dsDNA). Following a rapid staining process, these markers can be fluorescently imaged to facilitate detection. Importantly, this entire process is conducted within a narrow temperature range of 37 to 42 °C, eliminating the need for sophisticated temperature control systems. These features enhance the method's suitability for point‐of‐care (POC) diagnostics, where the use of large and complex equipment is not desirable.

We have demonstrated the analytical sensitivity of our virus detection method using RNA extracted from influenza A viruses (IAVs). Our findings revealed a limit of detection of 14 GEs of viral RNA per reaction, which is comparable to the sensitivity of RT‐qPCR, typically ranging from 10 to 100 GEs per reaction. [ 8 ] Additionally, we applied this method to blood specimens collected from five patients with HIV‐1 infection and two healthy individuals, yielding accurate diagnostic results within 40 min.

While traditional methods relying on nucleic acid amplification often enhance sensitivity and accuracy in diagnostics, the advent of technologies that enable direct recognition of virus‐derived nucleic acids—bypassing the complexities associated with the amplification—can significantly simplify and accelerate the diagnostic process. This new approach offers a more reliable alternative for rapid testing in clinical settings. In conclusion, the molecular‐level detection scheme, combined with a streamlined workflow and minimal equipment requirements, makes this method particularly well‐suited for POC applications, where accurate, sensitive, and timely diagnosis is critical for effective patient management and treatment decisions.


## Results


## Overview of the Molecular Imaging‐Based Detection of RNA Viruses

We have developed a sensitive method for detecting pathogenic RNA viruses, which mostly possess single‐stranded genomes. In this approach, sscDNA probes that contain significant portions of the viral genome serve as templates for the generation of virus‐specific double‐stranded circular DNA (dscDNA) markers in the presence of detection target viral particles ( Figure 1 a ). Initially, viral RNAs are extracted from the viral particles in the samples and subsequently fragmented using RNase III. The resulting single‐stranded viral RNA fragments bind to the probes and function as primers for the following reverse transcriptase‐based step (RT), which fills the sscDNA probes to form dscDNAs.

The different RNA fragments, randomly generated by RNase III digestion, bind to various sites on the probes, leading to the effective formation of filled dscDNAs due to the circular nature of the probes. In contrast, RNA fragments from non‐target viral particles do not bind to the probe, resulting in the absence of dscDNA marker formation (Figure 1a ).

For enhanced visualization, the virus‐derived dscDNAs are linearized by cleavage with specific restriction enzymes —NotI for markers specific to murine leukemia virus (MLV) and IAV, and BamHI or AgeI for those specific to HIV‐1. After linearization, the dscDNAs are stained with a dsDNA‐specific fluorescent dye, such as YOYO‐1, and then introduced into a simple microfluidic device for stretching (Figure 1b ). To facilitate DNA stretching, we constructed a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) microfluidic device on a positively charged glass surface, which immobilizes DNA molecules through electrostatic forces and stretches them via capillary forces (Figure 1b ). The linearly stretched dsDNAs serve as virus‐specific markers, indicating the presence of the detection target viruses. Fluorescence imaging of these marker molecules enables rapid diagnosis of infections without the need for nucleic acid amplification, achieving detection results in just 40 minutes.

The length of the virus‐specific marker DNA linearly correlates with the length of the constructed sscDNA probe, allowing for more sophisticated identification based on its predetermined size. The linearized dsDNA marker molecules can be stretched to reach lengths that proportionally correspond to the number of their constituent bases. For instance, dsDNA containing 10 000 bases can be stretched to 2.4 µm in a microfluidic device. [ 9 ]

Additionally, the use of fluorescent dyes with sequence‐dependent affinities for DNA eventually enables the conversion of probes with predefined sequences into virus‐specific markers exhibiting distinct color band distributions after staining (Figure 1c ). [ 10 ] For instance, a dye that preferentially binds to adenine and thymine‐rich (AT‐rich) regions can generate unique color patterns for marker molecules. In addition, this dye can be paired with a second dye that has sequence‐independent staining properties to create clearer color patterns. [ 10c ] This dual staining enhances the ability to recognize virus‐specific markers based on both length and clear color patterns, further increasing the accuracy of our detection method. The detection of a small number of marker molecules indicates the presence of a target viral particle, underscoring the sensitivity of our approach.

This new method contrasts with RT‐qPCR in two points. First, in our approach, viral RNAs function as primers for the generation of marker molecules, whereas in RT‐qPCR, they serve as templates. Consequently, our method does not require the same level of stability for viral RNAs as RT‐qPCR, which contributes to more reliable detection results. Furthermore, more degraded viral RNAs can be effectively utilized to bind to the probes and ultimately generate marker molecules. Second, our method can simplify the detection process by generating virus‐specific marker molecules in a single‐step filling of the sscDNA probes, eliminating the need for nucleic acid amplification (Figure S1 , Supporting Information).

RNase treatment of the viral RNA genome can produce over 300 fragments, as the genomes of highly pathogenic RNA viruses typically exceed 10 kilobases (kb) in length and the genomic RNA can be enzymatically fragmented into pieces as small as 30 bases. If the entire viral genome sequence is incorporated into the sscDNA probes, a single viral particle can theoretically yield more than 300 virus‐specific markers, significantly increasing the sensitivity of our imaging‐based detection.

Importantly, the three enzymatic steps—fragmentation of the viral genome, RT to form virus‐specific dscDNAs, and linearization of the dscDNAs—can all be conducted in a narrow temperature window of 37–42 °C. Consequently, our method operates at consistently low temperatures, eliminating the need for complicated temperature controls, reducing energy consumption.


## Design and Production of sscDNA Probes

sscDNA probes can be designed by incorporating sequences that are complementary to either partial or complete regions of viral RNA genomes (Figure S2 , Supporting Information). By integrating larger sequence segments, these probes can capture a greater variety of fragmented viral RNAs, thereby facilitating the generation of more virus‐specific marker molecules. In this study, we constructed probes that encompass 54% of the HIV‐1 genome and 24% of the IAV genome as experimental trials (Figure S2c,d , Supporting Information). To potentially enhance the detection of diverse strains of both viruses, we incorporated less variable sequences corresponding to the Pol gene for HIV‐1 and the PB2 and NS genes for IAV into the probes. [ 11 ]

To enable the efficient recognition of virus‐specific marker molecules through fluorescence imaging using a 100× lens, it is essential that the markers exceed a length of 3.0 kb, which establishes the minimum length for the designed probes. Additionally, viral sequences can be divided and incorporated into two or more segments within the probes, providing increased flexibility for binding with viral RNA fragments and thereby enhancing detection efficiency. The distribution of these viral DNA sequences across the probes can influence the formation of marker molecules. When utilizing sequence‐specific color band distribution to identify marker molecules, non‐viral sequences containing concentrated regions of specific bases can also be incorporated into the probes to enhance virus detection.

To efficiently produce sscDNA probes, we utilized the M13KO7 helper phage system. This system employs the phage to supply the essential proteins required for the replication and packaging of sscDNA, derived from phagemids, into phage particles. In this process, phagemids containing the desired viral sequences are introduced into bacterial cells (Figure 1d,e ( 1 )). These phagemids incorporate the f1 origin of replication, a sequence that is recognized and acted upon by the replicating proteins encoded by the M13KO7 helper phage. Importantly, the orientation of the f1 origin within the phagemid is crucial, as it dictates which strand of the phagemid will be replicated as sscDNA (Figure 1d ).

Once the M13KO7 phage infects the bacterial cell (Figure 1e ( 2 )), the double‐stranded phagemid DNA serves as a template to produce sscDNAs. These sscDNAs are subsequently packaged as genomic component into phage particles, which are then released into the culture medium. The sscDNA probes are finally extracted from these phage particles (Figure 1e (3),f ). Following the protocol for the production of phage particles using phagemids, helper phage, and bacterial cells, we prepared five probes for detecting three different viruses (Figure 1f ; Figure S2 , Supporting Information). Three probes of sizes 11.7 kb, 9.6 kb, and 4.4 kb were developed for the detection of HIV‐1, along with an additional probe measuring 6.9 kb for the detection of MLV. Furthermore, a single probe measuring 12.0 kb was produced specifically for the detection of IAV (Figure S2 , Supporting Information). The purity of all probes was validated by observing single bands on an agarose gel (Figure 1f ).


## Verification of Marker Molecule Formation from sscDNA Probes

In our method, RNA fragments generated through the cleavage of the viral RNA genome by RNase bind to the sscDNA probe. Following this binding, reverse transcriptase extends the 3ʹ end of the virus‐derived RNA primer by adding nucleotides, resulting in the synthesis of the complementary nucleic acids. Various viral RNA fragments can associate with different specific sites on the probe and are subsequently extended by reverse transcriptase, ultimately producing juxtaposed double‐stranded DNA fragments. This filling process for the probe occurs exclusively in the presence of viral RNA.

To validate the key concept of our approach, we tested the formation of virus‐specific marker molecules in the presence of virus‐derived nucleic acid fragments across various experimental formats. As a model probe, we first utilized a commercially available sscDNA extracted from the M13 phage, designated as M13mp18. In addition, we employed two synthetic DNA oligomers as primers representing model virus‐derived short nucleic acids: one primer was designed to bind to the sequence at positions 2,339 to 2,353, while the second primer targeted positions 5,790 to 5,810 within the 7,249‐base sequence of the M13mp18 sscDNA (Table S1 , Supporting Information). These primers were applied to the model probe to assess the capability of our method in forming specific marker molecules, which is initiated by the hybridization of the complementary nucleic acids.

The RT was conducted for durations of 3, 10, and 30 min at 42 °C, following a pre‐denaturation step at 65 °C. This pre‐denaturation step is designed to minimize short‐range hybridization between the bases on the probe molecules, thereby increasing the likelihood of primer binding to the probes. These temperature conditions are commonly utilized in reverse transcriptions. Notably, the 3‐min RT did not yield detectable levels of the desired dsDNAs as shown in gel electrophoresis analysis ( Figure 2 a , sample 2). In contrast, substantial amounts of dsDNAs, expected to be ≈7.2 kb in size, were clearly generated when the RT was extended beyond 10 min (Figure 2a , samples 3–4). To further evaluate the necessity of the pre‐denaturation step, we analyzed the dsDNA yield with and without this step. The results indicated that equivalent quantities of dsDNA molecules were obtained, regardless of the inclusion of the pre‐denaturation step (Figure 2a , samples 3–4 and 6–7). The levels of the formed dsDNA molecules in the absence the denaturation step were ≈99.8% (10‐min RT) and 88.3% (30‐min RT) compared to the corresponding cases with the denaturation step (Figure 2a , samples 3–4 and 6–7). This finding suggests that a brief single RT is sufficient to generate marker molecules from the probes in the presence of virus‐derived nucleic acid fragments.

After RT, dsDNA molecules longer than 7.2 kb were also observed, albeit in significantly smaller quantities compared to the primary expected molecules. The formation of these additional molecules is linked to the features associated with the reverse transcriptase‐based single‐stranded DNA filling reaction. Notably, the 5' and 3' ends of the synthesized DNA strand fragments are not ligated with neighboring fragments, resulting in a state that is considered “nicked”. Heavily nicked dsDNA tends to exhibit slower electrophoretic mobility than modestly nicked dsDNA, which can lead to the appearance of smear bands above the expected band on the agarose gel (Figure 2a ). [ 12 ] Moreover, reverse transcriptase possesses strand displacement activity, which may facilitate the continuous production of single‐stranded DNA more extended than expectation in the presence of a template strand. [ 13 ] This enzymatic characteristic implies that longer incubation times with reverse transcriptase can yield an increased quantity of DNA molecules with higher molecular weight. In an effort to limit the formation of these unintended longer molecules, we set the RT time to 10 min.


## Refinement of Viral RNA Genome Fragmentation

Subsequently, we conducted the dsDNA marker formation reaction using RNAs extracted from model HIV‐1 particles. To ensure an adequate supply of viral particles for optimizing experimental conditions and to maintain experimenters' safety, we utilized non‐replicating models HIV‐1 and MLV. The model viral particles were produced by introducing plasmids containing the sequences that encode the viral genomes and proteins into packaging cells, specifically human embryonic kidney 293T (HEK 293T) cells. As all viral genes have been eliminated from the genome, the resulting viral particles were unable to replicate. Instead, the genomes of the model viruses HIV‐1 and MLV were engineered to incorporate the gene for enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) under the regulation of a human ubiquitin promoter and the gene for near‐infrared red fluorescent protein (iRFP) under the control of the immediate early promoter enhancer of human cytomegalovirus, respectively. These viral constructs were designed to exhibit only single‐round infectivity.

The 5.1 kb RNA genome of model HIV‐1 was treated with RNase III, an enzyme known for generating 3' hydroxyl groups at the ends of the cleaved RNA fragments. [ 14 ] In our experimental method, the 3' hydroxyl ends are crucial, as new nucleotides can only be added to these ends of the primer‐acting viral RNA fragments during the initial RT. To assess the effectiveness of the RNase treatment, we analyzed the product of the enzymatic cleavage using a bioanalyzer following a 10‐min cleavage reaction (Figure 2b ). The untreated samples exhibited two major populations of RNA molecules, both longer than 4,000 nucleotides. Additionally, we observed the presence of shorter RNA fragments, likely the result of RNA degradation. In contrast, after treatment with RNase III, a significant increase in the number of RNA molecules shorter than 200 nucleotides was noted (Figure 2b ). Further analysis with a chip designed for small RNA detection revealed that most of the RNA fragments were within the range of 14 to 65 nucleotides, with a population peak observed at approximately 30 nucleotides (Figure S3 , Supporting Information).

Next, we performed RT using the RNA fragments and HIV‐1‐specific 11.7 kb sscDNA probes at 42 °C for 10 min. After the reaction, virus‐specific marker molecules were successfully recognized along with significant consumption of the sscDNA probes (Figure 2c ). In contrast to previous experiments using a single type of DNA oligonucleotide as a model for virus‐derived nucleic acids, the reaction products obtained from the fragmented viral genomes exhibited smear bands on the gel. Notably, smear bands were also observed even without fragmentation of the RNA genomes, indicating that the extracted viral RNA genomes may contain a diverse array of RNA fragments of varying sizes. We monitored the formation of virus‐specific marker molecules with varying amounts of fragmented viral genomes (Figure S4 , Supporting Information). When the quantity of the RNA fragments was reduced by 2‐fold and 8‐fold, the previously observed smear bands were significantly diminished (Figure S4 , Supporting Information).

In an effort to streamline the overall virus detection process, we attempted to shorten the RNase treatment duration. However, when the RNase treatment time was reduced to 3 min, while it appeared that the probe molecules were largely consumed, the yield of virus‐specific marker molecules was significantly lower (Figure 2c ). Consequently, we concluded that a minimum treatment duration of 10‐min with RNase III was necessary to achieve sufficient marker synthesis.


## Imaging‐Based Detection of Marker Molecules

While traditional RT‐qPCR‐based virus detection methods provide diagnostic results by quantifying marker molecules, our approach relies on imaging‐based recognition of the physical characteristics of the virus‐specific markers. To facilitate this recognition, virus‐specific dscDNA markers are first linearized, stained with fluorescent dye, and subsequently stretched. This process captures the natural length information of the linear marker molecules on a PDMS microfluidic chip. The microfluidic chip consists of 10 channels, each of which can be imaged across four fields of view (FOVs) ( Figure 3 a ). The recognition of a small number of individual linear marker molecules using a simple fluorescence microscope is sufficient for determining the presence of target viral particles.

To establish a positive control for the marker molecules derived from the 9.6 kb sscDNA, we prepared linear 9.6 kb dsDNAs through PCR. Under a fluorescence microscope, the sscDNAs appeared as dots, representing a compact molecular state formed through intra‐strand base‐pairing. In contrast, the linear 9.6 kb dsDNAs, serving as the positive control, aligned with the direction of the capillary force‐driven flow (Figure 3a ), allowing for a clear distinction between sscDNA and its linear dsDNA counterpart.

We initially examined the formation of marker molecules resulting from RT using 6.9, 9.6, and 12.0 kb sscDNA probes, in conjunction with synthetic DNA primers designed from the sequences of model MLV, HIV‐1, and replicating IAV. After these reactions with the primers containing the viral sequences, we observed a significant amount of linear DNA product. Fluorescence imaging confirmed that longer dsDNA markers were produced from the longer probes (Figure 3b ). In contrast, nearly no linear DNA was detected in negative control samples that utilized primers lacking viral sequences.

For quantitative analysis, we measured the lengths of all linear DNAs detected across a total of 40 FOVs using ImageJ software. Image processing filtered out non‐specific and low‐intensity noise signals based on the circularity and size of individual DNA images (Figure S5 , Supporting Information). The length data for the remaining linear DNAs were presented with histograms illustrating the number of molecules (y‐axis) versus their lengths (x‐axis) (Figure 3c ). The dsDNA markers of 6.9, 9.6, and 12.0 kb exhibited unimodal distributions, with median lengths of 1.98, 3.02, and 3.70 µm, respectively (Figure 3c ). Compared to the negative control for each probe, the total numbers of linear DNA molecules were much higher, a clear indication of the presence of virus‐derived nucleic acids (Figure 3c ). The small number of linear DNAs detected in the negative control was attributed to residual dsDNAs within the sscDNA samples that had not been removed during probe production (Figure S6 , Supporting Information).

Imaging of the sscDNA probes without any reaction revealed a level of linear DNAs similar to that observed in the negative control samples (Figure S6 , Supporting Information). Additionally, imaging and downstream analysis of the PCR‐amplified 6.9, 9.6, and 12.0 kb DNAs as standard molecules demonstrated median lengths of 1.96, 2.79, and 4.04 µm, respectively, indicating a size difference of 9.2% or less compared to the corresponding RT products (Figure 3c ). This suggests that the DNAs formed during the RT likely adopt an extended conformation, akin to those of the PCR amplicons.


## Identification of Marker Molecules after Sequence‐Specific Fluorescent Staining

In addition to measuring the length of virus‐specific DNA marker molecules, their sequences can also be utilized for identification purposes. To explore this possibility, we employed a fusion protein comprising a truncated transcription activator‐like effector (tTALE) and mNeonGreen (mNG), known as tTALE‐mNG. [ 10f ] This fusion protein demonstrates a strong preference for binding AT‐rich regions under specific buffer conditions. At elevated salt concentrations, staining DNAs with this fusion protein produces sequence‐specific color patterns ( Figure 4 a,b ).

The maps showing the frequency profile of AT sequences are presented as line plots in orange. The line plot illustrates the AT content ratios across consecutive 316 bp bins along the DNA sequence, starting from the 5' end. AT frequencies range from 0.37 to 0.62, highlighting the spatial sequence variations of the DNA. The brightness profile, scaled from 0 to 255, represents relative fluorescence levels, where brighter regions signify higher AT content. The AT‐rich regions within these markers were selectively stained for visualization. In the images, the 4.4 kb dsDNA markers are shown with light blue arrows, while the 9.6 kb dsDNA markers are represented with orange arrows. We generated two different types of marker molecules using 4.4 kb and 9.6 kb sscDNA probes, each designed to target different regions of the model HIV‐1 genome (Figure S2 , Supporting Information), in conjunction with the virus genomic RNAs. When the resulting markers from the two different probes were stained with tTALE‐mNG in an aqueous solution containing 100 mM NaCl, distinct color patterns were observed, corresponding to the computationally calculated AT sequence profiles (Figure 4b ). In particular, the strongly stained regions of the markers spatially matched the peaks of high AT content in the calculated profile. The markers generated from the two probes differed not only in length but also in their respective color staining patterns. In contrast, when using Tris‐EDTA (1 × TE) buffer, both types of marker molecules were uniformly stained without any discernible color patterning. Under this condition, the lengths of the markers became the primary distinguishing feature. [ 15 ]

Applying multiple probes at the same time can enhance detection accuracy for a given target virus. To confirm this possibility, we generated marker molecules concurrently using two different types of probes and the RNAs extracted from model HIV‐1. Since HIV‐1 RNAs contain the regions targeted by both probes, two distinct marker DNAs can be formed in a single RT. Staining these marker products with the fluorescent fusion protein under high‐salt conditions yielded two distinctly different color patterns, each associated with one of the probes (Figure 4c ). The requirement of the co‐formation of two different markers during virus detection can reduce the likelihood of false positive results. Moreover, if multiple probes capable of generating marker molecules with different lengths and color patterns target different viruses at the same time, a single detection process can produce multiplexed diagnostic results.

Fluorescence imaging of marker molecules provides essential information regarding their physical characteristics. The visual recognition of individual marker molecules offers the potential for sensitive virus detection results, comparable to those obtained through conventional methods that typically involve lengthy processes for amplifying and quantifying marker molecules.


## Imaging‐Based Detection of Replicating Live Viruses through One‐Pot Enzymatic Reactions

In this study, we propose a new method for virus detection that generates virus‐specific marker molecules through three consecutive short‐time enzymatic reactions. To streamline the detection process and eliminate the need for inter‐step purifications, we combined these reactions into a one‐pot format ( Figure 5 a ). In this approach, reagents were added sequentially to gradually modify the buffer environment, ensuring optimal conditions for each enzymatic reaction within the same reaction tube.

Samples of each concentration were tested in triplicate. Reliable fluorescence signal was detected in only one of the three replicates for the sample with 143 GEs. The process began with the fragmentation of viral RNAs by RNase III, carried out in a 5 µL reaction volume at 37 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 15 µL of a larger solution volume was introduced to adjust the conditions for the RT, thereby establishing an optimal environment for the second enzyme. Linearization of the dsDNA markers was performed in the same tube for an additional 10 min, which involved adding 80 µL of required reagents, including a type of restriction endonuclease. We confirmed that certain restriction enzymes can effectively linearize circular dsDNAs of 250 ng within this timeframe (Figure S7 , Supporting Information). The resulting linear dsDNA markers, produced through the three sequential enzyme reactions, were subsequently purified, stained with a fluorescent dye, and loaded onto a microfluidic chip.

We applied the imaging‐based virus detection method to replicating live IAV and assessed its analytical sensitivity. IAV‐specific dsDNA markers were generated using serial dilutions of viral RNAs, ranging from 1.4 × 10 8 to 1.4 × 10 1 GEs, following our outlined methodology. A 12.0 kb sscDNA probe was employed in the detection. The resulting products were diluted 100‐fold and visualized using single‐molecule DNA imaging (Figure 5b ). Detection of the virus was successfully achieved with 143 GEs of IAV per reaction. A total of 798 linear DNA molecules were detected, approximately twenty‐one times more than the non‐target virus sample (containing 3.4 × 10 8 GEs of MLV), which yielded 37 molecules. With the exception of the highest concentration sample, our results indicated a weak positive correlation between the number of viral RNAs and the number of linear dsDNA molecules detected (Figure 5c ).

To determine the limit of detection (LOD), we analyzed the undiluted detection reaction sample derived from viral RNAs, which contained 14.3 GEs (Figure 5d ; Figure S9 , Supporting Information). Imaging analysis of this reaction sample revealed the presence of 541 linear marker DNA molecules, significantly exceeding the 21 molecules found in the corresponding non‐target MLV sample (obtained from 3.4 × 10 8 GEs of MLV, Figure 5d,e ).

The reactions were conducted in a one‐pot system at temperatures below 42 °C. This method has demonstrated effectiveness in detecting as few as 14 GE copies within 40 min, encompassing the entire process from viral RNA extraction to imaging. This sensitivity is comparable to that of conventional RT‐qPCR, which is known to have a detection limit of 10–100 GE copies per reaction. [ 8 ] RT‐qPCR that we performed showed a sensitivity of 143 GE copies per reaction for the detection of the same IAV (Figure 5f ). Notably, our RT‐qPCR analysis required at least two hours to complete.

Our method demonstrated a linear relationship between the level of viral RNAs in the samples and the number of detected marker molecules, provided that the RNA levels were not excessively high (as evidenced by the samples containing 14.3 and 143 GEs of viral RNAs, Figure 5c ). In contrast, when elevated levels of viral RNAs were analyzed, a definitive linear relationship between these two quantities was not established (as shown by the samples containing >14 300 GEs of viral RNAs, Figure 5c ).

This finding can be attributed to two key properties of our method. First, linear marker molecules are recognized through fluorescence imaging when they bind to the surface of the microfluidic system, where exciting light is projected and emission light is directed toward the sensor. Consequently, even if numerous marker molecules are formed from high concentrations of viral RNAs, not all of them are detected. Second, enzymatic reactions typically yield maximum product output only within optimal substrate concentration ranges. [ 16 ] Similarly, both low and high concentrations of viral RNAs can result in suboptimal levels of virus‐specific marker molecules in our detection protocol.

While we anticipated a low yield of marker molecules at low viral RNA levels, we specifically investigated how high levels of viral RNAs impact marker formation. To explore this, we conducted RT using the M13mp18 ssDNA probe along with two RNA oligomers that bind to specific sites on the probe. The concentrations of the RNA oligomers varied from 150 fmol to 1.5 nmol. The highest yield of marker molecules was obtained at a moderate concentration of 15 pmol. However, further increasing the concentration of virus‐derived RNAs resulted in decreased marker formation (Figure S8 , Supporting Information).


## Detection of HIV‐1 from Clinical Samples

We finally assessed the efficacy of our method for detecting HIV‐1 in clinical samples. Seven blood samples were obtained, representing five HIV‐1‐infected patients and two healthy individuals. RNAs in the sample was extracted and purified using a column‐based kit, during which DNase I was applied to eliminate any linear DNA contaminants from the cellular materials in blood, as confirmed by single‐molecule DNA imaging (Figure S10 , Supporting Information). HIV‐1‐specific markers were generated with 9.6 kb sscDNA probes, and a 9.6 kb PCR amplicon sample was also prepared as a positive control. The selection of probe length is crucial for effective imaging analysis of marker molecules. We opted for a minimum length to ensure visibility of marker molecules under the microscope, as shorter probes may not produce distinguishable signals. Conversely, longer probes can result in lower detection efficiencies due to potentially hindered hybridization with virus‐derived nucleic acids, caused by possible intramolecular base pairing within the probes. After careful consideration of these factors, we determined that a 9.6 kb length would provide an optimal balance, enabling effective detection of viruses in challenging clinical samples while maintaining adequate yields for our analyses. To attain an analyzable concentration of individual DNA molecules, all the DNA products were diluted 100‐fold before being stained with YOYO‐1 for fluorescence imaging.

As shown in Figure 6 a , a significantly higher number of linear dsDNA molecules were detected in both the positive control and the HIV‐1‐infected samples compared to the HIV‐1 negative controls, which exhibited only a few linear DNA molecules. We subsequently measured the lengths of linear DNAs and presented the data as histograms (Figure 6b ). In the HIV‐1 negative samples, only 30 and 26 linear DNA molecules were recognized, whereas the HIV‐1‐infected samples yielded 1119, 930, 2340, 837, and 1620 linear DNA molecules for patients 1 through 5, respectively. We compared the length distributions of the dsDNA molecules from the patient samples to that of the 9.6 kb PCR amplicon. While some patient samples exhibited a broader peak in the histogram, they consistently peaked within the length of 2.0–2.4 µm range, similar to that of the PCR amplicon. This clear distinction in linear DNA formation between the samples containing target viral RNA and those lacking it underscores the suitability of our imaging‐based virus detection method for identifying viral RNA in complex biological specimens, such as blood.

## Discussion

In this study, we developed a single‐molecule DNA imaging‐based method for detecting RNA viruses, demonstrating its effectiveness with both laboratory‐generated and patient samples. Our approach harnesses the formation of hundreds or thousands of DNA markers through a single probe filling reaction, achieving detection limits comparable to that of conventional RT‐qPCR. The high sensitivity of our method is largely attributed to its ability to directly visualize individual marker molecules through fluorescence imaging. Additionally, the production of multiple RNA fragments from viral genomes, functioning as primers for the probe filling reaction from a single viral particle, can result in the generation of multiple copies of virus‐specific markers, further increasing detection sensitivity. The use of single‐stranded circular DNA as probes enhances the likelihood of marker formation, as randomly generated virus‐derived RNA primers can bind to various sites within the probes and enable almost complete filling of the probe molecules during RT.

Importantly, our method eliminates the need for nucleic acid amplification, streamlining the diagnostic process, reducing the time required to obtain detection results, and mitigating the risk of false positives—a common challenge in traditional methods. [ 17 ] We further demonstrate the capability of our technique to identify viruses within complex samples, such as blood, thereby affirming its utility in clinical diagnostics. This advancement may represent a meaningful leap in the field of viral diagnostics, particularly for highly pathogenic RNA virus infections, where rapid and accurate detection is essential.

As the world continues to face emerging viral threats, the demand for accurate, rapid, and accessible testing becomes increasingly vital. Our method with a narrow and low temperature operation range offers a straightforward solution that bypasses the complexities of thermal cycling and the extensive equipment typically associated with RT‐qPCR (Figure S1 , Supporting Information), thereby lowering barriers to implementation in diverse healthcare settings.

Based on the required reagents and equipment for preparing probes, generating, and imaging markers, we estimated the per‐test cost to be ≈$3.63, with the associated device costing ≈$4,263 (see Table S2 , Supporting Information). This cost is comparable to that of the RT‐qPCR‐based method, which has a per‐test cost of ≈$2.96 (for a commercial kit) and a device price of ≈$24 000. If our method is commercialized and involves large‐scale production of the detection kit, the cost per test could be significantly reduced. Consequently, our method presents a cost‐effective alternative for virus detection, particularly advantageous in resource‐limited settings where sophisticated laboratory infrastructure is often lacking.

The imaging capabilities of our method enable detailed visualization of individual dsDNA markers based on their length and color‐staining patterns, fostering a level of granularity in analysis that enhances both the accuracy and reliability of detection results. This enhancement can ultimately contribute to better epidemiological tracking, quicker patient isolation, and more effective treatment protocols during outbreaks.

Furthermore, our method's potential for multiplexing may enable healthcare providers to screen for multiple viral pathogens in a single test, which is particularly relevant in the context of co‐infections. The growing incidence of multi‐virus infections necessitates a comprehensive diagnostic approach that can accommodate the complexity of viral epidemiology. By employing multiple sscDNA probes, this technique may allow for simultaneous monitoring of several viruses, advancing our capabilities for comprehensive diagnostic assessments.

Our method involves three enzymatic reactions that can be performed sequentially in a single tube. Further integration of the steps for RNA extraction from viral particles and purification of the generated marker molecules could significantly simplify the diagnostic process. To facilitate these combined processes, a moderately designed microfluidic chip can be employed. To detect the marker molecules, we utilized a fluorescence microscope. Although not illustrated here, we attempted to develop a simple optical system comprising low‐quality lenses, optical filters, and a camera, which yielded adequately proficient detection results for viruses. As a next step in our research, we envision the realization of a cost‐effective optical system with a single chip that integrates multiple detection sub‐steps along with addition of the necessary reagents. This advancement has the potential to enhance the accessibility and efficiency of our diagnostic method across various settings.

However, we also acknowledge certain limitations in our study. The current reverse transcriptase used for the probe‐filling reaction exhibits strand displacement activity and shows sluggish extension from RNA primers, which hinders the formation of uniform marker molecules. It is also important to consider that dsDNA can be extended differently due to the flexible spatial displacement between the covalent bonds of phosphate and sugar in the DNA backbone. This molecular feature contributes to the length variation observed among identical dsDNA molecules. Addressing these factors will be crucial for improving the uniformity and quantitative accuracy of our method.


## Conclusion

Our single‐molecule DNA imaging‐based diagnostic method represents a new and promising tool for the direct detection of RNA viruses. Its capacity to accurately, sensitively, and rapidly identify viruses without the need for nucleic acid amplification marks a significant advancement in the field of viral diagnostics, particularly in POC settings. This approach offers an innovative solution that mitigates the typically reduced sensitivity associated with portable diagnostic systems, bringing its performance closer to that of advanced laboratory equipment. By enabling effective detection of RNA viruses, our method stands to improve diagnostic capabilities in diverse clinical contexts.


## Experimental Section


## Construction of Phagemids for Producing sscDNA Probes

Five different sscDNA probes were designed for the detection of MLV, HIV‐1, and IAV. These probes were generated utilizing the M13KO7 helper phage replication system, wherein specific phagemids were engineered to serve as templates for DNA amplification within bacterial cells. To construct the 4.4 and 6.9 kb phagemids designed for HIV‐1 and MLV detection, respectively, PCR amplicons containing were inserted a 1.4 kb fragment of the non‐replicating model HIV‐1 genome and a 3.9 kb fragment of the model MLV genome into the multiple cloning sites of the pBluescript SK II (+) phagemid, which contains the f1 origin of replication. This insertion was accomplished through restriction enzyme digestion followed by ligation. The orientation of the inserted viral DNA fragments was aligned with the f1 origin of replication to ensure that the strand containing the complementary sequences of the viral RNA was replicated as ssDNA. Similarly, additional phagemids were built to generate the 11.7 and 9.6 kb probes, both designed for HIV‐1 detection. The 11.7 kb phagemid was first constructed using a larger phagemid vector that contained sequences specific to HIV‐1. To enhance the efficiency of detecting replicating live HIV‐1, an additional 3.2 kb sequence from the HIV‐1 pol gene was incorporated into the constructs. The 9.6 kb phagemid was subsequently derived from the 11.7 kb phagemid by excising a 2.1 kb segment of HIV‐1 genome. For the 12.0 kb phagemid designed for IAV detection, dummy sequences unrelated to the target viral genomes were included to meet the required size specification. The detailed components of each phagemid, including the incorporated viral genomic regions, are illustrated in Figure S2 (Supporting Information).


## Production of sscDNA Probes

To produce sscDNA probes, the genome replication mechanism of the M13KO7 helper phage (NEB, Ipswich, MA, USA) was utilized, a derivative of the M13 bacteriophage. [ 18 ] Initially, 10 pg of the phagemid was introduced into XL1‐Blue MRF' cells, which are characterized by their expression of F‐pili due to the presence of the harboring F plasmid. To select for the transformed cells, 2xYT agar plates were prepared containing final concentrations of 5 µg mL −1 tetracycline and 100 µg mL −1 ampicillin. Tetracycline acted as a selective marker to ensure that only cells containing the F plasmid—and thus capable of expressing the F‐pili—would grow, while ampicillin was employed to select for cells carrying the phagemid DNA. Afterward, a single colony was inoculated into 5 mL of 2xYT medium and grown overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 250 rpm. The bacterial culture was then scaled up to 100 mL and incubated until the optical density at 600 nm reached 0.2–0.3. Transduction of the transformed bacteria with M13KO7 helper phages was performed at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. Following an additional 1.5‐h incubation, kanamycin was added to a final concentration of 25 µg mL −1 to inhibit the growth of non‐transduced cells that lacked the kanamycin resistance genes encoded by M13KO7. The phages released from the transduced cells were harvested after a three‐hour incubation. To precipitate the phages carrying the single‐stranded phagemids, 0.2 volumes of a solution was added containing 20% w/v polyethylene glycol 8000 and 2.5 m NaCl to the supernatant from the bacterial culture, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C. The precipitated phages were separated from the supernatant by centrifugation (MEGA 17R, Hanil, Korea) at 4 °C and 12 000 g for 30 min and then suspended in nuclease‐free water. Finally, single‐stranded probes were extracted from the phage particles using the DNA Clean & Concentrator‐5 kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions.


## Cell Culture and Virus Packaging

HEK 293T cells (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in Isocove's modified Dulbecco's medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at the condition of 37 °C and 5% CO 2 atmosphere. For packaging HIV‐1 model virus particles, plasmids encoding the viral genome (pFUGW, 10 µg), the Gag‐Pol polyprotein (pMDL gag‐pol, 5 µg), the vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) envelope glycoprotein (pcDNA IVS VSVG, 3.5 µg), and a regulatory protein (pRSV‐REV, 1.5 µg) were introduced into HEK 293T cells using a calcium phosphate‐based transfection method. Sixteen hours post‐transfection, the cells were washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, Thermo Fisher scientific) and replenished with fresh culture medium. At 36 and 60 h post‐transfection, the supernatant containing the viral particles was collected and filtered through a 0.45‐µm syringe filter. The virus particles were then concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 4 °C and 24 000 rpm for 2 h using an ultracentrifuge (Optima LE‐80K, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) equipped with an SW28 rotor (Beckman Coulter). The concentrated virus particles were resuspended in PBS. For packaging the MLV model virus, three plasmids were transfected into HEK 293T cells: the plasmids, each encoding the viral genome (pCLPIT GFP, 10 µg), the Gag‐Pol polyprotein (pCMV gag‐pol, 6 µg), and the VSV envelope glycoproteins (pcDNA IVS VSVG, 4 µg). Live IAV was produced by transfecting HEK 293T cells with eight plasmids, each containing the cDNAs for the segmented genomes of the A/WSN/33 strain, along with four protein expression plasmids encoding the PB2, PB1, PA, and NP genes. For the MLV model virus and IAV production, the substeps for post‐transfection washing, virus harvest, purification, and concentration followed the same protocols applied for the HIV‐1 model virus packaging.


## Isolation and Fragmentation of Viral Genomic RNA

Genomic RNA was extracted from the viruses using the Quick‐RNA Viral Kit (Zymo Research). To eliminate cellular genomic DNA, the extracted RNA was treated with DNase I (NEB) following the lysis step. The viral RNA was subsequently fragmented by applying 1.0 unit of Ambion RNase III (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a low‐salt buffer containing 5 m m NaCl, 10 m m Tris‐HCl, 10 m m MgCl 2 , and 1 m m 10x dithiothreitol (DTT) (pH 7.9, NEB) at 37 °C for 10 min. [ 19 ] The reaction temperature was effectively maintained using a temperature‐controlled dry bath (KA.31‐15, Korea Ace Scientific, South Korea). Blood samples were collected from five patients diagnosed with HIV‐1 infection, as well as from two healthy individuals. The study involving the detection of HIV‐1 from clinical specimens was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Asan Medical Center (IRB [YEAR]‐0905) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant local regulatory requirements. All participants provided written informed consent.


## Formation of dsDNA Markers

To generate virus‐specific dsDNA markers, a RT was performed. The reaction mixture contained 125 ng of sscDNA, 5 µL of viral RNA fragments, 0.3 µL of Moloney MLV reverse transcriptase (60 units, NEB), 2 µL of 10x RT buffer (NEB), 0.5 µL of 10 m m dNTP (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 µL of 10x DTT, 0.2 µL of 40 m m RNase inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and nuclease‐free distilled water to make a final volume of 20 µL. This mixture was incubated at 42 °C for 10 min. The subsequently formed dsDNA markers were linearized using a restriction endonuclease that recognizes a single site within the marker. NotI was used for samples with the 6.9 or 12.0 kb dsDNA markers, BamHI for the sample with the 9.6 kb dsDNA marker, and AgeI for the sample with both 4.4 and 9.6 kb dsDNA markers. For the linearization reaction, 20 µL of the RT product was combined with 1 µL of the appropriate restriction endonuclease (NotI, BamHI, or AgeI), 10 µL of 10x buffer (NEB), and 69 µL of nuclease‐free water (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 10 min. The final product was purified using the Wizard SV Gel and PCR Clean‐Up System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). A complete list of the primers used in this study is shown in Table S1 (Supporting Information).


## RT‐qPCR

To evaluate the analytical sensitivity of our method, RT‐qPCR was also performed as a reference. The RT‐qPCR reaction was conducted in a total volume of 10 µL, incorporating 1 µL of viral RNA, 5 µL of iTaq Universal SYBR Green reaction mix (2x) (Biorad, Hercules, CA, USA), and 1 µL of each forward and reverse primer targeting the M gene of IAV (3 µ m ). The remaining volume was adjusted with nuclease‐free water. The primer sequences were: forward primer, 5'‐CTTCTAACCGAGGTCGAAACGTA‐3', and reverse primer, 5'‐GGTGACAGGATTGGTCTTGTCTTTA‐3'. The RT‐qPCR reactions were conducted using the CFX96 Touch Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad) under the following thermal cycling conditions: an initial reverse transcriptase step at 50 °C for 10 min, followed by an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min. This was succeeded by 40 cycles consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and extension/annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. Fluorescence data were collected during the 60 °C phase of each cycle, and melting curve analysis was additionally performed to verify the specificity of the amplified products.


## PDMS Microfluidic Devices

A soft lithography method was employed to fabricate a PDMS microfluidic device mold designed for DNA deposition and elongation on a positively charged surface. Microchannels were patterned on silicon wafers using SU‐8 TF 6001 and SU‐8 TF 6002 photoresists (Kayaku Advanced Materials, Westborough, MA), following the procedures established in previous studies. [ 9 , 20 ] To create the PDMS device, a mixture of PDMS polymer base and curing agent (Sylgard 184, K1 Solution, Korea) was prepared at a 10:1 weight ratio, which was then poured onto the patterned wafer and cured at 65 °C for 4 h. Following curing, the PDMS device underwent plasma treatment for 1 min at 100 W in a vacuum plasma system (Femto Science Cute Basic, Korea) to render the PDMS surface hydrophilic. The PDMS devices were stored in deionized water at room temperature and air‐dried before being affixed to a positively charged coverslip for subsequent use.


## Glass Surface Preparation

The glass coverslips intended for DNA deposition were thoroughly cleaned using a piranha etching solution (30:70 v/v H 2 O 2 /H 2 SO 4 ) for 3 h. Following the process, the coverslips were rinsed thoroughly with deionized water, sonicated for 30 min, and rinsed again with deionized water to ensure complete removal of the piranha solution. To impart positive charges to the coverslips, the piranha‐cleaned surfaces were incubated in 250 mL of deionized water containing 70 µL of N‐trimethoxymethylsilylpropyl‐N,N,N‐trimethylammonium chloride in 50% methanol for 15 h at 65 °C. After incubation, the coverslips were rinsed three times with 99.9% ethanol and stored in 99.9% ethanol. The positively charged glass surfaces were air‐dried before use and were utilized within two weeks of preparation.


## Fluorescence Imaging of dsDNA Marker Molecules

For imaging DNA molecules, an inverted microscope was employed (Olympus IX70, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a 100× Olympus UPlanSApo oil immersion objective. Illumination was provided by a light‐emitting diode light source (SOLA SM II light engine, Lumencor, Beaverton, OR, USA). The emitted light was filtered using filter sets appropriate for YOYO‐1 (Semrock, Rochester, NY, USA) to establish the correct excitation wavelength. Fluorescence images were captured using a scientific‐grade complementary metal‐oxide‐semiconductor (sCMOS) digital camera (2048 × 2048, Prime sCMOS Camera, Photometrics, Tucson, AZ, USA) and stored in 16‐bit TIFF format with Micro‐Manager software. A 1:1 mixture of the DNA sample solution in TE buffer (10 m m Tris, 1 m m EDTA, pH 8) and a YOYO‐1 solution (100 n m in 10% β‐mercaptoethanol) was loaded into the microfluidic device and allowed to stand for 10 min to facilitate the flow of the solution through the microchannel. The DNA molecules within the microfluidic device were then imaged using the fluorescence microscope. For AT sequence‐specific staining of the DNA molecules, a method reported in a previous study was followed. [ 10c ] The DNA solution was combined with tTALE‐mNG at a final concentration of 2–20 n m and 100 m m NaCl, then incubated at room temperature for 10 min to allow for adequate binding of tTALE‐mNG to the DNA. After staining, the mixture was loaded into the PDMS microfluidic device and imaged using the same protocol applied for the YOYO‐1 stained DNA molecules. Image processing and analysis were conducted using ImageJ with Java plugins, complemented by custom Python programs to enhance data evaluation. In the processing of DNA images, a rolling ball radius of 50 pixels was established to identify and eliminate background noise from signals with variations of this magnitude. To further reduce noise, a smoothing filter was applied, which aided in the subsequent thresholding of the signals. The triangle method was employed to automatically determine the threshold value. The original grayscale images were then converted to a binary format suitable for downstream analysis. To filter out unreacted probes, color spots were excluded with an area of 100 pixels squared or less, as well as those with a circularity >0.7, from the analysis.
