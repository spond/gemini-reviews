## Abstract

Small RNAs (sRNAs) derived from the 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs) of bacterial messenger RNAs (mRNAs) represent a secondary functional output of transcripts, yet their evolutionary origins and adaptive roles remain underexplored. This study traces the evolutionary trajectory of UhpU, a 3′ UTR–derived sRNA from the uhpT gene, by comparing its biogenesis and regulatory functions in Escherichia and Salmonella . We show UhpU likely originated as a processed sRNA regulating phosphotransferase system genes to maintain hexose phosphate homeostasis. In Escherichia coli , UhpU gained an additional function in repressing regulator MprA, following a horizontal gene transfer and the subsequent acquisition of an independent promoter. These findings illustrate how sRNAs evolve and acquire additional regulatory roles that help bacteria adapt to diverse conditions.

Keywords: bacteria sRNA, adaptive evolution, post-transcriptional regulation, 3’ UTR, RNase E

Bacterial small noncoding RNAs (sRNAs) play a critical role in fine-tuning gene expression at the posttranscriptional level, acting in concert with transcriptional regulators to enable rapid adaptation to environmental changes ( 1 ). Most sRNAs exert their effects through imperfect base-pairing with the 5′ region of target mRNAs, a process that is typically mediated by RNA chaperones such as Hfq or ProQ ( 2 , 3 ). These interactions can repress translation initiation by occluding the ribosome binding site, or modulate mRNA stability by masking or exposing RNase recognition elements ( 4 , 5 ). In some cases, sRNA pairing within coding sequences may also impact translation elongation or cotranslational protein folding, further contributing to their regulatory versatility ( 6 ).

While bacterial sRNAs were initially characterized as independently transcribed elements from intergenic regions, it is now recognized that a subset of sRNAs originates from the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR) of mRNAs ( 7 ). These 3′-derived sRNAs are generated through two distinct biogenesis mechanisms ( 8 ): Type I sRNAs, such as DapZ and MicL, are transcribed from internal promoters located within protein-coding sequences, yielding sRNA transcripts that are independent of the overlapping mRNA ( 9 , 10 ). In these cases, the sRNA and the protein encoded by the overlapping gene are subject to separate transcriptional control. In contrast, Type II sRNAs are produced by endonucleolytic cleavage of an mRNA, by RNase E or RNase III in Gram-negative bacteria ( 11 – 13 ). These processed sRNAs often retain a Rho-independent terminator and are stabilized by RNA chaperones such as Hfq or ProQ. Chaperone binding enables base-pairing with target mRNAs, thereby modulating transcript stability or translation efficiency. Because Type II sRNAs share a transcriptional signal with their parental gene, they frequently regulate functionally related genes within the same metabolic or regulatory pathway ( 14 – 17 ). These regulatory sRNAs often operate alongside transcription factors in coherent or incoherent feed-forward loops to fine-tune gene expression dynamics ( 18 , 19 ).

Given their critical role in posttranscriptional gene regulation, understanding the origin and evolutionary trajectories of bacterial sRNAs is essential—particularly for Type II sRNAs derived from the 3′ UTR of mRNAs ( 20 ). These sRNAs extend the functional repertoire of protein-coding genes by generating distinct regulatory outputs. As such, investigations of coding gene evolution are incomplete without consideration of their associated noncoding elements. However, exploring the origin and evolution of bacterial sRNAs poses unique challenges due to their molecular characteristics ( 21 ). Unlike coding regions, where evolutionary pressures can be quantitatively assessed using metrics such as the ratio of nonsynonymous to synonymous substitutions (dN/dS) ( 22 ), sequence variations within the functional domains of sRNAs are difficult to interpret without target validation and experimental confirmation ( 23 ). This complicates efforts to identify positively or negatively selected residues across phylogenies. Furthermore, many sRNAs, especially those transcribed independently of coding genes, exhibit broad and often condition-dependent target spectra ( 24 ). Their functional plasticity facilitates rapid sequence evolution across bacterial lineages, particularly within the seed region that mediates target recognition ( 25 ). As a result, distinguishing bona fide sRNA–mRNA interactions from transient or nonfunctional associations remains challenging ( 26 ). While comparative genomics and computational analyses have offered valuable insights into the gain and loss of sRNAs across bacterial taxa ( 27 – 30 ), experimentally validated models that illustrate the emergence and adaptive evolution of functional sRNAs remain scarce.

In this study, we address a long-standing gap in understanding the evolution of bacterial sRNAs by comparing the biogenesis and regulatory functions of UhpU, an sRNA derived from the 3′ region of the uhpT coding gene in Escherichia and Salmonella . Our findings suggest that UhpU may have originated as a Type II sRNA processed from the 3′ UTR of uhpT mRNA by RNase E and that it ancestrally contributed to the repression of genes in the phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)-dependent sugar phosphotransferase system (PTS)—a pathway functionally linked to its parental gene, uhpT , which encodes a hexose phosphate transporter. This regulatory function may have supported hexose phosphate homeostasis and provided a selective advantage favoring the emergence of UhpU. We further show that within the Escherichia genus, UhpU evolved an additional function by posttranscriptionally repressing the transcriptional repressor MprA, a key regulator of multidrug efflux pumps. This regulatory connection was enabled by a horizontally acquired DNA insertion that extended the mprA 5′ UTR to include a UhpU-binding site. Following divergence from the common ancestor with Escherichia albertii , other Escherichia species, including Escherichia coli , Escherichia fergusonii , and Shigella acquired a mutation in the uhpT coding sequence that created a FliA-dependent promoter, allowing independent transcription of UhpU. This newly evolved promoter expanded UhpU expression beyond the conditions that induce uhpT , establishing it as a dual-biogenesis sRNA with both Type I and Type II characteristics. Although the precise sequence of evolutionary events cannot be definitively reconstructed, our results support a stepwise model in which UhpU expanded from a processing-derived metabolic regulator to an sRNA with broader regulatory connections and a FliA-dependent transcriptional program within a specific lineage of Escherichia .


## Results


## Lineage-Specific Acquisition of FliA-Activated UhpU Expression.

The UhpU sRNA was initially identified in E. coli through Hfq RIL-seq (RNA interaction by ligation and sequencing) ( 31 , 32 ). It is transcribed independently of its overlapping gene uhpT , which encodes a hexose phosphate transporter ( Fig. 1 A ) ( 33 ). The region of uhpU overlapping with the uhpT ORF is highly conserved, while the portion of uhpU outside the uhpT ORF shows lower conservation across different genera within the Enterobacteriaceae family ( SI Appendix , Fig. S1 ). Transcription of UhpU is regulated by the alternative sigma factor FliA (σ28) from an intragenic promoter located within the uhpT ORF ( Fig. 1 A ) ( 34 ). This results in two isoforms: a long UhpU (UhpU-L) isoform, which is over 200 nucleotides in length, and a small isoform (UhpU-S), approximately 70 nucleotides long, which predominantly accumulates during the mid-exponential growth phase (OD 600 ~ 1.0) in LB broth, and both isoforms were significantly affected by fliA deletion ( Fig. 1 B ). Deletion of hfq abolished the RNA levels of both UhpU-L and UhpU-S as expected. Differential RNA-seq analysis indicated that UhpU-L is a primary transcript of 265 nucleotides ( 35 ), which is insensitive to treatment with TEX (Terminator 5’-Phosphate-Dependent Exonuclease), an enzyme that specifically digests processed RNA with 5’-monophosphate ends. In contrast, UhpU-S is a processed sRNA of ~68 nucleotides that is sensitive to TEX treatment.

RIL-seq analyses have demonstrated that UhpU acts as a regulatory hub in E. coli , interacting with hundreds of mRNA targets ( 31 , 32 ). In contrast, only a limited number of mRNA interactions were detected in Salmonella Typhimurium ( S . Typhimurium) ( 36 , 37 ), which is closely related to E. coli in the phylogenetic tree. Further examination of published RNA-seq data from S . Typhimurium grown in LB medium revealed extremely low expression levels of UhpU ( 38 ), consistent with our northern blot results, which showed undetectable levels of UhpU in S . Typhimurium during LB growth ( SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A ). This suggests that the reduced UhpU–mRNA pairing observed in S . Typhimurium RIL-seq data may be due to its low expression levels.

When we analyzed the conservation of FliA-binding sites within the uhpT sequence across representative strains from different Enterobacteriaceae genera ( SI Appendix , Table S1 ), we found that only Escherichia and Shigella possess perfectly matched intragenic FliA binding sites: “TAAAAA” at the −35 region and “GCCGAT” at the −10 region, as previously reported ( Fig. 1 A ) ( 39 ). In contrast, other genera exhibited mutations in these regions. For example, the −35 region showed a T AAAAA to G AAAAA mutation, while the −10 region exhibited mutations such as GC C GA T to GC G GA C in Salmonella and Citrobacter , and GCCGA T to GCCGA C in Cedecea . These are all synonymous substitutions that do not alter the coding sequence of the UhpT protein. These findings suggest that the low expression of UhpU in Salmonella may result from mutations in the FliA binding sites. To test this hypothesis, we cloned the UhpU-L sequence, along with the intragenic FliA-binding site carrying mutations that mimic the sequences found in Salmonella at either the −35 or −10 regions, into a plasmid to assess the impact on UhpU expression in an E. coli strain carrying a uhpTU deletion. Indeed, we found that mutations in either the −35 or −10 regions, individually or together, both significantly reduced UhpU-L and UhpU-S levels ( Fig. 1 C ). Consistently, transcriptional GFP reporter assays showed that the wild-type (WT) promoter sequence drove nearly an order of magnitude higher fluorescence than any of the mutated variants ( SI Appendix, Fig. S3 ). Moreover, introducing these reporter constructs into a Δ fliA background resulted in uniformly low GFP expression across all variants, at levels comparable to the mutated constructs in WT cells, confirming that the transcription is FliA-dependent and the mutations directly impair its expression.

Considering that only Escherichia – Shigella lineage harbor the intragenic FliA-binding site, it is reasonable to infer that the common ancestor of Enterobacteriaceae lacked this site and that Escherichia acquired it through mutation after diverging from its most recent common ancestor (MRCA) with Salmonella spp. To examine this, we performed ancestral state reconstruction (ASR) using all available genome sequences of strains from different Enterobacteriaceae genera in the NCBI database ( SI Appendix , Table S1 ) ( 40 , 41 ). The analysis supported that the functional intragenic FliA-binding site was specifically acquired in the Escherichia – Shigella lineage (Posterior probability = 0.9998; Fig. 1 A ). Moreover, expanding the analysis within Escherichia species including E. albertii , E. fergusonii , various E. coli phylogroups and major pathotypes, and cryptic clades I–V revealed that E. albertii and clades II–V harbor the inactive FliA-binding site ( SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Dataset S1 ) ( 42 ), whereas clade I, E. fergusonii and E. coli contains the active FliA-binding site (Posterior probability = 0.9870). Consistently, northern blotting confirmed the absence of UhpU expression in E. albertii during LB growth ( SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B ). Together, these findings suggest that the intragenic FliA-binding site was most likely acquired in the common ancestor of clade I, E. fergusonii , and E. coli , thereby integrating UhpU into the FliA regulon in this lineage.


## The Target Spectrum of UhpU in E. coli .

We were interested in understanding what selective pressures drove this evolutionary change. To explore this, we initially investigated the biological function of UhpU by screening its potential targets. Although RIL-seq data suggested that UhpU serves as a regulatory hub, deletion of uhpU did not significantly affect transcriptome of E. coli when cultured in LB medium ( SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and Dataset S2 ). Only four genes ( ygeN , yriA , yibS, and ymiB ), all with low expression levels, exhibited significant changes of more than a twofold difference, and none of them had predicted UhpU binding sites at the 5’ end according to IntaRNA analysis ( 43 , 44 ). When comparing the expression of UhpU-paired mRNAs identified in the RIL-seq data between WT and UhpU-deleted strains, only two genes— fliC , encoding the flagellar filament flagellin, and srlD , coding for sorbitol-6-phosphate 2-dehydrogenase—showed significant changes ( P -values < 0.05), with minimal fold changes (~1.1-fold) ( SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B ). These findings suggest a disconnect between UhpU base-pairing and its actual regulatory effect on mRNA under the conditions tested.

To identify the targets regulated by UhpU, we conducted classical pulse-expression combined with RNA-seq screening. UhpU-L was cloned into a plasmid downstream of the pBAD promoter, and its expression was induced with L-arabinose for 10 min in LB broth. We identified 36 genes whose expression was significantly altered ( P -values < 0.05) by more than twofold, including 22 down-regulated and 14 up-regulated genes upon UhpU-L pulse-expression ( Fig. 2 A and Dataset S3 ). Among these, seven were previously identified as UhpU interactors in RIL-seq data, including the N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) PTS permease coding gene nagE , the ribose pyranase coding gene rbsD , the pyrimidine ribonucleoside hydrolase coding gene rihA , the galactose transporter coding gene mglB , the hypothetical gene yeaL , and the sRNA GcvB, which were repressed by UhpU-L, as well as the porin coding gene ompF , which was induced. Interestingly, three of the top 10 repressed genes included ptsH , which encodes the phosphocarrier protein HPr of the PTS, nagE and the sRNA GlmZ, which is involved in GlcNAc metabolism. Given that UhpU is located within the uhpT gene, which is involved in hexose phosphate metabolism, these findings suggest that the biological function of UhpU may be linked to UhpT, even though UhpU is independently transcribed.

## UhpU Represses Expression of the Transcription Regulator MprA in E. coli .

Our initial goal was to explore the biological function of UhpU and the selective pressure that led to the acquisition of a FliA binding site for its activation. To achieve this, we first examined whether UhpU could be integrated into FliA-regulated coherent or incoherent feed-forward loops. We did not observe significant changes in RNA levels upon UhpU-L pulse expression of any genes from the FliA regulon, as previously identified by ChIP-seq in E. coli ( Dataset S4 ) ( 45 ). However, lrhA , which encodes a transcriptional regulator of flagellar genes and was previously reported as a UhpU target ( 34 ), was significantly repressed (~1.81-fold). qRT-PCR at extended time points confirmed sustained lrhA repression together with upregulation of flhD and flhC , which encode the master activator of the flagellar regulon ( SI Appendix , Fig. S6 ). Since LrhA represses flhDC , and FlhDC in turn activates fliA , these findings suggest that UhpU participates in a multistep coherent feed-forward motif linking its own activation by FliA to regulation of the flagellar cascade. Such a role may have contributed to the evolutionary acquisition of the FliA-binding site within uhpT .

Because lrhA was not among the top UhpU regulated transcripts, we considered the possibility that other regulatory interactions may also provide the selective benefit driving acquisition of the FliA-binding site. To explore this, we used IntaRNA to predict base-pairing between UhpU-L and the most strongly regulated mRNAs identified in the pulse-expression assay. Intriguingly, we found that the predicted binding site on mprA (also known as emrR , which codes for a transcriptional repressor involved in microcin B17 synthesis and multidrug efflux), the gene most significantly regulated by UhpU-L, is located upstream of the annotated transcriptional start site (TSS), as determined by differential RNA-seq ( Fig. 2 B ) ( 35 ). This raised the possibility of an alternative TSS that could extend the 5’ UTR of mprA , thereby covering the predicted UhpU binding site. Furthermore, RIL-seq data identified a UhpU-interaction pair that included the predicted region, which was annotated to the upstream gene ygaH , as the captured region starts within the ygaH ORF ( 32 ). Notably, ygaH is part of the ygaZH operon, which encodes an L-valine exporter and is restricted to a limited number of Enterobacteriaceae genera. ASR revealed a near-certain probability of presence (0.9999) at the MRCA of Escherichia and Shigella , contrasting with low probabilities at more basal Enterobacteriaceae nodes ( SI Appendix , Fig. S7 ). This pattern supports an acquisition event in Escherichia , likely via horizontal gene transfer after divergence from the MRCA with Salmonella spp. Such an event may have provided the genomic context that facilitated the emergence of lineage-specific UhpU-mediated regulation of mprA .

To explore this further, we performed northern blotting to examine different isoforms of the mprA transcript and used 5’ RACE (Rapid Amplification of cDNA Ends) to investigate the corresponding 5’ ends. To our surprise, the main isoform we detected was not consistent with the previously annotated transcript from the mprA-emrAB operon (~3.5 kb, Fig. 2 B ). Instead, we observed a primary isoform around 900 nt, whose level was induced ~1.91-fold upon uhpTU deletion at OD 600 ~1.0, as quantified by qRT-PCR, and was fully abolished in the mprA -deleted strain ( Fig. 2 C and SI Appendix , Fig. S8 A ). Moreover, pulse expression of UhpU-L also reduced the level of this main isoform ( Fig. 2 D and SI Appendix , Fig. S8 B ), suggesting this mprA -containing transcript is indeed regulated by UhpU.

We next investigated which end corresponded to this ~900 nt isoform. If it originated from the previously annotated TSS, the 3’ end would be located within the downstream emrA gene, at approximately the ~100th codon region ( Fig. 2 B ). However, we did not find a significant Rho-independent terminator structure or a Rho-dependent terminator sequence in this region, thus excluding this possibility. In contrast, we identified another 5’ end besides the previously annotated transcriptional start site of mprA by RACE, located within the upstream ygaH gene at the 49th amino acid coding region ( SI Appendix , Fig. S9 ), which was also sensitive to RNA 5’ polyphosphatase that dephosphorylates 5’-triphosphorylated RNA, suggesting an alternative transcription start site (TSS) for mprA . Moreover, Term-seq analysis revealed a termination signal between mprA and emrA ( 46 ), at ~900 nt downstream of the 5′ end detected by RACE ( Fig. 2 B ), where we identified a Rho-independent terminator with a canonical GC-rich stem-loop followed by a poly-U tail. Together, these results define a transcript encompassing a truncated ygaH ORF and full mprA ( ygaH′-mprA ), with a predicted length of 888 nt, which matches the size detected by northern blotting. This indicates that the TSS within ygaH is likely the primary TSS for mprA .

To further validate that represses the expression of ygaH’-mprA transcript at the posttranscriptional level, we used plasmid-based translational GFP reporters ( 47 , 48 ). Since constitutive overexpression of UhpU is toxic to E. coli ( 34 ), we transformed the plasmids into S . Typhimurium to carry out the assay. Compared with the control vector pJV300, expression of UhpU-L from a constitutive P LlacO-1 promoter significantly decreased fluorescence by 5.49-fold ( Fig. 2 E ). Mutation of the predicted base-pairing region in UhpU-L decreased the repression to ~1.95-fold, suggesting that additional pairing sites might be involved. To investigate this, we used AlphaFold3 to model the UhpU– mprA 5′ end complex in the presence of Hfq. The model revealed a second potential contact site within the mprA ORF around codons 3 to 5 ( Fig. 2 B and SI Appendix , Fig. S10 ). Notably, although the two target regions are separated by ~43 nt in the mprA sequence, their mapped contact sites on UhpU are adjacent, implying a cooperative spatial interaction. Consistent with this, mutating both predicted contact sites abolished repression (~1.14-fold). Conversely, introducing double compensatory mutations in mprA::sfgfp partially restored regulation (~2.06-fold). In contrast, single-site UhpU-L mutants were unable to regulate the double-compensatory reporter. Taken together, these findings support a two-site interaction model in which UhpU engages two adjacent regions to repress mprA effectively.

To further support a direct interaction, we performed in vitro electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs), which demonstrated that UhpU binds the 5′ UTR of mprA with an apparent dissociation constant ( K_ d ) of approximately 250 nM ( SI Appendix , Fig. S11 ), a value comparable to other characterized sRNA–mRNA interactions such as RaiZ- hupA ( 3 ). More importantly, deletion of uhpTU also increased the protein levels of chromosomally FLAG-tagged MprA by ~1.41 fold ( Fig. 2 F and SI Appendix , Fig. S8 C ), and complementation of either uhpTU together or uhpU-L individually expressed from low-copy plasmid pXG-1 under control of their respective native promoters reduced MprA::3 × FLAG level. In contrast, complementation of uhpT alone had no effect, suggesting that MprA can be repressed by the physiological expression level of UhpU.


## Conserved Biogenesis of UhpU by RNase E Processing during Hexose Phosphate Metabolism.

Our ASR analysis within the Escherichia genus showed that the intragenic FliA-binding site in uhpT is absent in E. albertii ( SI Appendix , Fig. S4 ). Despite this, the predicted mprA -binding region on UhpU is identical to that in E. coli K-12 across all analyzed E. albertii genomes ( SI Appendix , Table S2 ). Moreover, all strains carry the ygaZH locus, and 85.8% also harbor a conserved UhpU-binding site in the mprA 5′ UTR, matching the sequence found in E. coli K-12. A similar pattern was observed for the UhpU– lrhA interaction region, with the binding sequence in lrhA identical to that of E. coli K-12 in all 816 strains examined. These observations suggest that UhpU– mprA and UhpU– lrhA interactions may also exist in E. albertii , raising the possibility of an alternative biogenesis pathway for UhpU in this species.

We also observed two UhpU isoforms in E. coli during growth in LB medium. Differential RNA-seq data revealed that the 68 nt UhpU-S isoform is a processed sRNA with a monophosphorylated 5’ end ( 35 ), suggesting that it may be processed from the UhpU-L isoform. Additionally, the 5’ end of UhpU-S is localized to a conserved AU-rich region near the uhpT stop codon ( Fig. 3 A ), where the sequence AAAUU perfectly matches the minimal RNase E cleavage site consensus (RN↓WUU, where R = G/A, W = A/U, and N = any nucleotide) ( 11 ), This suggests that UhpU-S might be processed by RNase E from UhpU-L. We hypothesized that if UhpU-S can be processed from UhpU-L, it should also be generated directly from the uhpT mRNA, which contains the full UhpU-L sequence. Such an alternative, FliA-independent biogenesis pathway may also exist in E. albertii , which harbors a potential RNase E recognition motif (AGGUU) in the corresponding region ( Fig. 3 A ). This may explain the presence of UhpU– mprA and UhpU- lrhA regulatory sequences in E. albertii . Consistent with this idea, previous studies showed that UhpU-L levels decrease while UhpU-S levels increase in the presence of glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), a hexose phosphate that activates uhpT transcription through the UhpABC system ( 34 , 49 ), We confirmed this pattern and found that deletion of uhpAB , but not fliA , significantly reduced UhpU-S RNA levels ( Fig. 3 B ), demonstrating that UhpU-S is primarily produced during G6P metabolism via UhpABC-dependent transcription of uhpT . A similar expression pattern was also observed in E. albertii during G6P metabolism ( SI Appendix , Fig. S2 B ), further supporting the existence of a FliA-independent biogenesis pathway in this species. To directly test whether RNase E mediates this process, we analyzed UhpU-S in a thermo-sensitive RNase E mutant strain ( rne -3071) of E. coli ( 50 , 51 ). Upon inactivation of RNase E at the nonpermissive temperature (44 °C), UhpU-S biogenesis was markedly reduced in both the control ( rne Ctr ) and thermo-sensitive ( rne TS ) strains compared to 28 °C ( Fig. 3 B ). However, UhpU-S levels were decreased more strongly in the thermo-sensitive strain, indicating that UhpU-S biogenesis depends on RNase E cleavage in E. coli .

We noted that the RNase E cleavage site is conserved between Escherichia and Salmonella ( Fig. 3 A ), suggesting that the processing mechanism and associated biological function of UhpU may also be conserved. To further test this, we examined whether UhpU is generated by processing of uhpT mRNA in S . Typhimurium during G6P metabolism. Indeed, we observed an ~70 nt UhpU species—comparable in size to UhpU-S in E. coli —accumulating in S . Typhimurium ( Fig. 3 C ). This band was abolished upon deletion of uhpAB , indicating that UhpU biogenesis in Salmonella similarly depends on uhpT transcription activated by UhpABC in response to G6P. In contrast to E. coli , where UhpU is completely abolished in the absence of Hfq, UhpU accumulation in S . Typhimurium was only moderately affected by hfq deletion and unexpectedly increased upon deletion of fliA , for reasons that remain unclear. Additionally, UhpU production was reduced in a temperature-sensitive RNase E mutant, suggesting that RNase E is also required for UhpU biogenesis in S . Typhimurium. Together, these findings support a conserved mechanism in which UhpU is processed from uhpT mRNA in an RNase E-dependent manner during hexose phosphate metabolism across Enterobacteriaceae.


## Conserved Posttranscriptional Regulation of UhpU during Hexose Phosphate Metabolism.

The conserved expression of UhpU in both E. coli and S . Typhimurium suggests a potential ancestral regulatory role during hexose phosphate metabolism. To explore this, we compared the target spectrum of UhpU between the two species during G6P metabolism using pulse expression combined with RNA sequencing. In E. coli , 21 genes including 4 sRNAs were repressed by more than twofold upon UhpU-L overexpression ( SI Appendix , Fig. S12 A and Dataset S5 ). In contrast, Salmonella UhpU displayed a broader target spectrum, with 31 genes repressed by more than twofold ( SI Appendix , Fig. S12 B and Dataset S6 ). Interestingly, only one target, ptsH , was repressed by both E. coli and Salmonella UhpU. ptsH encodes the phosphocarrier protein HPr, a component of the PEP-dependent PTS, which is crucial for sugar transport and phosphorylation ( SI Appendix , Fig. S12 C ). Additionally, the manXYZ operon that encodes the Enzyme II permease components that serve as the transport unit of the mannose PTS was also repressed by Salmonella UhpU and ranked among the top regulated genes. Given that UhpU originates from the 3’ UTR of uhpT , which encodes the hexose phosphate transporter, these findings confirm that 3’ UTR derivate sRNA primarily regulates genes within the same metabolic pathways as its parental gene.


## UhpU Posttranscriptionally Regulates Distinct PTS Components in E. coli and S. Typhimurium.

IntaRNA predictions revealed that UhpU base-pairs with different regions of the ptsH mRNA in E. coli and S . Typhimurium. In E. coli , the predicted interaction site lies between +12 and +26 nucleotides downstream of the ptsH start codon ( Fig. 4 A ), whereas in S . Typhimurium, the site spans positions +41 to +47 ( SI Appendix , Fig. S13 A ). The corresponding UhpU binding sites are located in a relatively conserved region near the Rho-independent terminator ( SI Appendix , Fig. S14 A ), in contrast to the more variable mprA -binding region. IntaRNA predictions suggest that the UhpU– ptsH interaction is stably maintained across several Enterobacteriaceae genera, including Klebsiella and Citrobacter ( SI Appendix , Fig. S14 B and C ). Consistent with this, qRT-PCR confirmed that ptsH expression was repressed upon UhpU induction in both Klebsiella and Citrobacter , supporting conserved regulation ( SI Appendix , Fig. S14 D and E ). Intriguingly, similar regulation has also been reported for sRNA RsaG of Staphylococcus aureus , a Gram-positive bacterium, which is also processed from uhpT mRNA by exoribonucleases J1/J2 ( 52 ). This suggests that the regulatory mechanism may be conserved even outside the Enterobacteriaceae family. In addition, UhpU is predicted to bind the manX coding sequence in S . Typhimurium, at positions +23 to +38 relative to the start codon ( Fig. 4 B ). To validate these interactions, we performed plasmid-based translational GFP reporter assays. In E. coli , UhpU-L overexpression repressed ptsH::sfgfp fusion expression by 2.00-fold ( Fig. 4 C ). This repression was abolished by point mutations in either UhpU-L or the predicted target site and was partially restored (1.42-fold repression) by introducing compensatory mutations into both. In contrast, UhpU overexpression in S . Typhimurium unexpectedly increased ptsH::sfgfp reporter fluorescence ( SI Appendix , Fig. S13 B ), matching the control vector pXG-1 and diverging from the repression pattern observed in RNA-seq data following UhpU pulse expression. However, UhpU significantly repressed manX::sfgfp expression in S . Typhimurium by 3.91-fold ( Fig. 4 D ). As with ptsH in E. coli , this repression was abolished by mutations in the base-pairing regions of either UhpU or manX , and partially rescued (1.40-fold repression) by compensatory mutations. These findings indicate that UhpU differentially regulates components of the PTS in E. coli and S . Typhimurium. In E. coli , UhpU targets the ptsHI-crr operon, modulating the levels of Enzyme I and HPr, which are shared components of the PTS for multiple sugars. In contrast, in S . Typhimurium, UhpU represses the manXYZ operon, which encodes Enzyme II components specific for mannose uptake and phosphorylation. Additionally, regulation of manXYZ may indirectly influence ptsHI-crr expression.

To determine whether these regulatory effects occur under physiological conditions, we chromosomally tagged ptsH and manX with 3 × FLAG and monitored protein levels by western blotting in different genetic backgrounds. In E. coli , deletion of the uhpTU locus led to a ~2.12-fold increase in PtsH::3 × FLAG levels ( Fig. 4 E and SI Appendix , Fig. S15 ). Complementation with uhpU-L alone or the full uhpTU operon—but not with uhpT alone—restored PtsH levels to WT or lower, confirming that repression is mediated by the sRNA, not the transporter. Consistent with this, a minor but significant growth delay was observed in the strain complemented with uhpT alone compared with the strain complemented with both uhpU-L and uhpT when α-methyl-glucoside (α-MG) was added to G6P medium ( SI Appendix , Fig. S16 ). This difference disappeared in the absence of α-MG or during growth in LB medium, suggesting that loss of uhpU enhances PTS activity during G6P metabolism, thereby increasing α-MG uptake and accumulation of the toxic intermediate α-MG-6-phosphate, which in turn impairs growth ( 53 ). A similar regulatory pattern was observed in Salmonella : Deletion of uhpTU resulted in elevated expression of both PtsH::3 × FLAG (~1.57-fold) and ManX::3 × FLAG (~3.26-fold) ( Fig. 4 F and SI Appendix , Fig. S15 ), although regulation of PtsH appears to be indirect. Notably, complementation with uhpT alone significantly reduced ManX::3 × FLAG expression to WT levels, while coexpression of uhpU and uhpT nearly abolished ManX::3 × FLAG levels. This effect may result from UhpT-mediated G6P uptake increasing intracellular G6P, which in turn represses manX transcriptionally ( 54 ). Thus, UhpU and UhpT appear to function synergistically, coordinating both posttranscriptional and transcriptional responses from a single mRNA transcript. Together, these results support a model in which UhpU regulates distinct targets within the PTS in Escherichia and Salmonella , reflecting an evolutionarily conserved role in maintaining hexose phosphate homeostasis.


## Connection between Flagellar Synthesis, Efflux Pump Regulation, and Hexose Phosphate Metabolism.

The presence of UhpU interaction sequences with mprA and lrhA in E. albertii , which lacks a FliA-binding site in uhpT , suggests that these interactions may have arisen when UhpU was generated solely during hexose phosphate metabolism. Because 3′ UTR–derived sRNAs not only regulate pathways associated with their parental genes but also connect them to functionally related external pathways ( 55 , 56 ), we hypothesized that the mprA - and lrhA -associated pathways might likewise be linked to hexose phosphate metabolism. To test this, we compared the transcriptomic changes of WT and Δ uhpTU E. coli strains during G6P metabolism. The Δ uhpTU strain displayed marked changes in the flagellar cascade, with upregulation of fliA and several class III genes, coupled with repression of class II genes ( SI Appendix , Fig. S17 and Dataset S7 ). These effects were absent in LB medium and correlated with reduced motility of Δ uhpTU on G6P but not on LB plates ( SI Appendix , Fig. S18 ), indicating that UhpTU-dependent G6P metabolism specifically influences flagellar regulation. In addition, expression of multiple efflux pump genes, including mprA–emrAB , marRAB , and evgAS , was significantly altered. Many of these were also among the most affected genes in our MprA pulse-expression experiments ( SI Appendix , Fig. S17 C and Dataset S8 ), suggesting that the efflux pump network controlled by MprA is likewise connected to UhpTU-dependent G6P metabolism. Together, these data reveal a functional link between flagellar synthesis, efflux regulation, and hexose phosphate metabolism, supporting a model in which UhpU regulation of lrhA and mprA emerged in the context of nutrient-responsive G6P metabolism.


## Discussion

Exploring the evolution of bacterial sRNAs is crucial for understanding how these molecules have adapted their regulatory functions in response to changing environmental pressures. In this study, we explored the evolutionary trajectory of sRNA UhpU by examining its functions and the sequence traces left by key evolutionary events. The biological roles identified for UhpU support a stepwise model of origin and diversification ( Fig. 5 ): Initially, UhpU was coexpressed with its parental gene uhpT and acquired the ability to posttranscriptionally repress components of the PTS. This likely marked its emergence as a processed sRNA from the 3′ UTR of uhpT mRNA, ensuring that uptake and phosphorylation of environmental hexoses did not exceed cellular demand and thereby maintaining intracellular hexose phosphate homeostasis. Within the Escherichia (including the Shigella lineage) genus, horizontal acquisition of the ygaZH locus led to aberrant activation of the transcriptional repressor MprA. Because this regulation could impose a cost–benefit imbalance, variants of UhpU capable of repressing MprA may have been selectively retained, mitigating deleterious effects while preserving the advantage conferred by ygaZH . In parallel, UhpU also gained the ability to repress lrhA , integrating into a multistep coherent feed-forward motif that links its regulation to the flagellar cascade. Finally, after the divergence of the MRCA of E. coli , E. fergusonii , clade I, and Shigella from E. albertii , mutations within the uhpT coding region converted a FliA-like motif into a canonical FliA-binding site. This change broadened UhpU expression beyond metabolic induction of uhpT , establishing it as a dual-biogenesis sRNA with both Type I and Type II features.


> **Fig. 5.:** Model depicting the origin and adaptive evolution of UhpU. UhpU likely originated in ancestral Enterobacteriaceae as a processed sRNA derived from the 3′ UTR of uhpT , coordinating PTS activity with hexose phosphate uptake. In Escherichia , acquisition of the ygaZH locus enabled UhpU-mediated repression of mprA , while UhpU also gained the ability to repress lrhA , linking it to the flagellar cascade. Later, mutations in the uhpT coding region generated a canonical FliA-binding site, providing lineage-specific transcriptional activation in E. coli , E. fergusonii , clade I, and Shigella . This stepwise path expanded UhpU’s roles from metabolic regulation to broader control of motility and stress responses.

Previous attempts to trace the origin and evolution of bacterial sRNAs have largely relied on comparative genomics. One common strategy focuses on the acquisition or loss of narrowly distributed sRNAs, which often lack sufficient conservation across taxa to allow evolutionary reconstruction ( 27 – 29 ). Another approach focuses on sequence variation in broadly conserved sRNAs such as Spot42 and RyhB ( 57 – 59 ). However, these sRNAs typically have complex, pleiotropic functions and broad target ranges, making it difficult to resolve the selective pressures that shaped their evolution. In contrast, UhpU possesses several distinctive features that make it an ideal model for studying the evolutionary trajectory of sRNAs. First, UhpU originates from the conserved gene uhpT , the broad distribution and conservation of uhpT across various bacterial species make it sufficiently ancient to allow us to track its ancestral role in hexose phosphate metabolism. Moreover, UhpU exhibits a more recently evolved biogenesis mechanism through independent transcription within Escherichia , providing insight into the evolutionary dynamics after it became a functional sRNA. Thus, the combination of UhpU’s conserved and more recent biogenesis mechanisms, along with its associated regulatory outcomes, offers valuable insights into the evolutionary history and functional diversification of sRNAs.

Our analyses revealed that UhpU employs two distinct seed regions to regulate different targets. A relatively conserved region near the Rho-independent terminator pairs with ptsH , as confirmed in E. coli , K. pneumoniae , and C. freundii tested in this study. In S . Typhimurium, an additional four-nucleotide insertion upstream of this region enables pairing with manX ( SI Appendix , Fig. S14 A ), effectively replacing ptsH as the PTS target of UhpU. This conserved region also mediates repression of lrhA , linking UhpU to the flagellar cascade. By contrast, the mprA -binding site lies closer to the end of the uhpT ORF and shows substantial variation among genera, restricting this regulatory interaction to Escherichia . These findings support a model in which UhpU combines a “locked” conserved seed that preserves its ancestral role in maintaining hexose phosphate homeostasis with a variable region that expands regulatory scope. This architecture differs from other conserved sRNAs with multiple seed regions, such as Spot42 and GcvB, in which all seed motifs are conserved but target either broadly conserved or context-specific genes ( 58 , 60 ). UhpU therefore illustrates a distinct co-option model of sRNA evolution, in which conserved seeds preserve ancestral functions while variable regions enable regulatory diversification.

The conservation of both UhpU– lrhA and UhpU– mprA interaction sequences in E. albertii indicates that these interactions preceded FliA-promoter acquisition. We therefore propose that such preexisting interactions facilitated selection of a FliA-dependent promoter in the lineage comprising E. coli , E. fergusonii, clade I and Shigella , where broader or differently timed UhpU expression could have provided a fitness advantage. The selective benefits maintaining these interactions remain unclear but may reflect the need to coordinate multiple pathways, including flagellar cascades and efflux control, with carbon source utilization. Previous studies have shown that flagellar regulation is tightly coupled to carbon metabolism ( 61 – 63 ). Consistent with this, our RNA-seq analysis of the Δ uhpTU strain during growth on G6P revealed induction of several class III genes encoding filament and motor components, accompanied by repression of class II genes encoding the basal body–hook and export apparatus. This uncoordinated expression pattern would be expected to impair flagellar assembly, as filament and motor components cannot be properly exported or anchored without a functional basal body–hook structure ( 64 ). In addition, our data indicate that MprA regulates multiple efflux regulators and transporters, including evgAS and marRAB , many of which were also dysregulated in the Δ uhpTU strain under G6P metabolism. Because efflux pumps are essential for detoxification, their regulation may likewise need to be integrated with metabolic state ( 65 ). Given the complexity of evolutionary processes, it is unlikely that a single selective pressure drove these regulatory connections. Rather, the combined benefits of linking UhpU to flagellar cascades, efflux pump control, and other yet-unidentified pathways may have contributed to the retention of these interactions and favored the later emergence of the FliA-binding site within uhpT , thereby expanding UhpU regulation from hexose phosphate-specific conditions to a broader range of environments.
