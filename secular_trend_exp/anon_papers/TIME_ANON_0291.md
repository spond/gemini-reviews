## Abstract

β-hemoglobinopathies, such as sickle cell disease and β-thalassemia, are common genetic disorders remaining significant global health challenges due to their associated morbidity and mortality. Increasing fetal hemoglobin (HbF) levels has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for these disorders. In this study, we report Myc-associated zinc finger (MAZ) as an indirect repressor of γ-globin ( HBG ) expression in human erythroid cells. Depletion of MAZ in HUDEP-2 and patient-derived β-thalassemia cells leads to significant inductions of both HBG mRNA and protein levels, resulting in increased HbF percentages and HbF + erythroid cells. We demonstrate that MAZ occupies at the promoter of MYB, a well-known HBG repressor. MAZ depletion reduced MYB levels. Restoration of MYB re-silenced the HBG levels in MAZ depleted cells. Our findings uncover the MAZ-MYB axis in HBG regulation, highlighting MAZ as a potential target to enhance HbF levels in patients with hemoglobin disorders.

## Introduction

Hemoglobinopathies are a group of inherited blood disorders characterized by abnormalities in hemoglobin structure or production, significantly impacting oxygen transport in the body. Two prominent examples are β-thalassemia and sickle cell disease (SCD). β-thalassemia is a common genetic disorder characterized by reduced (β+) or absent (β0) synthesis of β-globin ( HBB ) chains, leading to a deficiency in hemoglobin 1 . The severity of the disease is primarily influenced by the excess of unbound α-globin ( HBA ) chains, which precipitate in red blood cell precursors, resulting in mechanical and oxidative damage, a condition known as ineffective erythropoiesis. SCD arises from a mutation in the HBB gene, resulting in the production of hemoglobin S, which causes red blood cells to deform into a sickle shape under low oxygen conditions 2 , 3 . This deformation leads to painful vaso-occlusive crises and hemolytic anemia. Current treatment strategies for β-thalassemia include regular blood transfusions, iron chelation therapy to manage iron overload, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and in some cases, gene therapy which offers the potential for a cure 4 , 5 . For SCD, currently available treatments are limited to transfusions and hydroxyurea, although stem cell transplantation might be a potentially curative therapy. Several new therapeutic options are available, including gene therapy and gene editing 3 , 6 . Both disorders exemplify the diverse manifestations of hemoglobin abnormalities and highlight the importance of understanding their genetic underpinnings for developing effective treatments.

To adapt to the changing oxygen demands of the developing fetus and the newborn after birth, an essential biological process called hemoglobin switching plays a key role during development 7 . Hemoglobin switching, the perinatal transition from fetal hemoglobin (HbF, α₂γ₂) to adult hemoglobin (HbA, α₂β₂), is a key developmental event.This process is crucial in the context of β-thalassemia and SCD, as raising HbF levels can alleviate symptoms associated with HBB deficiency 8 . Studies have shown that sustained expression of HbF can significantly ameliorate the clinical manifestations of β-thalassemia and SCD, providing a therapeutic strategy for treating affected individuals 9 . Genome-editing approaches aimed to correct the disease-causing mutation or reactivate HbF are progressing from early development to clinical approval 10 – 12 .

The mechanisms underlying hemoglobin switching involve a complex interplay of transcription factor regulation, epigenetic modifications, and chromatin remodeling. Key transcription factors such as MYB and BCL11A play pivotal roles in repressing γ-globin (HBG) genes expression in adults 13 – 16 , while factors like EHMT2, LYAR, WDR5, and PRMT1 contribute to the intricate regulatory network that governs globin gene expression 17 – 20 . MYB is known to promote the expression of HBB while inhibiting HBG , whereas BCL11A acts as a direct repressor of HBG transcription, making them primary targets for therapeutic intervention 21 – 23 .

In this study, we identified Myc-associated zinc finger (MAZ) as an indirect HbF repressor, which has been implicated in the modulation of globin gene expression 24 , 25 . Studies have reported that the MAZ gene is involved in various cellular processes, including transcriptional regulation and working as a co-factor in CTCF mediated chromosome structure 26 , 27 . Our findings demonstrate that MAZ represses HBG in adult erythroid cells, where reduced or absent MAZ expression leads to the activation of HBG expression. Specifically, MAZ binds to MYB promoter and activate its transcription, thus indirectly silence HBG expression. These insights into the role of MAZ in HBG regulation underscore its potential as a therapeutic target for β-thalassemia and SCD.


## Results


## Decreased MAZ expression increases HBG levels in HUDEP-2 cells

Induction of HBG levels can clinically benefit β-thalassemia and SCD patients 28 – 30 . MAZ is reported as a regulator of the erythroid differentiation program and binds to the promoters of human HBA genes 25 . We sought to determine whether MAZ is also involved in HBG regulation. To investigate the role of MAZ in HBG regulation, we employed shRNAs to knockdown MAZ in a human adult-type erythroid cell line HUDEP-2 31 . Two independent shRNAs successfully reduced MAZ mRNA and protein levels compared to the scrambled control shRNA (Fig. 1 A, E, Fig. S5). To examine the effects of MAZ knockdown on HBG levels, we performed multiple analysis to measure the HBG mRNA and protein levels. Despite we noticed that the baseline of HBG levels might be elevated, the percentages of fetal hemoglobin (HbF), and HbF + erythroid cells. We observed that HBG expression levels were significantly induced in MAZ KD cells compared with controls (Fig. 1 A-D), and the percentage of HbF in total hemoglobin was increased (Fig. 1 F), along with an increased proportion of HbF + cells (Fig. 1 G-H). Notably, the differentiation of HUDEP-2 upon MAZ knockdown was comparable to controls (Fig. S1 ). Taken together, these results suggest that MAZ is a HBG repressor.

## Genetic depletion of MAZ increases HBG expression

The knockdown of MAZ by shRNAs may cause undesired off-target effects. Therefore, we further verified the MAZ functions using CRISPR/Cas9 technology in HUDEP-2 cells. We designed a sgRNA targeting MAZ exon2 and generated 4 MAZ knockout (KO) HUDEP-2 cell lines from single cell clones. The genotype of each cell line was confirmed by Sanger sequencing, which showed that all the four lines were homozygous harboring frame-shift mutations (Fig.S2A). Consequently, MAZ mRNAs were severely deceased and protein expression were completely depleted in those KO lines (Fig. 2 A, F, Fig.S5). Consistent with the knockdown results, we observed significant inductions of both HBG mRNA and protein levels, as well as the proportion of HbF + cells compared to wild-type (WT) clones (Fig. 2 B-H). Furthermore, we observed repression of HBE and MYB mRNA levels. (Fig. 2 D, E). Together, these results suggest that MAZ represses HBG expression in the adult-type HUDEP-2 cells.

( F ) Western blot of the protein levels of MAZ and γ-globin in MAZ knockout HUDEP-2 clones. ( G ) Flow cytometry results of HbF + cells in MAZ knockout HUDEP-2 clones. Results are shown as mean ± SD ( n = 3). ( H ) Representative flow cytometry results of the proportion of HbF + cells in MAZ knockout HUDEP-2 clones.


## Induction of HBG levels by repressing MAZ in severe β-thalassemia erythrocytes

In order to evaluate whether decreasing MAZ levels could activate HBG expression in a clinically relevant model, we examined the effects of MAZ repression in the cells derived from a β0/β0 thalassemia patient with CD41-42 (HBB: c.124-127delTTCT)/CD17 (HBB: c.52 A > T) mutations. We collected peripheral blood from the β-thalassemia patient and isolated CD34 + hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) under the consent of the Ethics Committee. We cultured and differentiated the patient-derived HSPCs towards erythroblasts using an in vitro culture system 32 . Attempts to generate complete MAZ-KO in primary thalassemia patient-derived erythroblasts were hampered by lower editing efficiency; thus, functional studies in primary cells relied on the highly efficient shRNA-mediated knockdown approach. We transduced shRNAs with lentivirus vector into the HSPCs then enriched the transduced cells by sorting the EGFP + cells to generate MAZ knock down (KD) cell lines. We observed significant reductions of MAZ at both mRNA and protein levels in the patient derived erythroblasts, suggesting successfully knockdown of MAZ in these cells (Fig. 3 A, F).


> **Fig. 3:** Induction of HBG levels by repressing MAZ in severe β-thalassemia primary erythroblasts. ( A-D ) The mRNA expression levels of MAZ , HBG , HBB and HBG/(HBG + HBB) detected by RT-qPCR. Results are normalized to GAPDH and shown as mean ± SD ( n = 3). ( E ) Flow cytometry analysis of HbF + populations in MAZ knockdown β-thalassemia erythroblasts on day 10 of differentiation. ( F ) Immunoblots of MAZ, GAPDH and HBG proteins. ( G ) Flow cytometry analysis of differentiation markers CD235a and CD71 in MAZ knockdown β-thalassemia erythroblasts during in vitro differentiation.

Prior studies have discovered that the HBG basal levels are higher in severe β-thalassemia patients. In line with these reports, we found that the percentages of HBG levels were ~ 33% in the erythroblasts derived from β-thalassemia patient. With the high basal HBG levels, we still observed a slight induction of HBG mRNA levels upon MAZ knockdown (Fig. 3 B-D). Concordantly, repression of MAZ led to an increase in HBG protein levels (Fig. 3 F, Fig.S5). Moreover, the proportion of HbF + cells increased from 38.9% to 51.1% compared to the controls, suggesting a pan-cellular HBG induction (Fig. 3 E). Meanwhile, expression levels of the differentiation markers CD235a and CD71 were comparable to scrambled shRNA controls during erythroblast maturation (Fig. 3 G). The cell morphologies were also comparable between MAZ knockdown and the control erythroblasts (Fig.S3), suggesting that MAZ knockdown had little effects on the differentiation of β-thalassemia erythroblasts.

The above results demonstrate that reduction of MAZ can induce the expression of HBG in the erythroblasts derived from severe β-thalassemia patients, providing a potential therapeutic target for β-thalassemia and SCD.


## The genome-wide binding profiles of MAZ

To investigate the mechanism by which MAZ suppresses HBG , we conducted a detailed analysis of the published ChIP-seq data of MAZ. We obtained MAZ ChIP-seq data in K562 and primary erythroid cells (PECs) from a previous report 25 and ENCODE database 33 . Genome-wide, MAZ binds to 32,781 and 10,088 peaks in K562 and PECs, respectively. There were 7,313 common MAZ binding sites shared by K562 and PECs (Fig. 4 A). Annotation of the peaks in K562 and PECs revealed that MAZ predominantly bound to the gene promoter regions (within 0–3 kb of the transcription start site, TSS). This preference was particularly pronounced in PECs, where approximately 75% of MAZ peaks were associated with promoters, within a narrow region (0–1 kb) around TSS (Fig. 4 B).

The MAZ occupied promoters were associated to ~ 10,000 and ~ 7,000 genes in K562 and PECs, respectively. 5,698 genes were occupied by MAZ in both cell lines. Notably, among them was the well-known HBG repressor MYB (Fig.S4A). Furthermore, we conducted GO and KEGG functional annotations of the common target genes of MAZ in both cell lines. The findings revealed that MAZ occupied genes primarily encoding transcription factors, playing roles in various signaling pathways, including AMPK, mTOR, and P53 (Fig.S4B, C) 34 – 36 .

Previous studies have shown that MAZ can activate the HBA genes and potentially mediates chromatin 3D structure along with CTCF to regulate the expression of Hox family genes 24 – 27 . We further compared the binding profiles of CTCF 37 , 38 and active histone marks (H3K4me3 39 and H3K27ac 40 at MAZ binding peaks. The common binding regions of MAZ in both K562 and PECs had stronger signals, and co-localized with CTCF and active chromatin marks (Fig. 4 C). These results suggest that MAZ may cooperate with CTCF to mediate distal interactions of enhancers to activate gene transcription (Fig. 4 D, E).


## MAZ indirectly represses HBG through activating MYB transcription

To investigate how MAZ represses HBG , we performed RNA-seq to measure global transcriptions upon MAZ knockdown in HUDEP-2 cells (Fig. 5 A). We identified 778 up-regulated genes and 792 and down-regulated genes (fold change > 2 and false discover rate (FDR) ≤ 0.01). As expected, MAZ was among the down-regulated genes (Fig. 5 A, C). Consistent with the RT-qPCR results, HBG1 and HBG2 genes were up-regulated (Fig. 5 A, D). Two well-known HBG repressors MYB and BCL11A were down-regulated (Fig. 5 A, D). We verified the expression and protein levels of MYB and BCL11A , and the results were in line with RNA-seq data (Fig. 6 A, B, Fig.S6). GO enrichment was analyzed with GSEA for biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions (Fig. 5 B). Conspicuously, we observed up-regulated genes enriched in cell junction assembly, circulatory system process. Given that MAZ occupied at MYB promoter region, these results imply that MYB could be the downstream effector of MAZ on HBG regulation.It has been reported that MYB regulates HBG through activating BCL11A 41 – 44 . Both MYB and BCL11A were identified as HBG suppressors by GWAS 13 , 45 – 47 . Therefore, we hypothesized that MYB is the primary downstream effector of MAZ on HBG regulation. To test this hypothesis, we performed rescue experiments by over-expressing MYB in the MAZ knockdown HUDEP-2 cells. In both two independent MAZ knockdown cell lines, the over-expression restored MYB levels comparable to the controls. Moreover, the HBG levels were re-silenced upon over-expression MYB (Fig. 6 C, D, Fig.S6).

Together, our results reveal that MAZ activates MYB to repress HBG levels in adult erythroblasts (Fig. 6 E).


## Discussion

In summary, we have elucidated the role of MAZ as an indirect repressor of HBG expression in human erythroid cells. Our findings demonstrate that the knockdown of MAZ in HUDEP-2 cells significantly induces both mRNA and protein levels of HBG , leading to an increase in fetal hemoglobin (HbF) percentages and HbF + erythroid cells. Additionally, these effects were further confirmed through CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout of MAZ, reinforcing the notion that MAZ can repress HBG expression. Importantly, our results also extend to patient-derived β-thalassemia erythrocytes, where MAZ depletion continues to enhance HBG expression, suggesting a critical therapeutic potential for targeting MAZ in treating β-hemoglobinopathies. While the current study establishes the MAZ-MYB-HBG axis, the temporal dynamics of this regulation during erythroid differentiation remain an important open question. Future work employing detailed time-course analyses in MAZ-knockout models will be crucial to elucidate the precise sequence of transcriptional events.

Several prior investigations have established a clear association between higher baseline HBG levels and β-thalassemia 8 , consistent with our findings where basal HBG levels in β-thalassemia patient-derived erythroblasts also showed an elevation. However, while past studies primarily focused on the interplay between other factors such as BCL11A and MYB, we found the direct binding of MAZ to MYB promoter region, consistent with previous research of which MAZ is essential to bypass MYB promoter repression and to induce MYB expression 24 . Our research highlights the specific involvement of MAZ in this regulatory network (Fig. 6 E). ATF4 binds to MYB intergenic enhancer and activates MYB expression 48 . How does ATF4 and MAZ mediate the interplay of MYB enhancer and promoter is still an open question. Furthermore, it is well-established that MYB enhancers are regulated by GATA1 complexes 49 . It is therefore conceivable that MAZ may cooperate with or contribute to GATA1-mediated MYB activation, a possibility that warrants future investigation.The down-regulation of MYB, alongside the observed effects on BCL11A, suggests a more complex regulatory mechanism that has not been fully described in earlier research. This differential regulation presents an opportunity to explore MAZ’s role further as a mediator of HBG expression alongside established regulators. Besides, we also noticed that the decrease of HBB expression under KO or KD of MAZ , supporting the competition model between enhancer and HBB / HBG promoters 50 .

Previous research has shown that MAZ functions as a co-factor in CTCF-mediated chromatin organization at the Hoxd cluster.The erythroid-specific chromatin structures of human β-globin locus is maintained by two CTCF binding sites HS5 and 3’HS1 51 , 52 . CTCF also occupies the murine α-globin genes and their regulatory elements 53 , 54 . These observations suggest that MAZ may also cooperate with CTCF to shape enhancer–promoter interactions and higher-order chromatin structure at the HBB and HBA loci. However, further studies are required to validate this hypothesis.

The induction of HBG via MAZ repression presents a promising strategy for the clinical management of β-thalassemia and SCD. Given that increasing HbF levels can alleviate the symptoms associated with these disorders, targeting MAZ represents another potential therapeutic avenue. Our results suggest that manipulating the MAZ-MYB axis may provide a powerful tool for enhancing fetal hemoglobin production in future treatments. Ultimately, our study underscores the importance of understanding the molecular underpinnings of hemoglobin regulation and opens pathways for innovative interventions in β- hemoglobinopathies.


## Methods


## Cell culture

The HUDEP-2 cell line was a kind gift of Dr. Yukio Nakamura (Cell Engineering Department, BioResource Center, RIKEN, Japan). HUDEP-2 cells were cultured in StemSpan SFEM (STEMCELL) medium containing 50 ng/ml SCF (R&D Systems), 3 IU/ml EPO, 1 µg/ml Dexamethasone,1 µg/ml Doxycycline, 2 mM GlutaMAX (Gibco). HSPCs were isolated from a β0/β0 thalassemia patient with CD41-42 (-TTCT)/CD17 (A > T) mutations and cultured in StemSpan SFEM II (STEMCELL) medium containing 100 ng/ml SCF, 100 ng/ml TPO,100 ng/ml Flt3L, 100 ng/ml IL-6, 0.75 µM SR1 and 35 nM UM171. HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM High Glucose (Gibco) containing 10% FBS, 2 mM GlutaMAX (Gibco).


## Generation of Maz KD cell lines

Primers containing sequences of short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting MAZ or scrambled shRNAs were annealed and cloned into GV248 lentivirus vectors. Each GV248 plasmid then co-transfected with psPAX2(Addgene #12260) and pMD2.G(Addgene #12259) into HEK293T cells. Then the viruses were collected from supernatant of HEK293T after 48 h. HUDEP-2 cells and HSPCs were transduced with virus. EGFP + cells were sorted by FACS for subsequent expansion. The sequences of shRNAs oligos are listed in Table S1 .


## Generation of Maz KO cell lines by CRISPR/cas9

The sgRNA targeting the 2nd exon of MAZ were synthesized from Genscript and incubated with 12 pmol of TrueCut Cas9 Protein v2 (Invitrogen) at room temperature for 15–30 min to form the RNPs. Then the RNPs were electroporated into 2 × 10 5 cells by Neon™ NxT Electroporation System (ThermoFisher) using program 1100 V, 10 ms, 3 pulse. The sequence of sgRNA is listed in Table S1 .


## Erythroblasts in vitro differentiation

Basic EDM medium was made with IMDM (Gibco) supplemented with 330 µg/ml Holo-human Transferrin (Sigma), 10 µg/ml Recombinant human insulin (Sigma), 2 IU/ml Heparin (STEMCELL), 5% AB human serum (Sera Care), 3 IU/ml EPO and 2 mM GlutaMAX. β-thalassemia HSPCs were cultured in EDM medium supplemented with 1 µM Hydrocortisone (Sigma), 100 ng/ml SCF, 5 ng/ml IL-3 (Peprotech) for 7 days. Cells were cultured in EDM medium containing 100 ng/ml SCF from Day 7 to Day 11. Finally, HSPCs were differentiated in basic EDM medium for another 7 days.


## RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol regent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized using a PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio). RT-qPCR was performed using TB GreenTM Premix Ex TaqTM (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara Bio) on a Viia7 Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The qPCR primers are listed in supplemental Table S1 .


## RNA-seq analysis

RNA-seq libraries were prepared from total RNA from cells using TruSeq stranded mRNA sample preparation kit (Illumina) and sequenced on NovaSeq6000 (Illumina). Approximately 16–20 million of Q30 > 90% trimmed and filtered high-quality 150-bp paired-end reads per sample generated by Fastp 55 were mapped to the human genome (hg38) using the STAR 56 with default parameters. Gene counts were overlapped with exons of RefFlat (UCSC, https://genome.ucsc.edu/ ) by featureCounts 57 . DESeq2 58 was used for differential gene expression analysis. Expression visualized tracks were generated with bigwig files performed by bamCoverage 59 then displayed by igvtools (IGV 2.8.6).


## Published chip-seq analysis

Peak files of MAZ ChIP-seq on primary erythroid cells, H3K4me3 ChIP-seq and H3K27ac ChIP-seq on HUDEP-2 cells were obtained from GEO datasets under GSM4135996 25 , GSM6199499 39 and GSM5267246 40 . Files of MAZ and CTCF ChIP-seq on K562 cells were acquired from ENCODE database on ENCSR163IUV 33 and ENCFF221XPQ 37 , 38 . MAZ ChIP peaks were annotated with ChIPseeker Tools 60 . Target motifs were derived from peak regions by using MEME-ChIP tool 61 . Binding peak intersect analysis was calculated by BedTools 62 , and the matrix between ChIP peaks and repeats was calculated using ComputeMatrix 59 . Heatmaps then were generated by plotHeatmap 59 using calculated matrix generated by ComputeMatrix. Peak visualized tracks were generated with bigwig files then displayed by igvtools (IGV 2.8.6).


## Giemsa staining

The cells were spread on glass slides and air-dried for 2–3 min. The cells were then incubated with 60 µl Wright-Giemsa Solution A (BASO, Zhuhai, China) for 50s. Wright-Giemsa Solution B (3–4 times of Solution A) was added onto Solution A and mix thoroughly to stain for 5–10 min. Slides were rinsed with tap water. The images were captured using a microscope (Leica Microsystems).


## Flow cytometry analysis

The cells were washed with PBS and incubated with APC-conjugated anti-CD235a antibody (BioLegend) and PE-conjugated anti-CD71 antibody (BD Biosciences) for 30 min at room temperature. Cells were washed twice and analyzed by the InvitrogenTM AttuneTM NxT flow cytometry system.


## Western blotting

The cells were pelleted and washed twice with cold PBS. Proteins were extracted in RIPA buffer with proteinase inhibitor cocktail (Roche).


## Quantification and statistical analysis

The software Prism Version 9 was used for data analysis. Comparisons were assessed by Student’s t test. For all statistical tests, a p value < 0.05 was considered significant. Significance was expressed using asterisks, where ns P > 0.05, * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, and *** P < 0.001.
