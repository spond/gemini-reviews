## Abstract

Understanding the developmental trajectory of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) remains a critical barrier for timely intervention in children. Here, we investigated the deficit brain maturation trajectory during childhood development in 35 ASD level 1 and 35 neurotypical children through an electroencephalography (EEG) approach. An empirical study of the potential EEG biomarkers was demonstrated in a comprehensive view of group difference and age-related group comparison using alpha power, peak alpha frequency and transfer entropy during resting. We found a significant disruption of directional brain network communication between regions in children with ASD compared to neurotypical children. Our results also suggested that the children with ASD had altered occipital alpha power and peak alpha frequency development. The present study revealed promising findings that underpinned the developmental disruption of autism spectrum disorder, which may provide a prevailing insight into the disease pathology mechanisms, paving the way for future intervention advancement.

Keywords: Autism spectrum disorder (ASD), Electroencephalography (EEG), Developmental neuroscience, Electrophysiology biomarkers, Transfer entropy (TE)

Subject terms: Biomarkers, Neurology, Neuroscience


## Introduction

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition represented by repetitive activities, deficits in language and sensory perception, impaired social interaction and daily task functioning 1 – 3 . Recently, an estimated 1% of the global prevalence has been diagnosed as having autism spectrum disorder, including approximately 2% of the worldwide child population at the age of 8 years 4 – 6 . The ASD etiology is caused by atypical brain maturation during childhood, which promotes dynamic degrees of hallmarks reinforced with a high risk of comorbid psychiatric ramifications 7 , 8 . While the ASD mechanisms underlying remain partially understood, no specific biomarkers have been identified to date. The enormous obstacle of ASD intervention is the intricacy of understanding the phenomena of this complex disorder, which extensively diversifies across age, gender and behavior manifestation. Electroencephalography (EEG) is a non-invasive technique for neuroelectrophysiological study that provides insight into spatial and temporal brain electrical activity through scalp measurement. Tremendous evidence represented the promising potential of EEG, which played a vital role in distinguishing the diverse characteristics of ASD neurophysiology 9 – 12 . Previous studies also reported EEG as a crucial digital phenotyping indicator to differentiate between individuals with and without ASD using machine learning classification with electrophysiological biomarkers (e.g. spectral power) 13 , 14 .

For decades, the EEG has been applied as a powerful neuroscience tool for studying brain maturation trajectory in early childhood development, especially multiple biomarkers that fluctuate relatively with age. The most widely used benchmarks are those of power spectra and functional connectivity extracted from the EEG signal of an individual who underwent a specific experiment (e.g. resting, cognitive tasks). Prior works have revealed atypical alterations in the spectral power of ASD patients, which markedly diversify in both reducing and enhancing aspects for all frequency bands across plentiful reports 10 , 15 . The alpha power and peak frequency have been emphasized for their strong relationship with cognitive performance, intelligence, attention and working memory, which is remarkably appropriate for developmental neuroscience study 16 – 21 . Numerous pieces of evidence support that the alpha peak frequency increases throughout brain development at early stages 22 – 25 . The alpha power trajectory is also identified as a substantial index that poses an intensified correlation with age upon different developmental stages 26 – 28 . Thus, these electrophysiological features could serve as a promising biomarker to robustly investigate the maturation trajectory in children with ASD that exhibit a vast majority of neurodevelopmental disruption in their childhood 29 .

An alternative approach for capturing the developmental progression is through functional connectivity, which indicates a high association with creative ability, IQ level, language expression and visual perception 30 – 33 . Previous studies demonstrated an immense correlation with age in early maturation, suggesting that the EEG coherence trajectory consistently increases relative to age since childhood 34 – 36 . In ASD research, EEG coherence was utilized for comparing the functional connectivity difference between ASD and neurotypical individuals in terms of abnormal brain connections, which showed how electrophysiological phenomena change in accordance with the disease 37 , 38 . While functional connectivity provides more meaningful information about neural activity synchronization, it lacks directional specificity and causality. On the contrary, effective connectivity delivers the directed flow of information and magnitudes, portraying insight into how one brain region influences another 39 . Transfer entropy (TE) is an effective connectivity estimation that is well-suited for the nonlinear dynamic nature of EEG data through its superior advantage over traditional coherence by offering directional, nonlinear information flow for better reflecting the underlying mechanisms of neural development 40 – 42 . There were several pieces of evidence that performed a visual temporal cognitive task using transfer entropy as their biomarker in ASD research 43 – 45 . Prior works in children have also applied directional information transfer for characterizing attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) from neurotypical development 46 , 47 . These findings highlight the utility of transfer entropy in capturing complex brain network dynamics across neurodevelopmental conditions. However, no studies have specifically applied transfer entropy as an effective connectivity index for evaluating resting-state EEG in individuals with autism spectrum disorder, representing a groundwork basis for its usage in studying the developmental trajectory of resting-state brain networks in children with ASD.

In summary, the paramount goal of this study is to investigate age-related changes in brain electrophysiological activity in children with and without ASD, which may elucidate the underlying mechanisms of the deficit maturation behind this abnormal development through vital EEG biomarkers such as spectral power, peak alpha frequency and transfer entropy. A quantitative analysis of eye-opened resting-state EEG was conducted to test how atypical neurodevelopment is reflected during childhood maturation in children with ASD. This study involved predefined hypotheses regarding group differences (ASD vs. NT) and age-related changes within each group (ages 5–8 vs. 9–12). Since there is no evidence of applying transfer entropy with the resting-state EEG in ASD research, our work aims to bridge this critical gap by comparing information flow patterns across group differences and two age-related subgroups of both ASD and neurotypical children.


## Results

Resting-state electroencephalography (EEG) data were collected from 70 child participants with an age range from 5 to 12 years during the eye-opened condition. Thirty-five ASD level 1 and thirty-five neurotypical children were recruited through Mahidol University, Thailand, and the Institute of Brain Fitness, Thailand. All children with ASD were previously diagnosed by clinical psychiatrists according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria. Table 1 summarizes the demographics of ASD and neurotypical participants for each analysis group. The ASD group ( n = 35) have a mean age of 8.46 ± 1.99 years (21 males (60%) and 14 females (40%)), while the neurotypical group ( n = 35) have a mean age of 8.48 ± 2.06 years (21 males (60%) and 14 females (40%)). There was no significant difference between the ages of the two groups (Independent t-test, p = 0.963). The paramount goal of this study is the age-related developmental characteristic of EEG in children with and without autism spectrum disorder. Therefore, the data were divided into 2 subgroups for both the ASD and NT groups, as denoted in Table 1 . All EEG data were obtained for at least 5 min (30 min maximum) using the 10–20 international electrode placement system for all recordings in this work (Fig. 1 ).

## Age-related maturation of occipital alpha power reveals developmental disruption in children with autism spectrum disorder

The significant EEG electrodes presented a crucial impact of log-transformed power divergence in occipital, parietal and temporal lobe areas between ASD and NT groups, especially in the alpha band (8–12 Hz). A Spearman’s correlation (ρ) between log-transformed power and age was computed to investigate neurophysiological alteration in children using the average log-transformed power within brain regions of interest. The alpha power at the occipital region (O1 and O2) of NT showed a significant correlation with age (ρ = − 0.469220535, p = 0.026772192), while ASD implied no meaningful correlation between alpha power and age (ρ = − 0.318207283, p = 0.374838082), as shown in Fig. 4 a. ## Age-related development of transfer entropy across brain regions May reflect disrupted brain networking communication upon childhood maturation in children with autism spectrum disorder

Transfer entropy (TE) is a method for effective connectivity used to measure directional informative flow between two brain regions. It implies inter-regional communication of the brain networking efficiency, which is critical in neurodevelopmental aspects. Hence, the transfer entropy was computed from the EEG time series data of ASD and neurotypical children to compare their directional connectivity across electrode pairs, expressed as a heat map visualization (Fig. 6 a, b). These heatmaps display pairwise TE values between EEG channels, which are averaged across participants within each group. Warmer colors indicate stronger directional information flow between brain regions. The effect sizes of the transfer entropy were calculated using Cliff’s delta for each EEG pairwise, indicating small to no differences between ASD and NT groups for all comparison pairs (Fig. 6 c). Among all pairwise comparisons, 10 out of 342 electrode pairs denoted a significant difference in transfer entropy between ASD and NT groups (Fig. 6 d). Eight pairs showed that the transfer entropy of ASD groups was greater than the NT groups, while two pairs presented that the transfer entropy of NT groups was higher than the ASD groups. These results demonstrated insignificant diversification of transfer entropy between the two groups, which may not be influenced by the atypical nature of electrophysiological characteristics in autism spectrum disorder children across ages 5 to 12 years.

## Discussion

A substantial hindrance to autism spectrum disorder (ASD) diagnosis and intervention in children is posed by their dynamic characteristics that are extensively diverse in brain electrophysiological activity, cognitive function and behavioral manifestations across age and gender. This study aims to provide considerable empirical evidence of electroencephalography (EEG) phenomena associated with altered brain maturation in children with autism spectrum disorder, which may underpin its perplexing hallmarks of this abnormal development. The EEG band power comparison indicated a substantial alteration in both absolute and relative alpha power across electrodes between ASD and neurotypical children, particularly a significantly lower alpha power at the posterior region of the brain. These phenomena have been observed by numerous previous studies, which reported decreased alpha power in ASD children during the resting compared to neurotypical children 9 , 48 – 51 . The age-related decline of absolute alpha power has been linked to childhood brain maturation in neurotypical development, as alpha power is reduced relative to older age 22 , 52 . This characteristic of ASD leads to a hypothesis of developmental trajectory disruption caused by atypical alpha power, which may affect their impaired maturation in daily task functioning, sensory perception, visual processing and working memory 53 – 55 . Therefore, a comprehensive analysis was carried out to investigate the crucial EEG biomarkers that may be notably influenced by the disease etiology of autism spectrum disorder, encompassing speculated alterations in transfer entropy, alpha band power and peak alpha frequency during childhood development.

Using transfer entropy (TE), we found that children with autism spectrum disorder revealed a significantly impaired developmental trajectory in less functional network maturation, while neurotypical children exhibited age-related progression in brain network connectivity (see Fig. 7 d, h). It may imply altered maturation of brain inter-regional communication in ASD children compared to neurotypical children. To our knowledge, no prior studies have investigated age-related alterations in brain network connectivity using transfer entropy of resting EEG in children with ASD. However, several published works applied transfer entropy for their effective connectivity estimation in ASD research. Previous results showed that transfer entropy substantially declined during the visual temporal cognitive task experiment, referring to weakened temporal visual processing in terms of information flow across the brain upon stimulus 43 – 45 . The recent article also reported the sleep-state transfer entropy of children with and without ASD, indicating a small amount of significant pairs among the tremendous number of pairwise comparisons 56 . Since prior studies have primarily focused on task-based EEG, our findings represent the first evidence of remarkable neurodevelopmental disruption in directional information flow of resting EEG across age groups in children with ASD.

On the other hand, our study investigated the transfer entropy between the two groups during resting, which served as the baseline state for their neural network connectivity. Hence, there was no noteworthy difference between the transfer entropy of ASD and NT groups in our study, suggesting that the reduction phenomena of transfer entropy in ASD children may be observed upon performing a cognitive task rather than in a resting or sleep state. Intriguingly, no studies have specifically applied transfer entropy as an effective connectivity index for evaluating resting-state EEG in individuals with autism spectrum disorder. Most ASD research has focused on coherence or other effective connectivity as developmental indicators for EEG functional connectivity measurement. Prior works suggested that functional connectivity is atypically reduced in the ASD group when compared to the NT group during resting or performing tasks 38 , 57 , 58 . Coben and colleagues reported neural underconnectivity pattern in frontal and posterior regions of the ASD individuals 37 . Another research conducted by Seymour et al. performed Granger causality between V1 and V4 visual areas that manifested a significant decrease in the ASD group during the sensory task experiment 59 . Although these methods were particularly suited for detecting abnormal EEG patterns in ASD that may underlie known differences in visual integration and working memory, especially in tasks requiring global processing or visual input. The transfer entropy provides an important characterization of brain network dynamics on how information propagates between regions or where disruptions appear in directional aspects, which cannot be captured by conventional functional connectivity metrics.

Our results also demonstrated the immature occipital alpha power in the 9–12 years group of children with ASD when compared to the 5–8 years group, while the 9–12 years neurotypical children showed alpha power reduction. The group comparison revealed that the occipital alpha power of children with ASD was critically lower than that of neurotypical children. Multiple pieces of evidence indicated the same occurrence of notable alpha power difference in the posterior region of children with ASD 51 , 60 – 62 . Our findings also suggested that the developmental trajectory of occipital alpha power upon increasing age denoted an insignificant correlation in the ASD group. A previous study reported the alteration of EEG spectral power between high-risk and low-risk ASD infants, mentioning the developmental trajectory changes in all frequency bands of the frontal lobe 28 . However, there was no age-related analysis of alpha power between age groups 5–8 and 9–12 years children with autism spectrum disorder that has been addressed for childhood development. Thus, the disrupted occipital alpha power trajectory found in this work may reflect atypical maturation of the occipital lobe as a considerable rationale, which may imply an underlying difference in visual processing and perception or working memory between ASD and neurotypical children.

Nevertheless, a strong correlation between occipital peak alpha frequency and age was observed in both ASD and NT children in this work. A recent study demonstrated a similar trajectory pattern of peak alpha frequency maturation regarding our study 63 . However, several previous studies have reported peak alpha frequency trajectory with significantly weak correlation to age in ASD children, while the neurotypical children displayed a strong correlation similar to our study 64 – 66 . On the other hand, there were also numerous pieces of evidence that showed no substantial group difference between ASD and NT children as well 48 , 67 . The peak alpha frequency is an imperative electrophysiology biomarker that indicates brain maturation during childhood. Abnormal development can cause non-verbal cognition impairment and lower neural network integrity 24 , 68 . It is also associated with visual temporal processing in early childhood development, portraying that higher frequencies are relative to faster visual temporal resolution 23 . In this study, children with autism spectrum disorder exhibited atypical development in peak alpha frequency at the occipital region between age groups 5–8 and 9–12 years, which was essentially lower when compared to the high progression trends of the neurotypical group. Our findings supported the hypothesis of impaired development in the occipital lobe of children with ASD, which showed less peak alpha frequency maturation in 9–12 years subgroup.

Ultimately, this study presents new insight into brain network maturation in children with autism spectrum disorder through the investigation of directional information flow in resting EEG between two age-related groups. Our work introduces the first evidence to evaluate resting-state transfer entropy for analyzing neurodevelopmental aspects in children with ASD. The results suggested that transfer entropy could play a vital role as a potential electrophysiological biomarker in autism spectrum disorder research, which may provide a superior understanding for impaired brain connectivity maturation during childhood, comparable to those of alpha band power and peak alpha frequency. The disrupted transfer entropy trajectory between age groups 5–8 and 9–12 years aligns with the trajectory patterns observed in the alpha band power and the peak alpha frequency at the occipital region, reflecting a broader disruption across power spectra and connectivity domains. The converged outcomes essentially support our hypothesis on the application of these critical biomarkers as meaningful indices for studying atypical functional brain development in children with ASD. However, while our findings are promising, they may be influenced by the limited sample size and diversity and should be interpreted with caution. Although the study employed a confirmatory design, its cross-sectional nature limits direct inferences about individual developmental trajectories, which would require formal longitudinal tracking. Thus, the observed differences across age groups should be interpreted as age-related patterns rather than definitive evidence of delayed maturation. Traditional power analysis was not applied in the subgroup analysis, as it is not well-defined for high-dimensional, non-parametric EEG measures such as Transfer Entropy. Instead, we relied on permutation testing and Network-Based Statistics, which are robust to small sample sizes and commonly used in exploratory EEG connectivity research 43 – 45 , 69 , 70 .

In this study, age-related group comparisons were used to maintain sufficient statistical power and reduce within-group variance, given our sample size ( n = 35 per group). Prior to conducting age-related comparisons, Spearman’s correlation was performed to identify biomarkers significantly associated with age within each group. Only those biomarkers showing significant correlations with age were included in subsequent age-related analyses. While modeling age as a continuous variable (e.g., via Spearman’s correlation) may more accurately reflect developmental effects, such approaches require larger sample sizes to ensure robustness and reliability, particularly in high-dimensional EEG analyses. In our case, the number of participants per individual age year was only five, which limited the statistical reliability of modeling age as a continuous variable. An additional limitation of this study is the absence of standardized cognitive or IQ assessments, which prevents statistical control for potential group differences in cognitive ability. While all children with ASD were classified as DSM-5 Level 1, we acknowledge that cognitive variability may influence EEG measures to some extent. However, given the resting-state nature of the experiment, we expect this influence to be minimal. Future studies with expanded cohorts may benefit from continuous age modeling to explore finer developmental trajectories. Further validation of our findings will require larger and more diverse samples, ideally allowing for narrower age subgrouping with sufficient statistical power in each group, being a stepping stone toward a better scientific understanding of one of the most enigmatic conditions remains in the history of developmental neuroscience.


## Methods


## Ethical statement

The EEG experiment procedure was approved by the Mahidol University Institutional Review Board (MU-IRB), in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki (protocol number MU-CIRB 2017/106.0606). Children with ASD were required to have a DSM-5 diagnosis of ASD Level 1, be aged between 5 and 12 years, have no history of psychoactive drug use, no neurological disorders (e.g., epilepsy), and no known genetic conditions (e.g., fragile X syndrome). All children’s parents or caregivers were required to read the research protocol and sign the informed consent before beginning the experiment. In this study, resting-state EEG was recorded during the eyes-open condition for at least five minutes, with the entire session completed within approximately thirty minutes. All EEG experiments were performed at the Brain-Computer Interface Lab, Mahidol University, Thailand, or the Institute of Brain Fitness, Thailand.


## EEG acquisition and preprocessing

EEG data were acquired using the BrainMaster Discovery 24E system with a 24-bit, 256 Hz sampling rate and a 50 Hz notch filter. The signals were recorded from 19 electrode channels according to the 10–20 electrode placement system and two reference (A1 and A2) channels (see Fig. 1 ). All electrodes were placed on the child’s scalp via an elastic cap with conductive gel applied. The electrode impedance was maintained below 50 kΩ throughout the entire experiment. The 0.5–40 Hz Butterworth bandpass filter was applied to raw EEG data. A notch filter at 50 Hz was also used to eliminate the power line noise. Subsequently, the filtered EEG data that contained artifacts were manually rejected by a well-trained EEG professional. Continuous EEG data retained an average of 7.2 ± 2.3 min (mean ± SD) after artifact rejection. Only EEG data of good quality with artifact-free data were used for further analysis.


## Spectral power

All preprocessed EEG data were transformed into spectral power by Welch’s method across all channels. The frequency spectrum was divided into 4 standard EEG bands: delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz) and beta (12–25 Hz). EEG features extracted from preprocessed EEG were used to compute absolute and relative power of each frequency band and peak alpha frequency across 19 EEG electrodes. The absolute power was normalized using log 10 transformation to reduce skewness, stabilize variance, and improve statistical validity and interpretability. For developmental trajectory analysis, alpha power and peak alpha frequency at occipital, parietal and temporal lobes were computed by averaging the log-transformed alpha power and peak alpha frequency across all electrodes within each region of interest for each individual separately prior to statistical analysis. The occipital region represents the mean values of O1 and O2 electrodes. The parietal region displays the mean values of P3, P4 and Pz electrodes. The temporal region presents the mean values of T3, T4, T5 and T6 electrodes. Other brain regions were excluded from the developmental trajectory analysis as no significant differences were observed in the frontal, central, or prefrontal lobes in either log-transformed or relative power comparison.

## Statistics and reproducibility

EEG data were obtained from age-matched 35 children with ASD and 35 neurotypical children who completed the resting state EEG recording during the eye-open condition for at least 5 min. Age group differences were conducted using an independent t-test. In this study, no gender distribution analysis was assessed, as the gender proportions were identical between the two groups. All statistical significance thresholds reported in this study refer to corrected p-values with significance defined as p < 0.05 after applying the appropriate multiple comparisons correction (e.g., Bonferroni).


## EEG power analysis

Group comparisons of log-transformed and relative power were performed using the Mann–Whitney U-test (two-tailed), and statistical significance was evaluated using the Bonferroni method for multiple comparisons correction (correction factor = 76, based on 19 electrodes × 4 EEG bands). Age-related subgroup comparisons were conducted only for the alpha band in the occipital, parietal, and temporal regions, based on prior Spearman’s correlation analyses between age and either log-transformed alpha power or peak alpha frequency. If no significant correlation was found in a given region, further subgroup comparisons for that region were excluded. Statistical comparisons for alpha power and peak alpha frequency between age subgroups were also assessed using the Mann–Whitney U-test (two-tailed) with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. All computations and topographical visualizations were performed using Python-based implementations, and the violin plots were illustrated using GraphPad Prism software.


## Effect size Estimation

Cliff’s delta ( δ ) was computed to estimate the effect size of differences between the ASD and NT groups across EEG power and transfer entropy (TE) features. Cliff’s delta is a non-parametric measure of effect size that quantifies the degree of overlap between two distributions, which identifies large effect size differences even in the absence of statistical significance 74 . The Cliff’s delta was calculated for each EEG frequency band across all 19 electrodes for both log-transformed power and relative power values, indicating the direction and strength of group differences visualized as topographical maps. For TE comparisons, the Cliff’s delta was computed for each electrode pair in the full 19 × 19 TE matrix. The values reflect the degree of group differences in directional information flow. These were visualized as heat maps to present spatial and directional patterns of altered connectivity. All computations and topographical visualizations were performed using Python-based implementations, and heatmap visualizations were generated using GraphPad Prism software.


## Correlation analysis

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was computed separately for the ASD and NT groups to evaluate the relationship between age and EEG spectral power features (alpha power and peak frequency). It is a non-parametric method that is suitable for handling non-normal distributions like developmental EEG data, while capturing monotonic relationships. The Spearman’s analysis was conducted using the mean values across electrodes within each region of interest for each individual, as mentioned earlier (see EEG power analysis). The Bonferroni method was applied for multiple comparison correction in both alpha power and peak alpha frequency. All analyses were performed using Python-based implementations, and visualizations were displayed using GraphPad Prism software.


## Transfer entropy analysis

Statistical comparisons of transfer entropy (TE) values were conducted using a non-parametric permutation testing approach combined with Network-Based Statistics (NBS) to assess group differences in directed information flow 75 . This statistical method is well-suited for controlling the family-wise error rate (FWER) of high-dimensional EEG connectivity data, while identifying clusters of significantly different connections in the network. Group comparisons (e.g., ASD vs. NT, or age-related subgroups) were performed using a permutation t-test ( n = 5000) for each electrode pair. Significant clusters were identified, as a predefined primary threshold ( p < 0.05, uncorrected) to define suprathreshold connections, which was compared to the null distribution obtained through permutations. Corrected p-values were computed using the family-wise error correction for each cluster. Only components surviving the corrected threshold ( p < 0.05) were considered statistically significant. All calculations were performed using Python-based implementations, and visualizations were illustrated using GraphPad Prism software.
