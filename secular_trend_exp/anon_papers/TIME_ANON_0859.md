## Abstract

Rhipicephalus sanguineus is a tick species characterized by high intraspecific variability, even among populations occupying the same habitats. This study investigated whether two distinct phenotypes of R. sanguineus , collected from four geographically separated regions, are associated with specific nucleotide polymorphisms in the mitochondrial cox 1 and 16S rRNA gene regions—markers commonly employed in phylogenetic analyses. Morphometric analysis based on established criteria clearly distinguished two morphotypes—Morphotype 1 and Morphotype 2—with statistically significant differences. This morphological differentiation was supported by phylogenetic and genetic divergence analyses, primarily based on mitochondrial cox 1 sequences, which revealed six polymorphic sites within the analyzed fragment. Among the identified haplotypes, two—Haplotype 2a and Haplotype 2b—were consistently associated with Morphotype 2. This genetic segregation was confirmed in extended analyses including reference isolates (morphologically and/or genetically defined either as R. sanguineus or R. hibericus ). These analyses also demonstrated a close phylogenetic relationship between Morphotype 1 and the R. sanguineus neotype, as well as between R. hibericus and both morphotypes. Although the 16S rRNA marker exhibited lower resolution, it still effectively distinguished the two morphotypes and corroborated the haplotype assignments established by the cox 1 phylogeny. The observed genetic divergence (p-distance of 1% in cox 1 gene) and the phylogenetic relationship between R. sanguineus and R. hibericus confirmed that R. sanguineus is a polymorphic species, with well-defined haplo-morphotypes, and that R. hibericus is not genetically distinguishable from R. sanguineus .

## Introduction

Rhipicephalus sanguineus (Latreille 1806) (Nava et al. [YEAR] ), the type species of the genus Rhipicephalus , has great importance to animal and human health. Its hematophagous behavior can cause clinical illness, either directly (anemia, skin lesions and paralysis, among others), especially in heavy infestations; or indirectly during the feeding process, when it may transmit pathogens with high zoonotic/pathogenic potential, as is the case of Rickettsia conorii complex , Coxiella burnetii (Derrick 1939) , Anaplasma platys (French and Harvey [YEAR]) , Ehrlichia canis (Donatien & Lestoquard 1935) and Hepatozoon canis (James 1905) (Dantas-Torres [YEAR] ; Barradas et al. [YEAR] ; Snellgrove et al. [YEAR] ; Moerbeck et al. [YEAR] ; Anastácio et al. [YEAR] ).

The existence of other closely related species of R. sanguineus that exhibit significant levels of interspecific and intraspecific morphological variability, has resulted in multiple, worldwide, misclassifications, as detailed in successive revisions (Dantas-Torres et al. [YEAR] , [YEAR] ; Hornok et al. [YEAR] ; Nava et al. [YEAR] ). In the past 20 years, molecular taxonomic studies involving collections of different origins have revealed genetic differences indicative of more than one species under the designation R. sanguineus (Szabó et al. [YEAR] ; Burlini et al. [YEAR] ; Moraes-Filho et al. [YEAR] ; Nava et al. [YEAR] ; Dantas-Torres et al. [YEAR] ). However, the resolution of cryptic species within the R. sanguineus complex, has only been possible since the redescription of R. sanguineus and the establishment of a neotype, based on morphological, genetic and biological data (Nava et al. [YEAR] ). This led to i) redescription of other neotypes, such as R. linnaei (Audouin, 1826) (Šlapeta et al. [YEAR] ), ii) reestablishment of species, as R. secundus Feldman-Muhsam, 1952 (Mumcuoglu et al. [YEAR] ) and R. rutilus Koch, 1844 (Šlapeta et al. [YEAR] ), iii) description of the new species R. afranicus Bakkes et al. [YEAR] (Bakkes et al. [YEAR] ).

More recently, Rhipicephalus hibericus sp. nov. Millán et al. [YEAR] , was proposed based on biological, morphological and molecular analyses (Millán et al. [YEAR] ). In thisstudy, two well supported clades were evidenced in the phylogenetic analyses targeting a cox 1 fragment. One clade, named R. hibericus , clusters three R. sanguineus haplotypes from Portugal and six nucleotide sequence types (ntST) of R. hibericus from Spain. The other clade named R. sanguineus s.s. clusters two sequences (Hungary and the USA), which group with one ntST from Spain. However, no reference and/or inclusion of cox 1 sequences from the neotype of R. sanguineus were made, despite16S rRNA sequences of the neotype being used in phylogenetic analyses shown as supplementary information.

Meanwhile, two recent comprehensive analyses (Dantas-Torres et al. [YEAR] ; Kelava et al. [YEAR] ) including sequences from various geographical origins identified as R. sanguineus , provided evidences of its distribution in the South Europe and some temperate areas of North and South America and North Africa. Both analyses highlighted gaps that need to be addressed, particularly regarding R. hibericus , which clustered with the R. sanguineus neotype and other recognized specimens of this species.

Furthermore, various studies involving phylogenetic analyses of the R. sanguineus complex had already identified two distinct and well supported clades, which received diverse designations: R. sanguineus temperate IIa (latter R. sanguineus sensu Nava et al. [YEAR] ) and R. sanguineus temperate IIb (Hungary) (Hornok et al. [YEAR] ); clade a (including sequences from the neotype, France, Portugal and Italy) and clade b (complete mitogenomes from Hungary and the USA specimens) (Šlapeta et al. [YEAR] ); R. sanguineus (clade K) and Rhipicephalus sp. (Clade F) (Kelava et al. [YEAR] ). Although morphological traits are available for the accurate description of the R. sanguineus neotype, which is represented by the first clade mentioned above, no morphological data on specimens from the second clade are available for comparison.

In the Iberian Peninsula, earlier morphological studies on R. sanguineus specimens revealed morphological variability, suggestive of the coexistence of R. sanguineus and R. turanicus Pomerantsev, 1936 (Papadoupolos et al. [YEAR] ; Sanchez-Acedo et al. [YEAR] ; Coimbra-Dores et al. [YEAR] ). These studies were based on genital aperture (Feldman-Musham 1956 ; Morel and Vassiliades 1962 ) and on spiracular plates of males and females (Estrada-Peña and Sánchez [YEAR] ; Walker et al. [YEAR] ), as distinctive traits. However, despite the persistence of morphological variability, especially regarding the aforementioned traits (Coimbra-Dores et al. [YEAR] ), later studies, based on three mtDNA markers (12S, 16S and cox 1), found no molecular evidence supporting the recognition of two species (Almeida et al. [YEAR] ; Coimbra-Dores et al. [YEAR] ).

Given that R. sanguineus may carry public health relevant pathogens, it is particularly important to establish a clear and well-supported taxonomy of this species. Thus, having access to R. sanguineus populations with specimens clearly evidencing the two distinct above-mentioned morphologies, the present study aimed to assess whether and how the two phenotypes are genetically supported. This association was inferred using molecular markers commonly employed in phylogenetic studies, specifically mitochondrial cox 1 and 16S rRNA gene fragments. Further evaluations were extended to specimens with established morphological descriptions and/or prior molecular identification – including sequences of the R. sanguineus neotype and R. hibericus —to better clarify their phylogenetic relationships and taxonomic placement.


## Material and methods


## Collection of ticks

A total of 52 ticks (males = 30; females = 22) were collected from vegetation by flagging and dragging in four different areas: Tapada da Ajuda (TA), Samora Correia (CL), Chão das Maias (CM), and Chão da Barba Pouca (BP), during May [YEAR] (Portugal) (Online Resource Table 1 ). Specimen collection was carried out with the supervision of a veterinarian, respecting international rules of animal handling, and permission from local authorities. They were preserved in 70% ethanol, becoming part of a tick collection housed at the University of Lisbon “Instituto Superior de Agronomia Tick Collection DataBase”. Six dog ticks previously evaluated (Coimbra-Dores et al. [YEAR] ; [REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al. [YEAR] ) were randomly selected for morphological and phylogenetic analyses.


## Morphological analysis

Specimens’ identification were based on Rhipicephalus literature (Walker et al. [YEAR] ; Coimbra-Dores et al. [YEAR] ; Estrada-Peña et al. [YEAR] ; Nava et al. [YEAR] ; Bakkes et al. [YEAR] ; Šlapeta et al. [YEAR] , [YEAR] ). Morphological evaluations were performed according to previous studies (Coimbra-Dores et al. [YEAR] ), highlighting spiracular plates (composed of an oval to globular body and a tail) in males and females, and the genital aperture (sclerites and aperture) in females. Though, female genitalia and spiracular plates from males and females were manually extracted with scalpels and needles and mounted in Líquido Tendeiro (Pagès and Sarto-I-Monteys [YEAR] ) on permanent slides. In females, the maximum height and width of the spiracular globular body, the width of the tail base, the height of the spiracular tail, the maximum height and width of one sclerite and the distance between them, were measured. In males, measurements were taken in the spiracular plate (total length, body width, width of the ending tail, and width of the adjacent festoon). The measurement values are displayed as mean (± SD) and the units are expressed in micra (µm).

After testing the normality assumptions using Shapiro–Wilk tests, a series of independent t–tests/Wilcoxon tests were performed to assess whether significant differences in the morphological traits existed between the two Morphotypes, recurring to R V4.5.0 (R core Team) within RStudio V [YEAR].04.02 + 496 (Posit Team [YEAR] ).


## Molecular analysis


## DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was extracted from individual ticks kept in 70% ethanol. Firstly, they were subjected to two washes in sterile ultrapure water to remove traces of ethanol and other waste. For total DNA extraction, the ticks were subjected to three cycles of freeze/thawing (−20 ºC/65 ⁰C), followed by maceration with a glass “piston” in 200 µL of extraction buffer (0.5 mg/mL proteinase K in 100 mM Tris HCl pH 8.9), and incubation at 65 ⁰C for 60 min. After proteinase K inactivation (5 min. at 95 ⁰C), the debris were pelleted (5 min. at 12 000 g) and the supernatant (extracted, non-purified, DNA sample) was recovered and kept at 4 ⁰C/−20 ⁰C (short/long term conservation).

The extracted DNA was directly used in PCR (after a 1:40 dilution in ultrapure water) or subjected to purification with the GRS Pure DNA kit (Grisp, Portugal), according to the manufacturer instructions.


## PCR amplification

A 600 nucleotide (nt) fragment of the mtDNA cox 1 was amplified using the following primer sets LCO_1490_F/HCO_2198_R (5’-GGT CAA CAA ATC ATA AAG ATA TTG G-3’/5’-TAA ACT TCA GGG TGA CCA AAA AAT CA-3’) (Folmer et al. [YEAR] ). DNA samples (1 µL/reaction) were subjected to PCR amplification, in 20 µL reaction mixtures with 10 µL of 2X Xpert Fast Hotstart master mix with dye (Grisp, Portugal), 1 µL of each primer (forward and reverse, at 10 µM), and 8 µL of ultrapure water. PCR conditions: initial step of 3 min. at 95 ⁰C, followed by 35 cycles of 15 s at 95 ⁰C, 15 s at the annealing temperature (45 ºC), 15 s at 72 ⁰C, and final step of 3 min at 72 ⁰C.

The 16S rRNA gene was also targeted for PCR amplification with the following primer sets: S0749/S0750 (Black and Piesman [YEAR] ). PCR amplification was carried out in a reaction mixture and cycling conditions identical to those referred above, except the annealing temperature (48 ºC).

The PCR products were sequenced using the same primers used for PCR, in the company StabVida, Portugal.


## Sequence data

The chromatograms of the sequences generated in this study were visualized in MEGA v11.0.13 (Tamura et al. [YEAR] ) and each sequence was manually edited when necessary.

The new sequences were individually compared to GenBank data by the nucleotide BLASTN program ( https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov ) to find out the corresponding best hits sorted by Identity and Maximum Score (Online Resource Table 2 ). They are recorded in GenBank, under the following accession numbers PV083774 to PV083806 ( cox 1 sequences) and PV397482 to PV397488 (16S rRNA sequences).


## Data analysis

A total of three datasets were built and evaluated. The “first dataset” (Online Resource Table 2 ) consisted of the cox 1 DNA sequences from the neotype and from Portuguese specimens morphologically identified: in this study and six isolates selected from previous studies (Coimbra-Dores et al. [YEAR] ; [REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al. [YEAR] ); R. rossicus was selected as an outgroup . A second dataset “ cox 1 global” was created by extending the previous dataset to include additional cox 1 sequences retrieved from GenBank (Table 1 ). These include sequences from R. sanguineus and R. hibericus (Black and Roehrdanz [YEAR] ; Almeida et al. [YEAR] ; Šlapeta et al. [YEAR] ; Millán et al. [YEAR] ; Kelava et al. [YEAR] ). Outgroup sequences are the same as those in the “first dataset”. The third dataset “16S” is comprised of 16S rRNA sequences, including 10 sequences with known morphotypes (from this and previous studies), the neotype’s 16S sequence, and sequences from previous studies (Black and Roehrdanz [YEAR] ; Nava et al. [YEAR] ; Hornok et al. [YEAR] ; Coimbra-Dores et al. [YEAR] ; Šlapeta et al. [YEAR] ; Millán et al. [YEAR] ). Alignments were performed with MAFFT v7.520 (Katoh and Standley [YEAR] ) with automatic parameter selection.


### Table 1: Mitochondrial sequences ( cox 1 and 16S rRNA) retrieved from GenBank

Evolutionary divergence between sequences was estimated in Mega 12 (Kumar et al. [YEAR] ). For the analyses involving cox 1, codon positions included were 1 st + 2nd + 3rd + Noncoding, and all ambiguous positions were removed for each sequence pair (pairwise deletion option). For the analysis involving the ribosomal 16S rRNA, all ambiguous positions were removed for each sequence pair (pairwise deletion option).

The meaning of the polymorphisms in the polypeptide cox 1 was evaluated, based on the “Codon usage table and inferred genetic code for Ixodes hexagonus and Rhipicephalus sanguineus ” (Black and Roehrdanz [YEAR] ).


## Phylogenetic analysis

For maximum reproducibility, all phylogenetic analyses were automated within a pipeline built on Snakemake v7.25.3 (Mölder et al. [YEAR] ) and run inside a docker container. All scripts, Snakefile, Dockerfile and documentation can be found in Zenodo repository ([REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al. [YEAR] ). The best-fit substitution model for each dataset was determined with ModelTest-NG v0.1.7 (Darriba et al. [YEAR] ), based on the corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc). Phylogenetic reconstruction by Maximum Likelihood was conducted in RAxML-NG v1.1.0 (Kozlov et al. [YEAR] ), implementing the selected model and assessing branch support with 1,000 bootstrap replicates under the FBP (Frequency Bootstrap Proportion) metric.

For Bayesian inference, aligned sequences were converted to Nexus format using a custom Python script built on Biopython v1.81 (Cock et al. [YEAR] ). Resulting files were modified to specify outgroup taxa and to ensure format compatibility. MrBayes v3.2.6 (Ronquist et al. [YEAR] ) analyses were run with two simultaneous, independent Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations of four chains each, over 1,000,000 generations, sampling every 100 generations. A burn-in of 25% of the sampled trees was applied before summarizing the posterior distribution. Phylogenetic trees were visualized and annotated using Toytree v2.0.5 (Eaton [YEAR] ), using the Maximum Likelihood tree as base, and placing support values from both approaches on each branch.

Morphotype 1 is characterized in females by a large genital aperture with conical and translucent sclerites (52.24 ± 16.01 µm height and 28.64 ± 8.41 µm width) spaced apart (66.70 ± 10.58 µm) (Fig. 1 a; Fig. 2 ). The comma-shaped female spiracle consists of an oval body, with its width (388.23 ± 86.74 µm) greater than its height (327.82 ± 74.87 µm), and a dorsal tail with a short basis (118.91 ± 24.40 µm) and an upward extension (101.33 ± 20.70 µm) (Fig. 1 b; Fig. 2 ). The comma-shaped male spiracular plate measure 637.08 ± 104.35 µm length, gradually narrowing towards the tail end, with the ending width (54.11 ± 10.52 µm), inferior to half of the adjacent festoon width, which reveal an average of 150.67 ± 26.33 µm width (Fig. 3 a; Fig. 4 ) .

Morphotype 2 presents in females a narrower genital aperture with cylindrical or conical sclerites (60.16 ± 9.93 µm height and 30.94 ± 5.18 width), opaque due to strong sclerotization and positioned next to each other (22.20 ± 6.12 µm) (Fig. 1 c; Fig. 2 ) . The spiracle is comma-shaped in females, with shorter width (332.68 ± 131.04 µm) than height (383.54 ± 79.59 µm), a large base of tail insertion (162.71 ± 27.67 µm) and a large upward extension (101.55 ± 33.83 µm) (Fig. 1 d; Fig. 2 ) . In males, the comma-shaped spiracular plate (530.63 ± 123.73 µm) in length evidenced the tail ending width (87.42 ± 23.80 µm) almost equal to the adjacent festoon width (127.00 ± 23.78 µm) (Fig. 3 b; Fig. 4 ) .

Significant differences between females of Morphotypes 1 and 2 were observed in sclerites height ( p –value = 3.394 × 10 –2 ), distance between sclerites ( p –value = 1.550 × 10 –8 ) and tail width ( p –value = 8.198 × 10 –3 ). In males, significant differences were noticed in spiracle length ( p –value = 2.893 × 10 –2 ), the end of spiracle tail ( p –value = 2.712 × 10 –4 ), adjacent festoon width ( p –value = 2.795 × 10 –2 ) and the index of adjacent festoon width and spiracle tail ending ( p –value = 1.124 × 10 –6 ).


## Molecular analyses


## Sequences generated in the present study

From the 52 specimens collected from vegetation, 33 were successfully sequenced for cox 1, 18 from Morphotype 1 and 15 from Morphotype 2. Out of these 33, six were sequenced for the 16S rRNA locus. The blastn best hits for sequences are reported in Online Resource Table 2 . With one exception (Algeria), the best hits were sequences from the Iberian Peninsula. Only five out of 28 in cox 1 sequences and six out of 10 in 16S rRNA sequences.

Regarding the cox 1 gene, all sequences ( n = 33) presented 98% to 100% identity (ID) with 111 sequences recorded as R. sanguineus (last accession: 12th February [YEAR]) from Europe, North Africa, and North and South America. The next matches, with only 95–96% ID, were nine sequences from different geographical regions (Spain, Hungary, Croatia, Italy, Morocco, the USA). Eight of these sequences were recorded as R. sanguineus , and three of them were complete mitochondrial genomes: NC_002074.1 = AF081829 (the first mitogenome to be sequenced from an unnamed isolate of R. sanguineus in the USA), OQ184024.1 (isolate P8/22–208:JS6373, a temperate haplogroup from Hungary) and ON756090 (isolate SAMN28408766 from the USA). Another sequence ( OR965523.1 ) from Spain, was recorded as ntST-5 of Rhipicephalus sp. 1 RRP-2023a. This set was then followed by sequences with only ≤ 92% ID with our sequences, starting with sequences identified as R. rutilus .


## Phylogenetic analyses and sequence divergence

The best ML/Bayesian phylogenetic tree constructed from the “first dataset”, using a 568 nt fragment (Fig. 5 ), revealed two distinct clades within the ingroup sequences. One clade has relatively good support [Bootstrap (BS)/Posterior Probability (PP) = 74/0.97] and clusters a group of sequences from Morphotype 2 (hereinafter referred to as M2a). The other clade, with very low support values is further divided in Morphotype 1 (M1) and Morphotype 2 (M2) sequences. Within these with only two secondary clades are supported: one within the M1 clade, containing the neotype sequences (BS/PP = 67/1.00), and the other containing all but one of the remainder sequences of Morphotype 2 (hereinafter referred to as M2b) (BS/PP = 56/0.99).

Sequence divergence was also evaluated using p-distances, which were calculated from the same multiple sequence alignment (MSA). The R. sanguineus sequences were initially categorized by morphotype (M1 and M2) and then grouped into four main categories/haplotypes belonging to M1 (M1a, M1b) and M2 (M2a and M2b) (see Table 2 ). Intra-haplotype p-distances were 0.0% (0.000) (shaded in dark grey on Table 2 ), while inter-haplotypes p-distances ranged from 0.5% to 0.9% (0.005 to 0.009) (shaded in light grey on Table 2 ). Several morpho–haplotypes have closely related sequences, with p-distances ranging from 0.1% to 0.4% (0.001–0.004) (shaded in medium grey on Table 2 ). These include CL12 related to M2–Haplotype 2b, and several sequences related to M1, such as BP30 and BP31 (named M1b due to a p-distance of 0.000 between them), A37 and CM21, as well as the neotype sequences (0.002 p-distance from M1b). CM21 also has the particularity of having a low p-distance (0.004) with sequences from one haplotype of the other morphotype: M2b. Also placed in a particular situation is SF3003, an isolate with Morphology 2 more closely related to M2b (p-distance of 0.002). Remarkably, however, it exhibits low p-distances of just 0.004 with sequences from M2a, and 0.005 with the M1 members.

Conversely, the p-distances between the sequences of R. sanguineus and R. rossicus (outgroup sequences) were higher than 10% (0.111–0.117).


## Identification of molecular polymorphisms

The segregation into morphotypes and haplotypes, as evidenced in either the phylogenetic tree or the p-distances table, is due to nucleotide differences at the six polymorphic positions shown in the 568-position MSA: 111, 183, 189, 348, 369 and 516 (Table 3 ). Sequences of M1 (M1a) have similar nucleotides, with the exception of CM21 (at positions 348 and 369) and BP30 and BP31 (at position 348), named M1b. Regarding Morphotype 2, there are two groups of sequences that differ in three positions (111, 183, 516); one group (M2a) differs from Morphotype 1 in five positions, and the other group (M2b) differs in four positions (Table 3 ).

The two sequences of R. sanguineus neotype (F1 and F2 generations) are next to Morphotype 1, except at position 348, like M1b (Table 3 ).


## The polypeptide cox1

It was found that all polymorphic positions correspond to the third nucleotide of the codon, for all codons involved in nucleotide substitutions. The amino acids specified by these synonymous codons (indicated between parenthesis) are as follows: histidine (CA C /CA T) at position 111; isoleucine (AT C /AT T and AT C /AT T) at positions 183 and 369, respectively; leucine (TT A /TT G and CT C /CT T) at positions 189 and 518, respectively; and glycine (GG C /GG T) at position 348.

In this phylogeny, the ingroup is divided into two main clades: one containing the neotype of R. sanguineus and all isolates with defined morphology, clustered with all other Iberian Peninsula sequences, except ntST-5 and CRETAV-1GC13/1(BS/PP = 85/1.00), and another, (BS/PP = 89/1.00) encompassing eight sequences from different geographical areas that were previously considered to have low identity (95–96%) with our sequences (BLAST analysis of sequences from this study).

The first clade ( R. sanguineus clade) is split in two other clades: one comprising sequences all M2a sequences from this study and sequences from two Portuguese isolates (SC3005 and S344) with similar morphology (BS/PP = 71/0.99), and another comprising all sequences from this work that are not M2a, the neotype sequences, and other sequences from Spain and Portugal. Similar to the previous tree, this clade further subdivides, revealing two clades with the sequence SF3003 (M2) placed between them. One of these clades is supported (BS/PP = 54/0.99) and comprises six sequences from Morphotype 2 isolates (CL12 and five from M2b) as well as two Spanish isolates of unknown morphology (ntST-1 and ntST-3). The other clade is not supported and comprises secondary branches, one of which is worth mentioning because it clusters all sequences of Morphotype 1 isolates, except CM21 (BS/PP = 45/-). This branch also encompasses three sequences of unknown morphology isolates (haplotypes I, II and III), and a clade with relatively high support (BS/PP = 67/1.00) containing the neotype sequences.

The estimated p-distances (Online Resources Table 5 ), based on the same MSA used in the extended phylogenetic analysis, follow the pattern observed in the phylogenetic tree and are consistent with the values found in the previous genetic divergence analysis, i.e. p-distances of 0–1% (0.000–0.012) between members of the R. sanguineus clade. The groups segregating the members of the two morphotypes, and now also including members with'unknown morphology', were clearly distributed into the groups related to those found in the previous analysis, with intra-group and inter-group p-distances of 0% and 1%, respectively.

Each group encompasses one Haplotype (p-distance 0.000) (shaded in dark grey in Online Resources Table 5 ) and'closely related genomes'(p-distance 0.001–0.004) (shaded in medium grey in Online Resources, Table 3 ); there are four'closely related genomes'for the M2a, two for the M2b and eight (the two neotype sequences included) for the M1 members. As with the phylogenetic tree and the previous p-distance analysis, Morphotype 1 is the most heterogeneous, with two members (haplotypes I, II) showing the greatest p-distances to Morphotype 2 members (1.1% and 1.2% from M2a) (shaded in light grey in Online Resources Table 5 ; like all situations evidencing p-distances of 1–2%). The neotype is undoubtedly closely related to Morphotype 1, with a p-distance of 0.2% from M1b and of 0.4% from the others (Online Resources Table 5 ).

Concerning the placement of the strains selected for the extended analysis, while both morphotypes have ntST that are genetically related to them (ntST-1 is identical to M2b), the four haplotypes are genetically related to Morphotype 1, with one of them (haplotype III) being identical to M1b. Of particular interest is the fact that two isolates from Spain (ntST-7 and CRETAV_1GC09_1) are genetically identical to isolate CM21 (Online Resources Table 5 ).

Regarding the members of the sister clade of Rhipicephalus sanguineus , the p-distances between them were 0–2%, and relative to the R. sanguineus clade they were 4–6%. The most divergent isolates were ntST-5 and Al-Hosema-A (p-distance of 2.1% between them).

The outgroup shows p-distances higher than 10.6% from both clades ( R. sanguineus and its sister clade) and 0% (0.004) intraspecies.

This global MSA evidenced the same pattern of molecular polymorphisms indicated for the previous dataset.


## Molecular evaluations extended to the 16S rRNA

A total of eight (8) readable sequences were obtained for the 16S rRNA marker. Querying these sequences against the NCBI’s database using Blastn to these sequences showed about 186 sequences identified as R. sanguineus with identities ranging from 96 to 100%; the next sequences, with an identity of 95%, were recorded as R. turanicus .

The ML/Bayesian phylogenetic reconstruction based on a MSA with 18 sequence and 237 positions (Online Resource Fig. 1 ) evidenced polytomy and the R. sanguineus sequences in successive supported branches. The first branch (BS/PP = 100/1.00) clusters all the sequences under analysis. Then, a second branch (BS/PP = 89/0.79) has another supported branch (BS/PP = 72/0.53) and two sequences in polytomy, which belongs to the"sister clade"in the cox 1 phylogeny.

From the supported branch mentioned above, diverge another well supported branch (BS/PP = 62/0.98) and two sequences from the “sister clade” in polytomy. Then, from this last branch diverge another supported branch grouping the eleven R. sanguineus sequences, including the neotype. The unnamed mitogenome sequence (NC_00274.1) segregates separately.

The R. sanguineus branch comprises four M1 sequences and the neotype sequence in a polytomous situation, separated from another supported clade (BS/PP = 59/0.98) with the M2a sequences in polytomy and M2b in a supported clade (BS/PP = 69/0.99).

The outgroup sequence is distinct from the others, and two additional sequences associated with the cox 1 “sister group” appear in polytomy alongside the R. sanguineus clade.

In the p–distance matrix based on the previous MSA, distances of 0–1% (0.000–0.009) were found between the sequences recognized as R. sanguineus and 1–3% (0.009–0.033) between them and five out six sequences of the isolates from the named “sister clade” in the cox 1 phylogeny; the latter ones have distances of 0–2% (Online Resource Table 6 ). However, p-distances of 0% (0.00) (shaded in dark grey in Online Resources Table 6 ) were always found within each haplotype (M2a, M2b), the Morphotype 1 (M1), as well as between the neotype sequence and M1.

On the other hand, a p-distance of 0.004 (still 0%) (shaded in medium grey in Online Resources Table 6 ) was found between the M1 and M2a, between the two M2 haplotypes (M2a and M2b), between the M1 sequences and the neotype sequence, as well as between the latter two and the unnamed mitogenome sequence NC_002074 .The greatest distance observed within the R. sanguineus clade was 1% (0.009) (shaded in light grey in Online Resources Table 6 ). This distance was found between the two isolates that cluster without polytomy in the 16S rRNA phylogenetic tree (M2b) and the M1. These two isolates are also the most distant from all the others on the table.

The outgroup sequences evidenced a p–distance of 10–11% from the others.


## Discussion

Considering the molecular data and the redescription of R. sanguineus based on the establishment of the neotype a few years ago (Nava et al. [YEAR] ), the present study confirms the presence of this species in the Iberian Peninsula, represented by two distinct morphotypes, the main subject of this study. Although the morphological variability of this species has been previously described by several authors (Papadoupolos et al. [YEAR] ; Sanchez-Acedo et al. [YEAR] ; Coimbra-Dores et al. [YEAR] ), for the first time it was corroborated by genetic variability, primarily revealed in the mitochondrial marker cox 1.

This study assessed the morphological traits that have been considered useful for discrimination between Rhipicephalus species, such as the female genital aperture (Feldman-Musham 1956 ; Morel and Vassiliades 1962 ; Walker et al. [YEAR] ; Mihalca et al. [YEAR] ) and the spiracular plates of males and females (Walker et al. [YEAR] ; Dantas-Torres et al. [YEAR] ; Mihalca et al. [YEAR] ), which had been supported by a multivariate analysis (Coimbra-Dores et al. [YEAR] ). Our results reinforce and emphasize the importance of these structures in diagnosing Rhipicephalus adults, clearly distinguishing two morphotypes in females and males (Figs. 1 , 2 , 3 and 4 and Online Resources Tables 3 and 4). It was also evidenced that Morphotype 2 exhibits a R. turanicus- like morphology, which was most likely the basis for the earlier misidentification of R. turanicus in the Iberian Peninsula (Estrada-Peña and Sánchez [YEAR] ; Papadoupolos et al. [YEAR] ).

Regarding the specimens collected from vegetation, it is worth noting that all the isolates with Morphotype 1 (M1) were collected in Chão das Maias (CM) and Casal da Barba Pouca (BP), while the isolates of Morphotype 2 were found in Tapada da Ajuda (TA) and Samora Correia (CL). Furthermore, the two M2 Haplotypes were found in both locations. This occurrence suggests that this variability is eventually established in those tick populations.

The existence of different morphotypes in ticks has also been observed and studied in other species, namely in Amblyomma maculatum and A. triste . The reassessment of the taxonomic status of the two species using a phylogenetic analysis with five molecular markers, revealed that they cluster within three main clades, supported but polytomic, leading to the formation of three monophyletic morphotypes. However, as the divergence values in a combined mitochondrial and nuclear multisequence dataset ranged from 0.0 to 1.7%, the authors considered that, despite morphological variability, the molecular data did not support the separation into different species (Lado et al. [YEAR] ).

Concerning the present study, its first purpose was achieved, firstly for the genetic marker cox 1. The morphological variability was clearly supported by differences found in the phylogenetic and genetic divergence analyses, further corroborated by identification of polymorphic positions in the sequences analyzed. The genetic divergence found led to the establishment of haplotypes associated with the two morphologies: Morphotype 1 with at least one Haplotype (M1a), and Morphotype 2 with Haplotype 2a (M2a) and Haplotype 2b (M2b). So far, the aforementioned haplotypes were confirmed in the 16S rRNA phylogeny, although with a low number of isolates assessed.

This study also showed that the Morphotype 1 and Morphotype 2 sequences cluster together with good to high support (BS/PP = 85/1.00), as well as with the sequences of the R. sanguineus neotype, sequences from Portugal (Almeida et al. [YEAR] ; Coimbra-Dores et al. [YEAR] ; [REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al. [YEAR] ) and the sequences attributed to R. hibericus (Millán et al. [YEAR] ). The divergence between all these sequences ranged from 0 to 1%, according to identity values of 99 to 100%.

Therefore, the phylogenetic and divergence analyses suggested a close taxonomic relationship between the members from the two morphotypes under evaluation, and from the other isolates just designated, namely R. hibericus (Millán et al. [YEAR] ) and, of utmost relevance, R. sanguineus neotype (Nava et al. [YEAR] ). Furthermore, we emphasize that the R. hibericus sequences are related to the two morphotypes we identified, as shown in Fig. 3 and in Online Resources Table 5 . Although the morphological description of the holotype (male) and paratype (female) of R. hibericus is detailed, it does not allow for comparisons due the lack of morphometric criteria. Nevertheless, the spiracle of the holotype (male) appears to fall within the parameters considered for Morphotype 2: the end tail is greater than half the width of the adjacent festoon.

Given the implications that designating R. hibericus as a new species may have on current and future taxonomic classifications, our results may contribute to that debate. Taken together, these results do not allow any distinction between R. hibericus and R. sanguineus , which was molecularly represented by the neotype. This statement is supported by comprehensive data that has recently been evaluated by other authors (Dantas-Torres et al. [YEAR] ; Kelava et al. [YEAR] ).

Additionally, in our phylogeny of the cox 1 marker, the ntST-5 of Rhipicephalus sp. 1 (individual #41) and the two sequences assigned as R. sanguineus s.s . in the description of R. hibericus (Millán et al. [YEAR] ), they cluster with high support (BS/PP = 89/1) inthe R. sanguineus sister clade. This sister clade also groups seven sequences retrieved from GenBank (Fig. 4 ), evidencing p-distances of 0–2% within this clade, while 4–5% was the value found from the sequences of the R. sanguineus clade. These results are reinforced by the blastn sort by identity to the cox 1 gene of the ntST-5 (last access in [YEAR], February 25), which clearly revealed the group of nine sequences earlier referred, with an identity of 98–100% (query coverages of 87–100%). Furthermore, that group (evaluated in our phylogeny) was clearly separated from the long list of R. sanguineus sequences (the neotype included) evidencing a maximum identity of 96% with them. Taken together, these results are suggestive of an undescribed cryptic species in the R. sanguineus complex, which might be a putative new species present in Spain, Hungary, Croatia, Italy, Morocco, and the USA.

It should be mentioned that members of this clade had previously been used in phylogenetic studies (Hornok et al. [YEAR] ; Šlapeta et al. [YEAR] ; Dantas-Torres et al. [YEAR] ), which have also confirmed its segregation from R. sanguineus . More recently, it was named Rhipicephalus sp. (Kelava et al. [YEAR] ). The attribution of two species to the clades shown in the cox 1 phylogeny was certainly part of the process of establishing R. hibericus (Millán et al. [YEAR] ).

Regarding the 16S rRNA phylogeny, it is also worth noting that this study found it to be useful. This marker clearly distinguished between the two morphotypes, and the three haplotypes previously identified in the cox 1 analysis. This finding aligns with the recent use of this marker to support the molecular characterizations in newly described or redescribed species within the genus Rhipicephalus , like R. sanguineus (Nava et al. [YEAR] ), R. linnaei (Šlapeta et al. [YEAR] ), R. afranicus (Bakkes et al. [YEAR] ) and R. secundus (Mumcuoglu et al. [YEAR] ).

As a final remark, it should be emphasized that the application of molecular analysis approaches gave additional objectivity and robustness to the assessment of the taxonomy of R. sanguineus . Moreover, our results highlight the existence of molecular support for the two morphotypes in the Iberian Peninsula. The biological concept behind these differences is still unknown and further ecological and biological studies are needed, including crossbreeding between individuals of both morphotypes. While the use of only two mtDNA markers ( cox 1 and 16S rRNA) imposes certain limitations on phylogenetic resolution, the findings of this study nonetheless contribute valuable insights to the ongoing debate. Future investigations could overcome these constraints by incorporating more comprehensive molecular data, such as complete mitogenomes, or applying high-throughput sequencing approaches (e.g., RAD-Seq).


## Conclusions

In Iberian Peninsula R. sanguineus is a polymorphic species exhibiting morphological and genetic variability. Primarily based on the molecular marker cox 1, it was demonstrated that the morphological variability observed in R. sanguineus is molecularly supported. Nevertheless, despite the consistent identification of morphological and genetic polymorphisms, the global data only supports the existence of a single identity.

Furthermore, the results obtained provided valuable data that helped to clarify the taxonomic classification of the Palearctic Rhipicephalus sanguineus and may contribute to a better understanding of the evolutionary processes that have shaped this species. However, some questions arise: 1) Could the morphological and molecular variability of R. sanguineus in the Iberian Peninsula be due to different population structures that have adapted to various ecosystems of this geographical region? 2) What impact might the morphological and genetic variability have on interactions with microorganisms and efficiency of transmission processes?

Further studies on this species with complex taxonomy will certainly contribute to improving our understanding of the remaining gaps and step forward in advancing knowledge on this issue.
