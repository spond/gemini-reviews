### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript develops and benchmarks publicly accessible Galaxy workflows and accompanying Jupyter notebooks for analyzing bacterial Transposon Insertion Sequencing (TIS) datasets across two major experimental architectures: Tn5-based TraDIS in *Escherichia coli* BW25113 (reanalyzing Goodall et al., BioProject `PRJEB24436`) and phage-assisted Mariner-based TnSeq with promoter-containing constructs in *Staphylococcus aureus* HG003 (reanalyzing Santiago et al., BioProject `PRJNA417822`). The authors survey the maintenance status of existing TIS software tools (Table 1) and construct end-to-end workflows covering barcode demultiplexing (Cutadapt), short-read alignment (Bowtie2 for TraDIS, Bowtie for 16–17 bp MmeI-digested TnSeq reads), 5'- or 3'-end insertion site coverage extraction (deepTools `bamCoverage`), and essential gene classification using three approaches: (1) parametric mixture/curve-fitting regression on gene saturation indices ($S$) in Scipy/Jupyter (fitting Exponential + Gamma for TraDIS and Exponential + Normal for TnSeq at likelihood ratio thresholds $X=4$ and $X=12$) alongside automated fitting via Bio-Tradis; (2) empty-site run-length Gumbel analysis via `TRANSIT` (`Tn5Gaps` / `Gumbel`); and (3) Hidden Markov Model (HMM) site-state classification via `TRANSIT`. Results are compared via UpSet plots against the original studies and external essentiality databases (Keio and PEC for *E. coli*; Chaudhuri et al. and Valentino et al. for *S. aureus*).

The study provides a transparent, well-documented, and fully reproducible open-science resource (with workflows, training tutorials, and Jupyter notebooks archived on GitHub) that addresses the practical complexities of trimming variable construct barcodes and correctly offsetting 5' vs. 3' insertion coordinates in TraDIS vs. MmeI-TnSeq reads. However, the statistical methodology and comparative evaluation contain several notable flaws: (1) the "manual regression" procedure relies on subjective visual curve tweaking ("plot several distributions and correcting the parameters to make them look like our data as much as we could," Methods line 35) and models bounded gene saturation indices $S \in [0, 1]$ with an unbounded Normal distribution in TnSeq, leading to non-intersecting densities and zero-probability artifacts (Figure 7 caption); (2) the manuscript makes directly contradictory statements about `TRANSIT` performance on the *S. aureus* TnSeq dataset (stating on line 84 that "Overall, TRANSIT performs more poorly than other methods" and on line 90 that "For this TnSeq dataset, TRANSIT performed better than the other methods"); (3) the explanation of mean vs. sum replicate aggregation in `TRANSIT` (Methods line 67) contains a mathematical error regarding how sample means behave; and (4) the text mischaracterizes HMMs as considering each site "independently of the previous states" (line 88). These methodological and textual contradictions can be resolved by replacing ad hoc visual curve fitting with maximum-likelihood mixture estimation (EM) and reconciling the contradictory evaluation claims.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Claim 1: The Galaxy TraDIS workflow and Exponential–Gamma saturation-index regression accurately reproduce the *E. coli* BW25113 essential gene calls of Goodall et al. and external databases (Keio, PEC): [Well Supported].**  
  On the *E. coli* TraDIS dataset, the authors' workflow and Exponential + Gamma density fit at threshold $X=12$ identify 364 essential genes compared to 353 in Goodall et al., recovering 230 of 232 core essential genes shared across Goodall, Keio, and PEC, plus 13 PEC-supported essential genes missed in the original study (Figure 2). The concordance across $X=4$, $X=12$, and automated `Bio-Tradis` (which shares 344 of 353 Goodall essential genes with the manual fit; Figure 3c) demonstrates that the preprocessing and saturation-index approach works reliably on high-density *E. coli* TraDIS data.

* **Claim 2: Fitting an Exponential and Normal distribution to the *S. aureus* TnSeq saturation indices via visual parameter initialization provides a reliable classification model: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  For the *S. aureus* TnSeq data, the authors replace the Gamma distribution with a Normal distribution for non-essential genes and fit the two curves separately after manual visual parameter guessing (Methods lines 35, 71). Because the saturation index $S$ is a bounded proportion on $[0, 1]$ and a Normal distribution has support on $(-\infty, +\infty)$, this ad hoc fit fails to form a coherent mixture distribution: as the authors themselves admit in the Figure 7 caption (line 74), *"There is no intersection between the two distributions; however, some genes have a null probability of belonging to either population."* Furthermore, in equation 1 ($\log_2(P(ES)/P(NE)) > \log_2(x)$, Methods line 41), the authors use unweighted component probability density values $f_{ES}(s)/f_{NE}(s)$ without mixture weights $(\pi_{ES}, \pi_{NE})$ and state that *"The use of a logarithm allows ignoring the direction of the ratio for the decision calculation"* (Methods line 43)—which is mathematically false ($\log_2(a/b) = -\log_2(b/a)$; only an absolute value $|\log_2(a/b)|$ ignores direction).

* **Claim 3: Comparative performance of `TRANSIT` Gumbel vs. saturation-index regression on the *S. aureus* TnSeq dataset: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  The manuscript directly contradicts itself within six paragraphs regarding whether `TRANSIT` Gumbel or the regression method performs better on the *S. aureus* TnSeq data. On line 84 (discussing Figure 8E), the authors write: *"TRANSIT and the regression analysis identified half of the full essential genes, about a third of domain-essential genes. Overall, TRANSIT performs more poorly than other methods."* Yet on line 90 (discussing Figure 9 on the exact same dataset), they write: *"TRANSIT prediction was closer to the other studies, with only two reported essential genes not predicted by other methods. The regression method identified 54 additional essential genes... For this TnSeq dataset, TRANSIT performed better than the other methods."* A benchmark cannot simultaneously conclude that a method performs best and worst on the same dataset without explicitly distinguishing precision vs. recall against defined ground truths.

* **Claim 4: Using the mean rather than the sum of read counts across 84 TnSeq samples in `TRANSIT` prevents false-positive insertions because a single mis-mapped read in 30% of 84 samples yields a mean of 0 vs. a sum of 15: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In Methods line 67, the authors justify averaging rather than summing counts across replicates in `TRANSIT` by stating: *"If one read align by mistake at a position in 30% of the 84 samples, the mean would be 0 when the sum would be 15."* First, $30\%$ of $84$ samples is $25.2$ samples (not $15$). Second, in `TRANSIT` Gumbel/Tn5Gaps, a TA site is classified as "impacted" (non-empty) if its read count exceeds the minimum read threshold (by default $>0$ or $\ge 1$); the arithmetic mean of 25 ones and 59 zeros is $25/84 \approx 0.30 > 0$, unless `TRANSIT` performs integer floor rounding on averaged counts prior to binarization—which the authors never state or verify. Moreover, earlier on line 67 of the Results, the authors state they used *"the control condition (containing 14 replicates)"*, whereas Methods line 67 refers to *"84 samples"* ($14 \text{ replicates} \times 6 \text{ constructs}$ or $12 \times 7$), leaving it ambiguous whether the promoter-construct libraries were pooled with control libraries during the `TRANSIT` run.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Replace Visual Curve Tweaking and Unweighted Densities with a Proper Two-Component EM Mixture Model
* **WHY:** Manually guessing parameters to make separate curves "look like our data" (Methods line 35) and fitting an unbounded Normal distribution that produces zero-probability gaps and no intersection with the Exponential component (Figure 7) undermines reproducibility and statistical validity.
* **WHAT:** Formulate the gene saturation index model as a proper two-component mixture density $p(s) = \pi_0 f_0(s; \theta_0) + (1 - \pi_0) f_1(s; \theta_1)$ on $s \in [0, 1]$ and fit it via Expectation-Maximization (EM) or maximum likelihood.
* **HOW:** In the provided Jupyter notebooks (`Essential genes in E. coli K12.ipynb` and `Essential genes in S. aureus.ipynb`), fit a two-component Exponential–Gamma or Beta-mixture / Zero-Inflated Beta model via maximum likelihood (using `scipy.optimize.minimize` on the negative log-likelihood of the raw gene saturation values $s_i$, rather than least-squares curve-fitting to histogram bins). Compute the posterior odds $\frac{\pi_0 f_0(s_i)}{\pi_1 f_1(s_i)} > X$ (and $ < 1/X$ for non-essential genes) and update Figures 1, 7, 8, and 9.

#### 2. Reconcile the Contradictory Evaluations of `TRANSIT` on the *S. aureus* TnSeq Dataset
* **WHY:** Stating that *"Overall, TRANSIT performs more poorly than other methods"* (line 84) and six paragraphs later that *"For this TnSeq dataset, TRANSIT performed better than the other methods"* (line 90) confuses the reader about the study's core benchmark conclusion.
* **WHAT:** Quantify sensitivity (recall), specificity/precision (against the 229-gene multi-study core essential set and the Santiago et al. curated set), and F1/Jaccard scores in a single summary table, and rewrite lines 84–90 so they are internally consistent.
* **HOW:** Explain clearly that `TRANSIT` Gumbel is more conservative on the *S. aureus* dataset (high precision: only 2 unique calls outside the multi-study consensus in Figure 9, but lower recall of Santiago domain-essential genes in Figure 8E), whereas the $X=4$ regression is more permissive (higher recall of Santiago calls, but 54 extra calls corresponding to growth-depleted rather than strictly essential genes).

#### 3. Correct the Mathematical Description of HMMs, Log-Ratios, and Replicate Averaging
* **WHY:** Several mathematical and algorithmic descriptions in the text are inaccurate:
  1. Line 88 states: *"Hidden Markov Model (HMM) methods consider each potential insertion site independently of the previous states to predict the state of the next position..."* In fact, the defining Markov property of an HMM is that the hidden state at site $t$ depends conditionally on the previous state $t-1$ via the transition matrix, while the observed emission (read count) depends on the current hidden state.
  2. Methods line 43 states that taking $\log_2(P(ES)/P(NE))$ *"allows ignoring the direction of the ratio."*
  3. Methods line 67 states that 1 read in 30% of 84 samples gives a sum of 15 and a mean of 0, and confuses the 14 control replicates (Results line 67) with the 84 total construct libraries.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  1. Rewrite line 88 to accurately state that HMMs model the hidden essentiality state of each TA site as a first-order Markov chain (where each site's state depends on the state of the preceding site via transition probabilities).
  2. Fix Formula 1 (Methods line 41–43) to specify $\log_2(P(ES)/P(NE)) > \log_2(X)$ for essential genes and $\log_2(P(ES)/P(NE)) < -\log_2(X)$ for non-essential genes.
  3. Clarify in Results line 67 and Methods lines 59, 67 exactly which TnSeq runs used the 14 control replicates versus all 84 construct datasets, and explain that `TRANSIT`'s mean-aggregation option rounds or thresholds fractional mean counts below 1 to 0.

#### 4. Fix Textual and Formatting Glitches in Figure 8 Legend and Table 1
* **WHY:** In the Figure 8f caption (lines 74 and 80), a number was accidentally scrubbed or corrupted as `"[YEAR] are common across all results"` (referring to the number of shared non-essential genes, likely ~2000+), and Table 1 is missing its text rows in the markdown/manuscript body (replaced by `[JWFFXGRAPHICS]s12866-021-02184-4tmc1.eps`).
* **WHAT & HOW:** Restore the exact count of common non-essential genes in the Figure 8f legend and format Table 1 as a standard text table rather than an embedded EPS graphic so that tool metrics (commits, contributors, licenses) are machine-readable and searchable.
