### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript investigates the nucleotide compositional heterogeneity of eukaryotic genomes using early long contiguous chromosomal sequences from five taxa: *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (chrs. IV and XV), *Caenorhabditis elegans* (chrs. I and V), *Arabidopsis thaliana* (chrs. 2 and 4), *Drosophila melanogaster* (chr. 2L and 3R), and *Homo sapiens* (contigs from chrs. 6, 21, and 22, plus 430 Mb of ORNL draft contigs). The authors introduce a normalized "compositional heterogeneity index" ($H_{gc}$), defined as the mean absolute difference in GC content between adjacent windows of length $l$ divided by the binomial standard error expected under a random nucleotide distribution, $\sqrt{P(1-P)/l}$. They also propose a greedy sliding-window segmentation ("decomposition") algorithm to identify compositionally homogeneous segments (which they redefine as isochores when length $\ge 100$ kb and internal 10-kb window range $\le 7\%$ GC, calibrated so that *S. cerevisiae* chr. IV forms a single segment). Using these tools, they report that $H_{gc}$ correlates positively with overall sequence GC content across multicellular eukaryotes ($r^2 = 64\%–74\%$), that human chromosomes are far more compositionally heterogeneous than implied by Bernardi's classic (>300 kb) isochore model, and that pooling 100-kb genomic windows into coarse GC bins computationally reproduces classic buoyant density centrifugation profiles while masking fine-scale heterogeneity.

The study addresses a foundational question in comparative genomics and provides an intuitive demonstration (Figure 5) of why 100-kb buoyant density centrifugation fractions can create the illusion of internally homogeneous >300-kb isochores despite substantial sub-100-kb GC variation. However, the mathematical formulation and statistical analyses suffer from several methodological flaws: (1) all three mathematical equations in the text (lines 33, 36, 113, 173, 176, 179) are missing or corrupted in the manuscript formatting, and the verbal definition of $H_{gc}$ conflates the standard error of a single window with the standard error of the difference between two windows (explaining why the authors' empirical null baseline in Table 1 is $\sim 1.13 \approx 2/\sqrt{\pi}$ rather than $1.0$); (2) the linear regressions between GC content and $H_{gc}$ ($N=11$ sequences from 5 species) treat multiple chromosomes from the same species and phylogenetically related taxa as independent data points, and appear heavily leveraged by yeast at the low end and human chr. 22 at the high end; (3) the greedy left-to-right segmentation algorithm is directional and sensitive to single-window spikes; and (4) Table 3 contains an obvious numerical duplication error (`10.291` repeated identically across three different rows for 100-kb windows). All of these issues are addressable via analytical correction and re-analysis of the existing sequences.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Claim 1: The Compositional Heterogeneity Index ($H_{gc}$) has a theoretical and simulation baseline of $\sim 1.1$ independent of window size and mean GC content under a random nucleotide distribution: [Moderately Supported].**  
  In Table 1, simulations of 10,000 random fragments yield $H_{gc} \approx 1.12–1.15$ across GC contents from 5% to 50% and window sizes of 10 kb and 100 kb. However, the authors never derive the exact analytical expectation of their statistic. If $GC_i \sim \mathcal{N}(P, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma = \sqrt{P(1-P)/l}$, then the difference between two independent windows $D = GC_i - GC_{i-1}$ follows $\mathcal{N}(0, 2\sigma^2)$, and its expected absolute value is $E[|D|] = \sigma \sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{2/\pi} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \sigma \approx 1.12838\,\sigma$. Because the authors normalized $E[|GC_i - GC_{i-1}|]$ by the single-window standard error $\sigma$ rather than the standard error of the difference ($\sqrt{2}\sigma$) or the half-normal expectation ($\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sigma$), their baseline sits at $2/\sqrt{\pi} \approx 1.13$. Moreover, the manuscript assumes an i.i.d. binomial nucleotide model rather than a first-order Markov (dinucleotide) model, even though CpG dinucleotide depletion and AA/TT runs slightly alter local variance in real genomes.

* **Claim 2: Compositional heterogeneity ($H_{gc}$) strongly correlates with overall GC content across multicellular eukaryotes regardless of genome size ($r^2 = 64\%$ for 50-kb and $r^2 = 74\%$ for 100-kb windows): [Fragile].**  
  The regression in Figure 1A ($r^2 = 64\%, P = 0.002$ and $r^2 = 74\%, P < 0.0001$, line 58) is computed across the 11 sequences in Table 2 (2 *S. cerevisiae*, 2 *C. elegans*, 2 *A. thaliana*, 2 *D. melanogaster*, and 3 *H. sapiens* sequences). Treating multiple chromosomes/contigs from the same species as statistically independent observations violates phylogenetic and intra-genomic non-independence. Furthermore, inspecting the text (line 58) shows that yeast has "extremely low compositional heterogeneity... despite the fact that their GC content is comparable to those of the nematode and *Arabidopsis* chromosomes," while human chr. 22 (and the short 3.9-Mb MHC contig on chr. 6) sits at the extreme high-GC, high-$H_{gc}$ tail. Without yeast and the two GC-rich human contigs, it is unclear whether a general inter-species correlation holds across multicellular eukaryotes.

* **Claim 3: Human chromosomes are far more heterogeneous than predicted by the classic (>300 kb) isochore model, and 100-kb computational fractionation replicates buoyant density centrifugation profiles while concealing sub-100-kb heterogeneity: [Well Supported].**  
  The comparison in Figure 5 between computational 100-kb window binning of 430 Mb of draft human sequence and Bernardi's experimental buoyant density centrifugation classes (L1, L2, H1, H2, H3) is the strongest result in the paper. Combined with the segment length distributions in Figure 3 (showing that very few homogeneous segments exceed 300 kb even under a permissive 7% GC fluctuation limit, particularly on GC-rich chr. 22 where average segment length is 150 kb and isochores >=100 kb cover only ~30% of the chromosome), this directly demonstrates that buoyant density classes reflect mean GC pooling of internally heterogeneous ~100-kb DNA fragments.

* **Claim 4: Calibrating the decomposition algorithm on *S. cerevisiae* chr. IV (yielding a 7% fluctuation limit over 10-kb windows) provides an objective universal standard to define isochores ($\ge 100$ kb) across all eukaryotes: [Overstated].**  
  The decomposition algorithm (line 185) scans 5'-to-3' in 10-kb windows (5-kb step), seeding a segment when $|gc_i - gc_{i+1}| \le FL$ and extending until $\max(gc) - \min(gc) > FL$. First, this greedy unidirectional scan is asymmetric (running 3'-to-5' will produce different segment boundaries) and non-robust to single-window outliers (such as a single 10-kb transposable element cluster or rDNA/subtelomeric repeat), which the authors themselves note divided yeast chr. IV into seven segments at $FL = 5\%$ despite segment means all lying between 37.6% and 39.4% (line 116). Second, calibrating $FL = 7\%$ simply to force the 1.5-Mb yeast chr. IV into a single uninterrupted block makes the threshold dependent on chromosome length: under an i.i.d. or weakly correlated process, the expected range ($\max - \min$) of $N$ windows grows with $N$ (extreme value theory), so a threshold that spans $N \approx 300$ overlapping windows in yeast without breaking will inevitably break more often in a 28-Mb chromosome even at identical local variance.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Restore Missing Equations, Correct the $H_{gc}$ Normalization Constant, and Fix Table 3 Typos
* **WHY:** All displayed equations in Results and Methods (lines 33, 36, 113, 173, 176, 179) are blank in the manuscript text, and Table 3 lists the exact same number (`10.291`) for 100-kb adjacent windows across three completely different datasets (`21 all`, `22 contig`, and `22 all`, lines 90–92), which is a clear copy-paste error (since `22 contig` at 50-kb is `12.817` and `21 all` at 50-kb is `7.990`).
* **WHAT:** Insert the explicit mathematical equations and correct the duplicated entries in Table 3.
* **HOW:** Write out Equation 1 as $\overline{\Delta GC} = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1} |GC_{i+1} - GC_i|$, Equation 2 as $SE = \sqrt{\frac{P(1-P)}{l}}$, and Equation 3 as $H_{gc} = \frac{\overline{\Delta GC}}{\sqrt{P(1-P)/l}}$. Explicitly note that under an i.i.d. binomial null, $\mathbb{E}[|GC_{i+1}-GC_i|] = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{P(1-P)}{l}} \approx 1.128\sqrt{\frac{P(1-P)}{l}}$, which analytically explains the empirical simulation baseline of 1.12–1.14 in Table 1 without requiring ad hoc interpretation. Recalculate and replace the corrupted `10.291` values for `22 contig` and `22 all` (Win = 100 kb, Adjacent Windows) in Table 3.

#### 2. Control for Phylogenetic Non-Independence and Leverage in the GC vs. $H_{gc}$ Regression
* **WHY:** Computing an ordinary least squares regression across 11 chromosomes drawn from 5 species (with 3 from human, including a 3.9-Mb MHC contig on chr. 6) inflates degrees of freedom and confounds intra-genomic variation (human chr. 21 vs. chr. 22) with inter-species differences.
* **WHAT:** Re-evaluate the relationship between GC content and $H_{gc}$ both within species and across species-level means, and test sensitivity to outliers.
* **HOW:** (1) Report the correlation across the 4 multicellular species means (excluding unicellular yeast, which the text already notes follows a different regime); (2) compute the intra-human correlation across the 430 Mb of ORNL contigs (or across individual human chromosomes) separately from the inter-species comparison; and (3) verify that human chr. 6 contig `NT_001520` (3.9 Mb) is not biasing $H_{gc}$ due to its 6–10$\times$ shorter length compared to the 15–28.5 Mb chromosomes.

#### 3. Replace or Benchmark the Greedy Range Segmentation Against Standard Change-Point / Jensen-Shannon Segmentation
* **WHY:** The greedy 5'-to-3' range-bounding algorithm ($\max - \min \le FL$) is order-dependent (3'-to-5' gives different segments) and terminates a long homogeneous region whenever a single 10-kb window fluctuates by $>FL/2$ from the local mean.
* **WHAT:** Test whether the segment length distributions in Figure 3 are robust to scan direction and compare them against a symmetric segmentation method (or a null permutation test).
* **HOW:** Run the decomposition algorithm in both 5'-to-3' and 3'-to-5' directions on the Table 2 sequences and report the concordance of segment boundaries and length distributions. In addition, compare the empirical segment length distributions in Figure 3 against recursive entropic (Jensen-Shannon divergence) segmentation or circular binary segmentation, which detect statistically significant shifts in mean GC content without relying on an arbitrary yeast-calibrated $7\%$ hard range cutoff.

#### 4. Quantify the Masking Effect of Repetitive Elements in a Dedicated Table
* **WHY:** Line 101 states that masking interspersed and tandem repeats in human chromosomes 21 and 22 increases compositional heterogeneity ($H_{gc}$), concluding that repeats homogenize chromosomal GC content. However, excising repeats and concatenating the remaining sequence segments artificially brings distant genomic regions into direct adjacency and shrinks the physical span represented by a 50-kb or 100-kb window.
* **WHAT:** Test whether the increase in $H_{gc}$ after repeat masking is a genuine biological property of non-repetitive DNA or an artifact of sequence excision and window compression.
* **HOW:** Recompute $H_{gc}$ on fixed physical windows (e.g., 50 kb and 100 kb of genomic coordinate span) where repetitive bases are masked as `N` and excluded from the numerator and denominator (setting $l$ in the standard error normalization to the number of unmasked bases $l_{\text{unmasked}}$ in each window), rather than concatenating unmasked fragments across excised repeats. Report the unmasked vs. physical-window-masked $H_{gc}$ values in Table 3.
