### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript combines experimental evolution in a turbidostat with ultra-deep duplex sequencing and mathematical modeling to investigate why small multicopy plasmids (specifically pBR322 in *Escherichia coli* DH5$\alpha$ under $30\ \mu\text{g/ml}$ tetracycline selection) remain genetically stable during host-plasmid coadaptation. Across two short-term (60 h; replicates R1, R2) and two long-term (318 h; replicates R6, R7) turbidostat runs sampled every 12–84 h (24 duplex-sequencing datasets total), the authors detect low-frequency single-nucleotide variants (<1% allele frequency) clustering exclusively at 10 sites in the ColE1 replication control region (positions 3,027–3,035 in loop II' of RNAI and position 3,118 in the $-35$ promoter of RNAII). These plasmid variants rise early and then crash toward zero as two chromosomal mutations—`treB` (Val112Glu, reaching 92% in R6 and 39% in R7) and a `recA1` $\to$ `recA` reversal (Asp161Gly, reaching 91% in R6 and 7% in R7)—sweep through the host population. The authors formulate a 7-parameter discrete-generation model of intracellular plasmid partitioning, replication, and piecewise copy-number selection, optimizing parameters via least squares (`optim`, 1,863 random restarts). Workflows and simulation notebooks are shared online, though the key mathematical equations in the Materials and Methods are blank in the manuscript text.

The experimental application of duplex sequencing to track sub-1% plasmid evolution in real time is creative and uncovers a clear empirical phenomenon: transient emergence and subsequent clonal-interference purging of ori-region copy-number mutants. However, the mathematical modeling and several experimental interpretations have substantial weaknesses: (1) all mathematical equations in the Materials and Methods (lines 35–85) are missing/blank in the manuscript text; (2) the deterministic/mean-field model fits 7 free parameters to a single summary trajectory ($\beta_t$, the sum of 10 site frequencies) across only ~6–11 time points and *fails* to reproduce the empirical crash to zero (instead plateauing near 0.5%, as acknowledged on line 140); (3) the piecewise fitness function assigns cells with a chromosomal mutation ($g=1$) a flat survival probability of 1.0 regardless of plasmid copy number up to $\kappa_T$, implausibly assuming that chromosomal adaptation (`treB`/`recA`) eliminates the metabolic and membrane-potential toxicity of runaway plasmid copy number; and (4) plasmid copy number was never experimentally measured (e.g., via qPCR) to confirm that the RNAI/RNAII variants actually altered copy number in vivo. These issues can be addressed by restoring the missing equations, fixing the fitness function so copy-number burden applies on both host backgrounds, and reframing the model's conclusions.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Low-frequency (<1%) plasmid variants arise reproducibly at 10 sites within the ColE1 replication control region (bases 3,027–3,035 in RNAI loop II' and 3,118 in the RNAII $-35$ promoter) early during turbidostat adaptation and decline as chromosomal mutations (`treB` Val112Glu and `recA` Asp161Gly) sweep: [Well Supported].**  
  Duplex sequencing across four independent replicates (R1, R2, R6, R7) consistently identifies recurring trajectories at these exact 10 functional regulatory nucleotides while the rest of the pBR322 plasmid shows only transient single-family "flickering" variants. Whole-genome sequencing of the host and plasmid-curing growth assays (Table 1) cleanly demonstrate that population fitness gains are driven by the chromosomal `treB` and `recA` sweeps.

* **The 7-parameter mathematical model accurately captures plasmid evolutionary dynamics and demonstrates that intracellular copy-number selection prevents plasmid mutations from fixing: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  First, the model is severely overparameterized relative to the data: 7 parameters ($\gamma, \mu, r, R, S, \kappa^*, \kappa_T$) are fit by unweighted ordinary least squares to the summed allele frequency $\beta_t$ across a handful of time points, yielding an optimization surface with massive parameter non-identifiability (requiring 1,863 random restarts). Second, the fitted plasmid mutation rate $\mu = 5.09 \times 10^{-5}$ per plasmid per generation (Table 2) is **5,000-fold higher** than the biological point mutation rate of the 10-bp target region in *E. coli* ($\sim 10^{-9}\text{ bp}^{-1}\text{ gen}^{-1} \times 10\text{ bp} = 10^{-8}$, or $10^{-6}$ across the whole 4.3 kb plasmid), indicating that the optimizer used an unphysically high mutation influx $\mu$ to force an immediate rise in $\beta_t$. Third, as the authors admit (line 140), the fitted trajectory plateaus at ~0.5% and fails to reproduce the empirical disappearance of mutant plasmids in R6/R7 because the model assumes a constant generation time and assigns $g=1$ cells an invariant fitness of 1.0 for any $1 \le w+m < \kappa_T$, completely turning off selection against mutant plasmids once the chromosomal mutation fixes!

* **The piecewise fitness function $S(g, w+m)$ (lines 75–76) is biologically well-specified across host genetic backgrounds ($g=0$ vs. $g=1$): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  According to lines 75–76, when a cell acquires the chromosomal mutation ($g=1$, which empirically is `treB` Val112Glu + `recA` reversal—general medium adaptations that increase growth rate in plasmid-free cells; line 122), its survival probability is set to a flat $1.0$ for all plasmid copy numbers from $1$ up to $\kappa_T - 1 = 24$, whereas for $g=0$ cells fitness peaks at $\kappa^* = 20$ and declines linearly between $\kappa^* = 20$ and $\kappa_T = 25$. Because `treB` and `recA` adapt the host to LB medium/recombination rather than detoxifying TetA efflux pumps, setting $S(1, w+m) = 1$ between $\kappa^*$ and $\kappa_T$ removes the selective penalty against over-replicating mutant plasmids ($R = 3.417 > r = 1.539$) on the $g=1$ background. This mathematical artifact is the exact reason why mutant plasmids linger forever on the $g=1$ background in Figure 2 instead of being purged!

* **Intracellular *trans*-acting RNAI vs. *cis*-acting RNAII interactions create a heteroplasmic balancing act that resists copy-number evolution: [Moderately Supported].**  
  The biological discussion of ColE1 regulation is insightful, but the mathematical model does not actually distinguish *trans*-acting RNAI mutations (bases 3,027–3,035) from *cis*-acting RNAII promoter mutations (base 3,118): the model assigns mutant plasmids an intrinsic *cis* replication advantage ($R = 3.417$ vs. $r = 1.539$) while leaving wild-type plasmid replication $r$ inside the same heteroplasmic cell unaffected. If RNAI acts in *trans* on all co-resident plasmids (line 128 & 145), a mutation in RNAI should increase the replication rate of both wild-type and mutant plasmids within that cell rather than conferring a $2.2\times$ *cis*-selective advantage to the mutant plasmid molecule inside the cell.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Fix the Fitness Function $S(1, w+m)$ so Copy-Number Burden Applies on the Evolved Chromosomal Background
* **WHY:** Setting $S(1, w+m) = 1.0$ for all $1 \le w+m < \kappa_T$ (line 75) assumes that chromosomal adaptation to LB medium (`treB`/`recA`) abolishes the metabolic cost of carrying $21\text{–}24$ plasmids, while mutant plasmids continue to out-replicate wild-type plasmids within every $g=1$ cell ($R = 3.417$ vs. $r = 1.539$). This mathematical artifact causes the model to plateau near $0.5\%$ instead of crashing to zero as observed empirically in Figure 2.
* **WHAT:** Modify the fitness function for $g=1$ so that the chromosomal mutation increases baseline growth rate (or scales overall fitness by $1 + s_{\text{chrom}}$) while preserving the copy-number optimum $\kappa^*$ and penalty above $\kappa^*$.
* **HOW:** Parameterize $S(g, k)$ multiplicatively as $S(g, k) = (1 + g \cdot s_{\text{chrom}}) \cdot f_{\text{plasmid}}(k)$, where $f_{\text{plasmid}}(k)$ increases up to $\kappa^*$ and decreases from $\kappa^*$ to $\kappa_T$ for both $g=0$ and $g=1$. Re-run the simulation to verify that once $g=1$ sweeps, cells carrying high-replication mutant plasmids ($R > r$) overshoot $\kappa^*$ and are purged by purifying selection, reproducing the empirical crash in R6 and R7.

#### 2. Constrain the Plasmid Mutation Rate $\mu$ to Biologically Plausible Values for a 10-bp Target
* **WHY:** In Table 2, the inferred plasmid mutation rate is $\mu = 5.09 \times 10^{-5}$ per plasmid per generation. Since $\beta_t$ sums variants at only 10 specific nucleotide sites (3,027–3,035 and 3,118), a per-plasmid rate of $5.09 \times 10^{-5}$ implies a per-base mutation rate of $5 \times 10^{-6}\text{ bp}^{-1}\text{ gen}^{-1}$—four orders of magnitude above the known *E. coli* point mutation rate of $10^{-10}\text{–}10^{-9}\text{ bp}^{-1}\text{ gen}^{-1}$ cited by the authors on lines 16 and 112.
* **WHAT:** Fix or tightly bound $\mu$ and $\gamma$ to empirically established *E. coli* target-size mutation rates ($\mu \in [10^{-9}, 10^{-7}]$ for the 10-bp ori target; $\gamma \in [10^{-7}, 10^{-5}]$ for beneficial chromosomal targets) and allow standing genetic variation during the 14-hour pre-turbidostat overnight growth (~14 generations from single colony to time point 0) to seed initial mutant frequencies.
* **HOW:** Include the ~25–30 generations of colony expansion + 14 h overnight culture prior to $t=0$ in the forward simulation with $\mu = 10^{-8}$ per plasmid per generation, reducing the number of free fitted parameters from 7 to 5 and eliminating the unphysical mutation-rate estimate.

#### 3. Separate *cis*-Acting (Site 3,118) and *trans*-Acting (Sites 3,027–3,035) Trajectories
* **WHY:** The authors emphasize that RNAI (3,027–3,035) acts in *trans* on all plasmids in the cell whereas the RNAII promoter (3,118) acts in *cis* (line 145), yet they sum both loci into a single $\beta_t$ curve and model all mutants as having a purely *cis* replication advantage ($R > r$).
* **WHAT:** Plot and analyze the empirical allele frequency trajectory of the *cis*-acting RNAII promoter variant (site 3,118) separately from the *trans*-acting RNAI loop II' variants (sites 3,027–3,035).
* **HOW:** Add a panel to Figure 1/Figure 2 comparing the summed frequency of sites 3,027–3,035 against site 3,118 across R1, R2, R6, and R7, and test whether the *cis*-acting variant reaches a higher relative frequency or persists longer than the *trans*-acting variants.

#### 4. Restore All Omitted Mathematical Equations and Raw Sequencing Accession Numbers
* **WHY:** All 11 displayed mathematical equations in the Materials and Methods (lines 35, 60, 62, 64, 66, 68, 70, 72, 75, 77, 80, 82, 84) are missing/blank in the manuscript text, making it impossible to verify the exact algebraic transitions for plasmid replication ($w_{\text{new}}$) and selection. Furthermore, while the Data Availability section links to a GitHub repository for the R optimization, no NCBI SRA / ENA accession number is provided for the 24 duplex-sequencing datasets and 4 host whole-genome sequencing datasets.
* **WHAT & HOW:** Insert the complete LaTeX/typeset equations at lines 35–84, and deposit the raw fastq files for the 24 plasmid duplex-sequencing runs and 4 host WGS runs in NCBI SRA, reporting the BioProject accession number in the Data Availability section.
