### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript investigates the evolutionary divergence of human duplicate genes within a tissue-specific mRNA coexpression network constructed from microarray data across 79 human tissues (U133A and GNF1H arrays mapped to 14,342 Ensembl build 34 genes with 1-to-1 probe-set correspondence). Connecting gene pairs expressed ($AD > 200$) in at least $T \ge 7$ shared tissues with Pearson correlation $R \ge 0.7$ of $\log_2$-transformed expression values yields a giant component of 12,897 nodes (6,507 duplicate genes and 6,390 singletons; mean degree $\langle k \rangle = 132.4$). Across 698 independent duplicate pairs ($0.05 \le K_S < 2$) present in the network (and 1,547 pairs where at least one copy is present), the authors report that (1) duplicates have lower average degree than singletons (Table 1: 120.11 vs. 144.92; text line 44: "102.1" vs. 144.9), (2) the fraction of shared coexpressed partners $n_{12}/(n_1 + n_2 - n_{12})$ declines rapidly with $K_S$ ($R = -0.66$, averaging 6.6% at $K_S \approx 0.13$), (3) the combined partner count $n_1 + n_2 - n_{12}$ of duplicate pairs (227.9) exceeds the singleton average (144.9), implying rapid acquisition of new partners, (4) partner divergence is asymmetric ($r_s = -0.19$ between randomly assigned X and Y axes), and (5) removing hubs across all genes breaks the network faster than removing hubs restricted to duplicates or singletons alone. Perl/C scripts, the binary adjacency matrix, and duplicate pair tables are provided as Additional files 5–7.

The study addresses an interesting evolutionary systems biology question and is commendable for bundling its Perl/C code, binary adjacency matrix, and gene-pair lists directly in the supplementary files. However, several statistical and network-construction flaws undermine its quantitative conclusions: (1) computing Pearson correlation $R \ge 0.7$ across *only the $T$ tissues where both genes have $AD > 200$* (as Few as $T=7$ tissues out of 79) means different gene pairs are correlated over different sample sizes ($7 \le n \le 79$) and completely ignores discordant tissue expression (e.g., two genes expressed in 40 tissues each that overlap in only 7 tissues); (2) the negative Spearman correlation ($r_s = -0.19$) in Figure 7A is a statistical artifact of conditioning on 1,547 pairs where *at least one copy is in the network* (excluding $(0,0)$ pairs while retaining 849 $(k,0)$ and $(0,k)$ pairs on the axes); (3) comparing the combined partners of conditioned in-network duplicate pairs ($n=698$, where *both* copies must have degree $\ge 1$) against all singletons biases $n_1 + n_2 - n_{12}$ upward; (4) the in silico "attack" comparison (Figure 8C–D) is a trivial mathematical consequence of restricting removal to a 50% subset (duplicates only or singletons only), which by definition leaves 100% of the hubs in the other 50% of the network intact; and (5) there is a numerical discrepancy between Table 1 ($\langle k \rangle = 120.11$ for duplicates) and the main text (line 44: "102.1"). All of these issues can be resolved through computational re-analysis using the authors' existing scripts and matrices.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Human duplicate genes rapidly lose shared coexpression partners with evolutionary time ($K_S$), dropping to 6.6% overlap at $K_S \approx 0.13$ (~50 MY): [Moderately Supported].**  
  The negative trend between Jaccard similarity $n_{12}/(n_1 + n_2 - n_{12})$ and $K_S$ across binned pairs ($R = -0.66, P < 0.003$; Figure 5) is clear. However, the edge definition requires $R \ge 0.7$ computed *only across the $T \ge 7$ tissues where both genes exceed $AD > 200$* (lines 25, 108). Because microarray measurements have substantial technical noise, two duplicate genes with identical true expression profiles that are moderately correlated ($R \approx 0.65$) will have independent threshold crossings against third-party genes, severely depressing the binary edge overlap $n_{12}/(n_1+n_2-n_{12})$ even at low divergence. Moreover, probe sets hitting multiple genes (case 3: 18,718 genes / 4,377 probe sets; line 104) and pairs with $K_S < 0.05$ (line 112) were excluded due to cross-hybridization, removing the youngest, most conserved duplicates.

* **Duplicate gene pairs rapidly acquire new coexpressed partners because $n_1 + n_2 - n_{12} = 227.9 > n_s = 144.9$: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  To test whether duplicate pairs gain new partners ($n_1 + n_2 - n_{12} > n_s$), the authors compare the 698 duplicate pairs **for which both genes were present in the giant cluster of the network** ($n_1 \ge 1$ and $n_2 \ge 1$; line 56 & 63) against the average degree of singletons in the network ($n_s = 144.9$). However, as revealed in the very next section (line 72), out of 1,547 duplicate pairs with at least one member in the network, **849 pairs had one copy with degree 0 (outside the network)**, and out of 4,997 total independent duplicate pairs (line 112), 3,450 pairs had *neither* copy in the network! Conditioning the partner-gain analysis on the 698 elite pairs where *both* copies survived expression filtering and achieved degree $\ge 1$ creates a severe selection bias: the sum of two positive degrees ($n_1 + n_2 - n_{12}$) in doubly-connected pairs is guaranteed to exceed the singleton average even under pure partner loss or neutral random assignment.

* **Divergence of duplicate genes is asymmetric because the scatter plot of degree $n_1$ vs. $n_2$ across 1,547 pairs shows a negative Spearman correlation ($r_s = -0.19, P < 0.001$; Figure 7A): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In Figure 7A (line 72), the authors take 1,547 duplicate pairs where *at least one copy is present in the network* ($n_1 > 0$ or $n_2 > 0$, excluding all pairs where $n_1 = 0$ and $n_2 = 0$), randomly assign copies to X and Y, and compute Spearman's $r_s = -0.19$. Because 849 of the 1,547 pairs have one gene absent from the network ($n_1 = 0, n_2 > 0$ or $n_1 > 0, n_2 = 0$), truncating the $(0,0)$ quadrant forces 55% of the data onto the positive X and Y axes $(>0, 0)$ and $(0, >0)$. Any bivariate distribution of independent or even positively correlated non-negative variables truncated on $(X > 0 \cup Y > 0)$ with a large point mass at zero automatically induces a negative correlation between $X$ and $Y$! Furthermore, computing a correlation after random assignment of interchangeable pairs $(X,Y)$ depends on the random seed; the proper statistic is the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) across all 4,997 pairs (including $(0,0)$) or comparing $|n_1 - n_2|$ against random pairs of genes matched for $K_S$ and expression level.

* **The coexpression network is resilient to degree-based attack on either duplicate genes or singletons alone, but vulnerable when duplicates and singletons are attacked together (Figure 8C–D): [Overstated].**  
  In Figure 8C–D (lines 84–88, 118), "attacking 50% of nodes" in the "duplicates only" experiment means removing 6,449 of the 6,507 duplicate genes while leaving **100% of the 6,390 singleton genes (including all singleton hubs, which have a higher average degree of 144.92) untouched**. Conversely, attacking 50% of "all genes" removes the top 6,449 highest-degree hubs across the *entire* network (both duplicates and singletons), leaving only low-degree peripheral nodes. It is a mathematical truism on any graph that deleting the top 50% global hubs destroys the giant component faster than deleting 99% of one half of the nodes while leaving every hub in the other half intact.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Control for Sample-Size ($T$) Bias and Tissue Discordance in Coexpression Edge Construction
* **WHY:** Computing Pearson's $R$ exclusively over the $T$ tissues where *both* genes have $AD > 200$ (lines 25, 108) means that a gene pair co-expressed in only $T=7$ tissues is evaluated on 7 data points (where $|R| \ge 0.7$ occurs with high probability by chance: two-tailed $p = 0.08$), whereas a pair expressed in 79 tissues is evaluated on 79 data points ($p = 8 \times 10^{-13}$). It also connects genes that have completely discordant presence/absence across the remaining 72 tissues.
* **WHAT:** Reconstruct the coexpression network using a uniform correlation across all 79 tissues (or a $p$-value / FDR-calibrated correlation threshold that accounts for the number of shared tissues $T$).
* **HOW:** Compute the Pearson (and Spearman) correlation across all 79 tissues (flooring unexpressed tissues at $\log_2(200)$ or using Fisher's $z$-transformation $z = \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1+R}{1-R}\sqrt{T-3}$ to require a uniform statistical significance threshold across variable $T$), and verify that the partner-loss and partner-gain curves (Figures 5–6) hold.

#### 2. Eliminate Truncation Conditioning Bias in the Partner-Gain (Figure 6) and Asymmetry (Figure 7) Analyses
* **WHY:** Restricting Figure 6 to the 698 pairs where *both* copies are in the network ($n_1 \ge 1, n_2 \ge 1$) inflates $n_1 + n_2 - n_{12}$, while restricting Figure 7A to the 1,547 pairs where *at least one* copy is in the network ($n_1 + n_2 > 0$, excluding $(0,0)$ but keeping 849 $(k,0)/(0,k)$ pairs) artificially induces the negative Spearman correlation $r_s = -0.19$.
* **WHAT:** Evaluate $n_1 + n_2 - n_{12}$ and degree asymmetry across all duplicate pairs where both copies have valid microarray probe sets on the chip (including degree-0 nodes), and compare against a null distribution of randomly paired singletons processed through the exact same inclusion filters.
* **HOW:**
  1. For partner gain (Figure 6): Compute $n_1 + n_2 - n_{12}$ across all $1,547$ pairs (where one copy has degree 0) and across all pairs with valid probe sets, and compare the 698 doubly-connected pairs against **random pairs of in-network singletons** ($n_{s1} + n_{s2} - n_{s1,s2} \approx 144.9 + 144.9 - 0.019 \times 289.8 \approx 284.3$). Notice that two random in-network singletons have an expected union of **~284 partners**, which is *greater* than the 227.9 partners of duplicate pairs!
  2. For asymmetry (Figure 7A): Replace the random-axis Spearman correlation on zero-truncated pairs with the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) and compare the observed absolute degree difference $|n_1 - n_2| / (n_1 + n_2)$ against the Wagner symmetric binomial partitioning null model (Figure 7B) matched on total partner count.

#### 3. Fix the Asymmetric Hub-Attack Baseline in Figure 8C–D
* **WHY:** Removing 6,449 nodes from the 6,507 duplicates deletes both hubs and low-degree nodes among duplicates while shielding 100% of singleton hubs, making it an invalid comparison against removing the top 6,449 global hubs across all 12,897 genes.
* **WHAT:** Compare degree-based removal of the top $k$ duplicate hubs against degree-based removal of the top $k$ singleton hubs at small $f$ ($1\%, 2\%, 5\%, 10\%, 20\%$ of total network nodes, where only true hubs are removed), and compare against a null where the 12,897 nodes are randomly partitioned into two halves of ~6,448 nodes and attacked within one half only.
* **HOW:** Add a dashed baseline curve in Figures 8C and 8D showing the effect of degree-based attack restricted to a random 50% node subset (repeated 100 times). This will show that the difference between the "All genes" curve and the "Duplicates / Singletons" curves in Figure 8C–D is entirely explained by restricting the attack pool to 50% of the nodes.

#### 4. Reconcile Numerical Discrepancies Between the Main Text and Table 1
* **WHY:** Table 1 (line 37) reports that the average degree $\langle k \rangle$ of the 6,507 duplicate genes is **120.11**, whereas the main text (line 44) states that duplicate genes had an average node degree of **"102.1 vs. 144.9; Table 1"** (a transposition typo of `120.1` $\to$ `102.1`). Additionally, line 118 states that 30% of 12,897 genes is "3770 genes," whereas $0.30 \times 12,897 = 3,869$ genes.
* **WHAT & HOW:** Correct `102.1` to `120.1` on line 44 to match Table 1 (since $(6507 \times 120.11 + 6390 \times 144.92) / 12897 = 132.40$, confirming Table 1 is the mathematically consistent value), and verify whether `3770` on line 118 was a typo for `3869` or if only 3,770 nodes were actually removed in the 30% simulation step.
