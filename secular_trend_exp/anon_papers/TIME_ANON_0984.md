## Abstract

Pea ( Pisum sativum L.) is a high‐nutrient, cool‐season legume of increasing relevance in plant‐based nutrition and sustainable agriculture. As demand for alternative protein sources increases, improving pea seeds’ nutritional content and quality through genomics‐assisted breeding has become a priority. Despite its importance, limited research has explored the genetic basis of nutritional traits in pea. In this study, 267 accessions from the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Pea Single Plant Plus Collection were evaluated across 3 years at two USDA‐certified organic farms in South Carolina to (1) assess phenotypic variation, (2) characterize the population structure and origin, and (3) perform a genome‐wide association study (GWAS) using 54,316 single‐nucleotide polymorphism markers on five nutritional traits: protein concentration, sulfur‐containing amino acids (SAAs), dietary fiber, total starch, and protein digestibility (PDg). Population structure analysis using ADMIXTURE and principal components analyses identified 10 ancestral subpopulations. GWAS identified 17 marker‐trait associations for protein, SAA, and PDg, including a genomic hotspot on the proximal end of chromosome 5 associated with both protein and SAA. This region harbors candidate genes involved in seed development, germination, and protein biosynthesis, suggesting potential roles in protein and SAAs accumulation. These findings provide valuable insights into the genetic architecture underlying key nutritional traits and highlight candidate target genes for breeding high‐quality, biofortified pea cultivars. This research expands the genetic potential of pea as a sustainable and nutritious crop alternative for plant‐based food systems.

## INTRODUCTION

Pea ( Pisum sativum L.), known as dry, green, or field pea, is a cool‐season pulse crop cultivated globally for human consumption and animal feed (Smýkal et al., [YEAR] ). Due to its rich composition of a variety of nutritional and bioactive compounds—including protein, dietary fiber, starches, and micronutrients—pea consumption has been associated with numerous health benefits. The various health benefits of peas, as reviewed by Wu et al. ( [YEAR] ), have been demonstrated in several in vitro and in vivo experimental studies. These benefits include anti‐inflammatory, anti‐hypertensive, anti‐obesity, anti‐cancer, antioxidant, anti‐fatigue, anti‐diabetic, antimicrobial, and anti‐renal fibrosis effects (Wu et al., [YEAR] ). As the demand for plant‐based protein grows (Ohr, [YEAR] ), legumes such as pea have emerged as a viable and sustainable alternative to animal‐based protein, which typically requires substantial inputs and is a greater environmental burden (Espinosa‐Marrón et al., [YEAR] ).

From nutritional, economic, and environmental perspectives, pea represents a cost‐effective and sustainable source of protein and essential nutrients. Agronomically, it contributes to improved soil health and fertility through biological nitrogen fixation, enhances water‐use efficiency, and reduces emissions into the environment (Foyer et al., [YEAR] ; Powers & [REDACTED_AUTHOR], [YEAR] ; Stagnari et al., [YEAR] ). In addition, organically grown peas are gaining attention for their potential health and environmental benefits ([REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al., [YEAR] ). Several reports have demonstrated that organic production systems, which eliminate or greatly reduce the use of synthetic agrochemicals, provide a range of benefits for human health, soil quality, biodiversity, and environmental sustainability when compared to conventional farming practices (Murphy et al., [YEAR] ; Reganold & Wachter, [YEAR] ; Tuck et al., [YEAR] )

Despite these benefits, pea has historically received less breeding attention than major cereal crops, leaving opportunities for genetic improvement, particularly to enhance nutritional concentration and quality (Foyer et al., [YEAR] ; [REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al., [YEAR] ). In the context of sustainable plant‐based diets and global food security, biofortification and targeted breeding could help make pea a more effective source of essential dietary requirements ([REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al., [YEAR] ). Pea has naturally high levels of protein, ranging from 20% to 25% of dry seed weight, ranking just below lentil ( Lens culinaris Medik) with 26.1% protein content (Iqbal et al., [YEAR] ; Shanthakumar et al., [YEAR] ), although values as high as 33.9% (Sá et al., [YEAR] ) and 34.2% ([REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al., [YEAR] ) have been reported. Similar to other pulses, pea is relatively low in the essential sulfur‐containing amino acids (SAAs)—methionine and cysteine—thus limiting its amino acid profile (Sá et al., [YEAR] ). Increasing SAA concentration is, therefore, a critical target for biofortification. Pea seeds are also a rich source of dietary fiber, with total fiber content ranging from 23% to 31% (Kan et al., [YEAR] ). Starch content in pea seeds ranges between 34% and 41% (S. Wang et al., [YEAR] ), and protein digestibility (PDg) has been measured at up to 89% in in vitro assays (Sá et al., [YEAR] ). While pea seeds are naturally nutrient‐dense, further improvements in key traits such as seed protein concentration, SAAs, and PDg, as well as other nutritional components such as dietary fiber and total starch, are critical for enhancing their overall nutritional composition for human health (Sá et al., [YEAR] ; [REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al., [YEAR] ; Wu et al., [YEAR] ).

The genetic diversity of germplasm collections is essential for uncovering novel allelic variations that can be effectively harnessed for crop improvement (Foyer et al., [YEAR] ). Resources such as the diverse United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Pea Single Plant Plus Collection (PSPPC) (Holdsworth et al., [YEAR] ) represent a rich reservoir of genetic variation and offer untapped potential for improving many traits of interest in pea (Swarup et al., [YEAR] ; [REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al., [YEAR] ; [REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al., [YEAR] ). However, quantitative trait loci (QTLs) and genes underlying key seed traits such as protein, SAAs, fiber, starch, and PDg remain largely unexplored in pea. Therefore, the objectives of this study were to (1) quantify variation and correlations in seed nutritional traits across a diverse pea germplasm collection evaluated under organic field conditions in South Carolina (SC); (2) characterize the population structure, geographic origin, and genetic diversity among the accessions; and (3) identify genetic loci and candidate genes associated with key seed nutritional and composition traits in pea using genome‐wide association studies (GWAS). This study aims to provide the genomic locations of QTLs associated with key nutritional traits for breeding programs focused on developing superior high‐nutritional pea cultivars adapted to the SC and east coastal regions of the United States.


## MATERIAL AND METHODS


## Plant materials and field evaluation

In this study, a diverse subset of 297 accessions from the USDA PSPPC collection (Holdsworth et al., [YEAR] ) was selected based on their outstanding agronomic performance in SC, desirable agronomic and nutritional traits (data not shown), and their potential for incorporation into the Clemson University Organic Pulse breeding program. This subset comprised 267 genotyped PSPPC accessions (representing 59 countries) (Figure 1A ), 30 non‐genotyped accessions, and two commercial cultivars, AAC Carver and CDC Inca, all of which were evaluated in the field (Table S1 ).

The field experiments were conducted over 3 consecutive years ([YEAR], [YEAR], and [YEAR]) at two locations in a randomized complete block design with three replications/blocks in [YEAR] and two reps in [YEAR] and [YEAR]. In [YEAR], 297 accessions were planted at the Walter P. Rawl & Sons USDA‐certified organic location in Pelion, SC (RAWL20). In [YEAR] and [YEAR], the trials were conducted at the Clemson Organic Farm, Clemson, SC (CLEM21 and CLEM22, respectively). In [YEAR], 285 accessions were tested, whereas in [YEAR], a set of 101 accessions was selected and evaluated based on their previously measured protein concentration and agronomic adaptation to the organic breeding program from earlier trials. The three trials were planted with different field dimensions and plot configurations. The RAWL20 trial had field dimensions of 231′ × 176′, with plots measuring 4′ × 5′ and consisting of two rows with 15‐inch spacing. The seeding rate was approximately 50 seeds per plot, with alley spacing of 36 inches and row spacing of 18 inches. In the CLEM21 trial, the field dimensions were 192′ × 198′, with plots also measuring 4′ × 5′, but consisting of four rows with 15‐inch spacing. The seeding rate was approximately 104 seeds per plot, with alley spacing of 48 inches and row spacing of 18 inches. Finally, the CLEM22 trial had field dimensions of 330′ × 66′, with larger plots measuring 10′ × 5′ and four rows per plot, spaced 15 inches apart. The seeding rate was approximately 240 seeds per plot, with alley spacing of 60 inches and row spacing of 12 inches. All three trials were managed under similar organic practices, including mechanical inter‐row cultivation until canopy closure, supplemented by hand and flame weeding until harvest. No irrigation, fertilizer, or any chemical treatments were applied, and the fields were USDA‐certified organic. Pods were hand‐harvested at maturity, stored and dried in a drying barn, and then threshed using an Almaco Belt Thresher (Almaco, USA). Seed samples were then collected and ground for nutritional composition analysis, including traits such as protein, SAAs, total starch, dietary fiber, and PDg.


## Trait phenotyping

Total protein (%), SAA (%), dietary fiber (%), total starch (%), and PDg (%) were measured with a Cary 630 Fourier‐transform mid‐infrared spectrometer (Agilent Technologies) equipped with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory to collect spectral data from homogenized dry pea flour associated with the diversity panel. Each spectrum was acquired with 100 scans. A background scan with 200 scans was conducted every 30 min during spectral acquisition. The instrument was operated at a spectral resolution of 2 cm −1 , with a zero‐fill factor of 2, and employed a Happ–Genzel apodization. The spectral acquisition was performed with Micro Lab Personal Computer software (version B.06.5) (Agilent Technologies). To prevent sample carryover, the ATR crystal surface was cleaned with high‐performance liquid chromatography‐grade (assay > 99%) methanol (Fisher Scientific Company) between every sample. The phenotypic data based on the acquired spectra were predicted from partial least squares models designed for each trait as described previously ([REDACTED_AUTHOR], [REDACTED_AUTHOR], et al., [YEAR] ; [REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al., [YEAR] ). All chemometric analyses were performed with Micro Lab Expert software (version 1.1) (Agilent Technologies). Phenotypes were adjusted for 15% moisture for further analysis. SAAs data were not collected in [YEAR].


## Phenotypic and statistical analysis

The distribution and correlation among observed traits were calculated using the “ ggpairs ” function of the GGally R package (Schloerke et al., [YEAR] ), and descriptive statistics were calculated with the “ sumTable ” function of the vtable R package (Huntington‐Klein, [YEAR] ). The principal components analysis (PCA) biplot (PCA‐Biplot) analysis was performed with the “ prcomp ” function of the stats R package (R Core Team, [YEAR] ), and the results were displayed with the “ fviz_pca ” functions of the factoextra R package (Kassambara & Mundt, [YEAR] ). Broad‐sense heritability ( H 2 ) was calculated using the variance components of the fitted models. A test for normality was performed using the Shapiro–Wilk test with the “ shapiro.test ” R function, with a 5% significance level (R Core Team, [YEAR] ). The phenotypic data were grouped into five environments (subsets), namely RAWL20+CLEM21+CLEM22 (or PSP3), RAWL20+CLEM21 (or RA20CL21), RAWL20, CLEM21, and CLEM22, for calculating Bayesian random effects (Bayesian best linear unbiased predictions [BLUPs]). BLUPs were estimated with the “ stan_lmer ” function of the rstanarm R package (Goodrich et al., [YEAR]) using the models below (Table S2A , with and without block) and the “ ranf ” function of the lme4 R package (Bates et al., [YEAR] ) to extract the estimated values for the pea accessions.

## Genotyping

The PSPPC and Pisum fulvum ( n = 456) accessions have been previously described and sequenced (Holdsworth et al., [YEAR] ). A variant call format (VCF) file previously generated was used for genetic analysis (Powers et al., [YEAR] ). Variants were filtered using VCFtools v0.1.17 (Danecek et al., [YEAR] ) to include only biallelic single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (−min‐alleles 2 −max‐alleles 2) with a 5% minimum minor allele frequency (MAF) (−maf 0.05) and a maximum of 20% missing genotypes (−max‐missing 0.2). Beagle v5.4 was used to impute missing genotypes (Browning et al., [YEAR] ), with parameters set to default except for effective population size, which was set to 100,000. The filtered VCF file with 54,315 SNPs for 267 accessions evaluated in this study (Table S1 ) was converted into a numeric matrix (0 indicates homozygous for the major, 1 for heterozygous, and 2 homozygous for the minor allele) with VCFtools and into a haplotype map (HapMap) format using TASSEL v5 (Bradbury et al., [YEAR] ). Plink BED format was generated with VCFtools with the “—plink” flag. SNP density plots across the seven chromosomes were generated using the CMplot R package (Yin et al., [YEAR] ).


## Population structure and genetic diversity

Ancestry coefficients were estimated using ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 (Alexander & Lange, [YEAR] ) with 54,315 SNPs for 267 accessions. The admixture analysis was performed for multiple K ‐values ( K = 3–12). The optimal number of populations ( K = 10) was determined by selecting the model with the lowest cross‐validation (CV) error. Accessions were categorized into subpopulations based on their highest ancestry coefficient (>0.5). The admixture plot was generated using the ggplot2 R package (Wickham, [YEAR] ). The global distribution of accessions by ancestral subpopulation was visualized using ISO3 country codes for 59 unique countries. The codes for two accessions were missing and excluded from the figure. The “ ddply ” function in the plyr R package (Wickham, [YEAR] ) was used to calculate the mean ancestral coefficients for each country of origin. These values were then multiplied by the number of accessions per country before being translated into a SpatialPolygonsDataFrame using the “ joinCountryData2Map ” function, and ancestral population pie charts for each country of origin were added with the “ mapPies ” function of rworldmap R package (South, [YEAR] ). The PCA with accessions classified by ADMIXTURE subpopulation was performed with the “ prcomp ” function, and the results were displayed with ggplot2 .

Genetic diversity estimates within and among the 267 PSPPC accessions were estimated using VCFtools. The average nucleotide diversity ( π ) and Tajima's D to assess whether the population is neutrally evolving or under selective pressure (Tajima, [YEAR] ) were estimated using a nonoverlapping sliding window of 100 kb. The window of 100 kb was chosen to prevent sampling errors that may result from variations in SNP distribution when using low‐coverage sequencing (Gusnanto et al., [YEAR] ). Heterozygosity was calculated per locus using VCFtools and reported as the average across all polymorphic sites. The average value across the genome is reported for the three estimates.


## Genome‐wide association analysis

To identify marker‐trait associations (MTA), GWAS was conducted using the 267 genotyped and phenotyped accessions for five nutritional traits (protein, SAAs, fiber, total starch, and PDg). Analyses were performed across five environments (PSP3, RA20CL21, RAWL20, CLEM21, and CLEM22) using the Genome Association and Prediction Integrated Tool (GAPIT) v3 package in R (J. Wang & Zhang, [YEAR] ). GAPIT's model selection with the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) was used to identify the optimal number of principal components (PCs), which determined that the kinship matrix sufficiently accounted for population structure; therefore, PCs were not included in the analyses. Furthermore, GAPIT analysis was performed using the multiple analyses flag on and the VanRaden kinship matrix algorithm to control for population structure with seven models included: (1) generalized linear model (GLM), (2) mixed linear model (MLM), (3) compressed MLM (CMLM), (4) settlement of MLM under progressively exclusive relationship (SUPER), (5) multiple loci mixed model (MLMM), (6) fixed and random model circulating probability unification (FarmCPU), and (7) Bayesian‐information and linkage‐disequilibrium iteratively nested keyway (BLINK). Bonferroni thresholds of p < 0.05 (0.05/54,315 = 9.206e‐07, or −log 10 = 6.036) and p < 0.01 (0.01/54,315 = 1.841e‐07, ‐−log 10 = 6.735) were used to determine significance. The significant SNPs with an MAF below 0.05 and with near‐zero percentage of variance explained (PVE) were excluded. Manhattan and quantile–quantile (Q‐Q) plots were drawn using CMplot. Linear models were fitted for each significant SNP using the “ lm ” function from the stats R package to confirm associations with phenotypic data. Allelic effects were visualized using boxplots generated with ggplot2 .


## Linkage disequilibrium and identification of candidate genes

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) blocks were determined using PLINK (Purcell et al., [YEAR] ) by calculating pairwise correlations ( r 2 ) of significant SNPs with all adjacent SNPs within a 1‐Mb window. The minimum r 2 threshold was set to zero to consider all SNPs. LD blocks were determined to decay either at the first SNP with r 2 < 0.4 or at 100 kb upstream and downstream the final linked SNP, whichever was less. The 200‐kb window accounted for the low SNP density in many genomic regions, which would otherwise lead to highly inflated LD block sizes ([REDACTED_AUTHOR], [REDACTED_AUTHOR], et al., [YEAR] ). Only SNPs with an MAF greater than 0.05 were considered. The genes within LD blocks and the chromosome and position coordinates were retrieved from the general feature format (GGF3) file of the P. sativum Cameor genome v1a ( https://www.pulsedb.org ) using a custom Linux awk code.


## RESULTS


## Summary statistics and correlations

Broad‐sense heritability estimates, derived from variance components of the fitted models, were generally low: 0.21 for protein, 0.13 for SAA, and 0.15 for starch. Heritability estimates for total fiber and PDg were zero. Descriptive statistics for key seed composition traits were calculated for individual and all environments (Table S3 ). The average values were 19.0 ± 0.89% (range: 16.6%–21.9%) for protein, 0.21 ± 0.05% (range: 0.10%–0.52%) for SAA, 22.5 ± 1.29% (range: 18.0%–26.9%) for fiber, 49.0 ± 2.57% (range: 38.9%–57.2%) for total starch, and 85.0 ± 0.70% (84.0%–87.00%) for PDg. Although the Shapiro–Wilk test showed deviation from normality at a 5% level of significance, visual inspection of the histograms showed that protein, dietary fiber, and starch distributions were relatively normal (symmetry and no heavy tails), while SAA concentration had a slightly skewed distribution and PDg data were grouped at four discrete values, which were controlled by calculating the BLUPs. Across environments, pairwise correlation analysis revealed that protein content showed a strong positive correlation with fiber ( r = 0.68) and PDg ( r = 0.59), suggesting that higher protein concentrations are associated with increased fiber and PDg. Additionally, fiber and PDg were strongly correlated ( r = 0.64). Overall, protein, fiber, and PDg appeared to be tightly associated, whereas SAA and starch did not correlate significantly with any other traits (Figure 2 ). These relationships were generally consistent across environments and maintained for the estimated trait BLUPs (Figure S1 ). PCA‐biplot analysis of phenotypes and BLUPs revealed wide phenotypic variability among the pea accessions; however, no distinct clustering was observed based on the ADMIXTURE‐derived subpopulation structure. Axis 1 (PC1) explained between 46.5% and 43.5% of the total phenotypic variance, and axis 2 (PC2) accounted for 20.4% and 20.4% for observed trait values and estimated BLUPs, respectively (Figures S2–S3 ). The PCA‐biplots supported the strong significant correlations between protein, fiber, and PDg. The threshold was used to identify top‐performing accessions for each trait or combination of traits (Figure S3 and Table S2B ). Accession PI_180693 exhibited high values for four traits (protein, fiber, starch, and PDg) (Figure S3 , in green). When evaluated individually, 14, 12, 11, 14, and 14 accessions ranked in the top 5% across environments for protein, SAA, fiber, starch, and PDg, respectively (Table S2B ), with the top three performers for each trait shown in aqua in Figure S3 .

## Population structure and genetic diversity

The VCF file (Powers et al., [YEAR] ) contained 54,315 high‐quality SNPs distributed across the genome (Figure S4 ). Using the genetic information, the 267 PSPPC accessions from 59 countries (Figure 1A ) were clustered into 10 ancestral subpopulations (Figure 1B ) by ADMIXTURE analysis with the lowest CV error (Figure S5 ). Accessions were categorized into subpopulations based on their highest ancestry coefficient (>0.5). Subpopulations 1 through 10 were composed of 10 (violet), 20 (light blue), 11 (blue), 37 (pink), 31 (yellow), 25 (red), 20 (orange), 15 (purple), 17 (brown), and 81 (green) accessions, respectively. Mostly subtle visual associations were seen between subpopulations and regions of origin (Figure 1A ). Subpopulation 10 showed the most admixture with the other subpopulations. The PCA supported the ADMIXTURE clustering. The first three PCs accounted for 11.6, 3.9, and 2.9% of the total variation (Figure 1C ), where subpopulations 1, 4, 6, 8, and 9 were the most differentiated clusters. Observed average nucleotide diversity ( π ), Tajima's D statistic, and heterozygosity were estimated to study genetic diversity within the 267 accessions of the PSPPC population. Nucleotide diversity ranged from 0.89 (subpopulation 4) to 1.14 (subpopulation 5). The overall average for all subpopulations was 1.11. The Tajima's D varied across subpopulations, ranging from 0.06 (subpopulation 3) to 0.51 (subpopulation 5). The overall average Tajima's D was 0.99. Finally, overall average heterozygosity was 0.88 and varied from 0.73 (subpopulation 8) to 0.89 (subpopulations 4 and 5) (Table 1 ). The overall averages for the three estimates suggest that in general, the population exhibits moderate‐to‐high genetic variation, which is beneficial for improving the traits of interest.

## Genome‐wide association studies

GWAS analyses identified 77 MTAs or significant lead SNPs for five nutritional traits (protein, SAAs, fiber, total starch, and PDg) (Figures S6–S15 ); however, a total of 17 peak SNPs were analyzed further because these had either post‐filtering minor allele frequencies equal to or above 0.05 or PVE far from zero (>0.3%). These were found for protein, SAA, and PDg and supported by multiple environments and GWAS models (Figure 3 and Table S4 ). While no lead SNPs were significantly associated with multiple traits, seven SNPs located in distinct LD blocks on chromosome 5—particularly near the proximal end between 61.7 to 78.5 Mb—were significantly associated with both protein content and SAA (Figure 3 and Table S4 in bold). Markers in this region explained, on average, 1.6% (from 0.4% to 3.1%) of the variation in protein and 12.9% (from 7.5% to 18.3%) of the variation in SAA. In contrast, the other 10 significant SNPs were scattered across multiple chromosomes, showing less consistent clustering than those found on chromosome 5. However, some SNPs explained considerable variation: S3LG5_434614474 on chromosome 3 explained 10.3% of protein variation; S2LG1_5715307 and S2LG1_292238595 on chromosome 2 explained 8.6% and 9.7% of SAA variation, respectively; and S6LG2_102304910 on chromosome 6 explained 29.3% of PDg variation. Compared to 10 peak SNPs associated with protein and six with SAA across the genome, few or no SNPs were associated with other traits: zero with fiber, one with PDg (Table S4 ). To confirm the association between individual significant SNPs and trait values, p ‐values from fitted linear models were estimated. Fifteen MTAs showed significant associations ( p < 0.05), supporting their association phenotypic values (Figure S16 ). However, two markers (chr4LG4_76004069 and chr7LG7_47365879) appeared to have only minor effects on protein content and did not have statistical significance.

## DISCUSSION

Pea is an important food legume for both human nutrition and sustainable agriculture due to its nutrient‐dense seed composition, affordability, and agroecological benefits, such as biological nitrogen fixation and soil health improvement (Foyer et al., [YEAR] ; Stagnari et al., [YEAR] ). Despite its value and potential for higher consumption, pea remains underutilized, and nutritional breeding efforts and resources have lagged compared to major crops (Powers & [REDACTED_AUTHOR], [YEAR] ). Enhancing the nutritional quality of pea—particularly traits such as protein concentration and digestibility—has become a priority in breeding programs employing traditional breeding and advanced genetic and genomic tools (Guindon et al., [YEAR] ).

The release of the P. sativum reference genome (Kreplak et al., [YEAR] ) and the development and characterization of genetic resources such as the PSPPC diverse collection (Holdsworth et al., [YEAR] ) provide new opportunities to accelerate trait discovery and genomic‐assisted breeding in pea. Powers et al. ( [YEAR] ) conducted a GWAS on seed micronutrients (including iron, phosphorus, and zinc) using the PSPPC collection, but GWAS targeting seed nutritional traits such as protein content, SAAs, dietary fiber, total starch, and PDg using the PSPPC collection had not been yet conducted. This knowledge gap provided the basis for the current research. This study evaluated the PSPPC germplasm under organic field conditions in SC to determine the population structure, phenotypic variation, and genetic basis of key seed nutritional traits through GWAS. Our findings reveal novel MTAs and candidate genes significantly associated with protein content and SAAs, two traits critical for pea improvement and human nutrition (Ohr, [YEAR] ).

Phenotypic variation among genetically diverse germplasm is essential for identifying valuable accessions for selecting and enhancing desirable traits (Tayeh et al., [YEAR] ). This study observed phenotypic variation across traits and environments (Table S3 ), with broad‐sense heritability ranging from 0 to 0.21, indicating generally low genetic control and large environmental influence under the tested conditions. Heritability values are comparable with those reported in pea, where H 2 was low for protein and PDg (0.25 and 0.09, respectively) ([REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al., [YEAR] ). Protein content among the PSPPC accessions ranged from 16.7 to 21.9 (mean 19 ± 0.9), which aligns with previous reports (20%–25%) (Iqbal et al., [YEAR] ; Shanthakumar et al., [YEAR] ; [REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al., [YEAR] ), with a lower bound (16.7%) slightly below reported ranges. Similarly, fiber ranged from 18.0 to 26.9, comparable to reported values of 23.23%–30.72% (Kan et al., [YEAR] ), and starch content varied from 38.9% to 57.2%, broadly consistent with previously reported levels between 34% and 42.7% (S. Wang et al., [YEAR] ). PDg ranged from 84.0% to 87.0%, slightly below the 88.6% reported by Sá et al. ( [YEAR] ). The mean SAA content was 0.21 ± 0.05, consistent with the known SAA‐limited levels in pulse crops such as pea. Notably, 12 genotyped accessions (PI_220174, PI_227258, PI_269761, PI_272216, PI_280619, PI_324702, PI_343292, PI_347490, PI_413703, PI_494077, PI_505127, and PI_505144) ranked in the top 5% (95th percentile) across environments, making them promising donor parents for future breeding efforts (Table S2B ). Strong positive correlations were observed among protein, PDg, and dietary fiber ( r = 0.59–0.68; Figure 2 ), suggesting selecting for high protein content may simultaneously select for high fiber and PDg. Conversely, the absent correlations between SAAs and other traits indicate increasing SAA content may require independent, targeted breeding strategies while maintaining overall seed quality. To improve multiple traits simultaneously, selection index methods are commonly employed (Yan & Frégeau‐Reid, [YEAR] ).

GWAS identified 17 MTAs in local linkage disequilibrium with 44 unique gene IDs for protein, SAA, and PDg (Table 2 , Tables S5 and S6 ), and associations were demonstrated by fitted linear models for each significant SNP. A hotspot at the proximal end of chromosome 5, encompassing multiple LD blocks, harbors 10 candidate genes potentially involved in protein and SAA accumulation. While functional characterization in pea remains limited, comparative evidence from other legumes or model species provides valuable context for putative functions. For example, genes such as Psat5g034840 encode protein phosphatase 2C (PP2C) significantly associated with protein are implicated in abscisic acid (ABA) signaling pathways. In A. thaliana , clade A PP2Cs, such as ABI1 and ABI2, act as negative regulators of ABA signaling, influencing seed germination and early seedling growth. Mutations in these genes can lead to altered sensitivity to ABA and reduce seed development (Park et al., [YEAR] ). The APETALA2 (AP2) domain gene (Psat5g043760, associated with SAA) is involved in flower and seed development in Arabidopsis (Jofuku et al., [YEAR] ; Ohto et al., [YEAR] ), influences seed mass (Ohto et al., [YEAR] ), and is associated with linoleic acid content in soybean seeds (Qin et al., [YEAR] ). Additional candidate genes, without a direct evidenced role in seed development and nutritional traits biosynthesis, such as the eukaryotic translation initiation factor eIF2A (Psat5g034880), BRCT domain protein (Psat5g034920), and dicer dimerization domain protein (Psat5g034960), were associated with both protein and SAA content, suggesting pleiotropic effects (Solovieff et al., [YEAR] ). The eIF2A facilitates protein synthesis under stress conditions in eukaryotes (Lowe & Montell, [YEAR] ); the BRCT domain protein is involved in DNA repair (Vladejić et al., [YEAR] ); and the dicer dimerization domain, important for dicer function, is involved in a wide range of biological processes (Vergani‐Junior et al., [YEAR] ). ATPases such as Psat5g037840 annotated as an E1‐E2 ATPase, and Psat5g037880, encoding a cation‐transporting ATPase, both associated with protein, are integral membrane proteins that utilize ATP hydrolysis to transport ions across cellular membranes, maintaining ion gradients that play crucial roles in a wide range of fundamental cellular and physiological processes during plant growth and development and stress adaptation (Zhao et al., [YEAR] ). BED zinc finger proteins (e.g., Psat5g042760 associated with protein) regulate transcription during growth, development, and cellular responses to biotic and abiotic stresses (Hussain et al., [YEAR] ).

Several studies have used QTL mapping and GWAS to investigate agronomic and nutritional traits in pea. Most of the loci identified in the present study do not overlap with previously reported associations, highlighting the novelty of our findings. Zhou et al. ( [YEAR] ) identified QTL associated with seed protein concentration using a recombinant inbred line (RIL) population, located on chromosome 2 (291.3–454.5 Mb) and chromosome 3 (24.1–437.2 Mb) (Zhou et al., [YEAR] ). These regions overlap with those identified in our study, suggesting possible shared genetic control of seed protein content across diverse genetic backgrounds and mapping populations. In contrast, Gali et al. ( [YEAR] ) reported 251 MTAs across 135 diverse pea accessions, including crude protein QTL on chromosome 3 (138.3 and 194.5 Mb) and chromosome 5 (145 Mb) (Gali et al., [YEAR] ). While agronomic and seed quality traits have been explored using the USDA Pea Single Plant (PSP) collection, protein‐related traits were not investigated in that study (Cheng et al., [YEAR] ). More recently, Uhdre et al. ( [YEAR] ) analyzed crude seed protein and fat concentrations in the USDA pea diversity panel, identifying protein‐associated loci on chromosome 1 (34.3 Mb), chromosome 3 (327.1 Mb), chromosome 4 (206.0 Mb), and chromosome 7 (434.8 Mb) (Uhdre et al., [YEAR] ). In addition, Klein et al. ( [YEAR] ) conducted a meta‐analysis involving 1213 RILs and identified 21 QTL associated with seed protein content across multiple genomic regions (Klein et al., [YEAR] ).

These findings provide valuable insights and key genetic targets for marker‐assisted or genomic selection pipelines to enhance pea nutritional quality. Improving protein content, SAA levels, and PDg is critical to address nutritional gaps and support the growing demand for plant‐based protein (Ohr, [YEAR] ). The genes identified encompass diverse biological functions, with many directly implicated in seed development and nutrient metabolism, thus reinforcing their value for future functional validation and trait improvement.

ADMIXTURE analysis of the 267 pea accessions from 59 countries revealed significant genetic diversity, as evidenced by the identification of 10 ancestral subpopulations. PCA supported these findings, indicating a complex population structure with varying degrees of admixture. These results align with those previously reported, where 10 subpopulations were identified for the PSPPC collection, except for one subpopulation represented by P. fulvum accessions (Powers et al., [YEAR] ), which were not included here. The overall nucleotide diversity ( π = 1.11) indicates a relatively high level of nucleotide variation within the PSP collection, which is beneficial for breeding as it provides a broad genetic base for trait improvement (Nei, [YEAR] ). Moreover, the Tajima's D values of 0.99 suggest a deviation from neutral evolution ( D = 0), potentially indicating balancing selection or population structure (Tajima, [YEAR] ). Finally, the average heterozygosity of cross subpopulations was 0.88, indicating relatively high genetic variability within the PSP germplasm (Kanaka et al., [YEAR] ) (Table 1 ). Together, these results support the exploration of novel genetic diversity of the PSPPC germplasm in breeding programs and offer a framework to identify and select genetically diverse parental lines (Bari et al., [YEAR] ) to optimize gains in protein and other nutritional traits.

The loci and candidate genes identified in this study provide a foundation for future functional validation studies to (1) confirm the SNP–trait associations, particularly with the relatively low heritability estimates for protein (0.21) and SAA (0.13), and (2) uncover causative genes and pathways underlying (Varshney et al., [YEAR] ) nutritional traits in pea. Follow‐up studies could include test marker in independent populations, fine mapping, allele mining, RNA sequencing for gene expression profiling, and CRISPR‐Cas9 genome editing to understand gene function. Additionally, metabolomics and proteomics can be integrated to elucidate causative genes (Langridge & Fleury, [YEAR] ; Thomson et al., [YEAR] ). These studies will accelerate the development of nutritionally enhanced pea cultivars tailored to organic production systems through marker‐assisted and genomic selection approaches.


## CONCLUSION

Pea is a nutritionally rich crop with significant potential to contribute to global biofortification efforts, particularly as a sustainable source of plant‐based protein. While its naturally high protein content makes it valuable, further enhancement of protein content and other nutritional traits is still needed. This study advances the knowledge base of pea seed composition by identifying novel MTAs and candidate genes associated with protein, SAAs, and PDg. Moreover, the targeted exploration and utilization of genetic variation within diverse germplasm collections, such as the PSPPC population, remain essential for advancing pea breeding and biofortification efforts ([REDACTED_AUTHOR] et al., [YEAR] ).
