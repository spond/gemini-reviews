# FEXP_ANON_145

## Abstract

Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML) is particularly challenging to treat due to the T315I BCR::ABL1 mutation. Although fungal metabolites are known for their pharmaceutical potential, none are approved for CML. Our study screened approximately 2000 fungal secondary metabolites to discover inhibitors targeting the T315I- BCR::ABL1 mutant protein.

We conducted comprehensive analyses to elucidate the interactions between the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein and selected fungal metabolites. These analyses included molecular docking, ADMET assessment, molecular dynamics simulations, principal components analysis, exploration of free energy landscapes, and per-residue decomposition.

We identified a range of binding affinities for fungal secondary metabolites, from −11.2 kcal/mol to −2.90 kcal/mol, with the co-crystal ponatinib showing a binding affinity of −9.9 kcal/mol. Notably, twenty seven fungal metabolites had affinities ≤ -10.0 kcal/mol, surpassing ponatinib. Eight compounds, including Phellifuropyranone A and Meshimakobnol B, showed favorable drug-likeness. Molecular dynamics parameters, including RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and SASA, confirmed that Phellifuropyranone A and Meshimakobnol B bind stably to the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein. Additionally, PCA, DCCM, and free energy landscapes analyses validated the consistency of the molecular dynamics parameters. MM/PBSA analysis indicated that Phellifuropyranone A (–22.88 ± 4.28 kcal/mol) and Meshimakobnol B (−25.86 ± 3.51 kcal/mol) bind similarly to ponatinib (−25.54 ± 6.31 kcal/mol). Per-residue decomposition explored residues MET290, VAL299, ILE315, and PHE359 as crucial for binding to the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein.

Phellifuropyranone A and Meshimakobnol B show significant potency as inhibitors of the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein, comparable to ponatinib. These compounds may serve as effective alternatives or synergistic agents with ponatinib, potentially overcoming drug resistance and improving treatment outcomes in Chronic Myeloid Leukemia.


## Background

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) is a clonal expansion of progenitor hematopoietic stem cells, resulting from the fusion BCR::ABL1 oncogene. 1 This oncogene accounts for 15 %-20 % of newly diagnosed leukemia cases. The BCR::ABL1 oncogene encodes a BCR::ABL1 tyrosine kinase (TK) that activates various downstream targets, such as c-Myc, STAT5, and CrkL. 2 , 3 These targets play crucial roles in regulating cell differentiation, proliferation, migration, and other cellular behaviors. For sustained disease control in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), the use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors targeting BCR::ABL1 proves effective. BCR::ABL1 inhibitor, stands as the exemplar of targeted therapy in CML, offering a safe and effective treatment for most patients diagnosed with chronic phase disease. 4 Under the selective pressure of TKI, approximately 50 % of chronic phase (CP) cases develop drug resistance. This resistance arises from recurrent BCR::ABL1 amino-acid exchanges, altering the conformation of the ABL1 tyrosine kinase pocket. Consequently, the activity of the most commonly prescribed TKI is impaired at various levels. Notably, the T315I BCR::ABL1 mutation poses the most significant challenge, as it entirely disrupts contact between the ABL tyrosine kinase domain and these three TKI. 5 , 6 , 7 The T315I mutation represents a unique alteration in the ATP binding region of the BCR::ABL1 protein. This mutation alters the conformation of the binding site in such a way that most currently available tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKI) are unable to effectively bind and inhibit the activity of the BCR::ABL1 protein. As a result, leukemia cells harboring this mutation are resistant to the majority of TKI therapies. 8 , 9 In chronic phase chronic myeloid leukemia, mutations in BCR::ABL1 are a well-characterized common mechanism of resistance to first-line drug treatments. 10 Analysis of BCR::ABL1 mutations is recommended to aid in selecting the appropriate therapy for chronic myeloid leukemia patients post-imatinib treatment failure, as certain commonly encountered mutations can lead to clinical resistance to nilotinib and/or dasatinib. 11 The presence of the T315I mutation in patients with chronic myeloid leukemia has been associated with reduced efficacy of nilotinib, which can complicate efforts to manage the disease effectively. This mutation presents a significant hurdle in achieving favorable treatment responses, necessitating careful consideration and potentially alternative therapeutic strategies 12 The frequency of ABL gene mutations among chronic myeloid leukemia patients who develop resistance to imatinib is a critical aspect in understanding treatment outcomes. Investigating the results of switching to second-generation tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) following resistance to imatinib provides valuable insights into the efficacy of alternative therapies in managing the disease. 13 The prominence of the T315I mutation underscores a substantial obstacle in addressing chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), as it severely constrains the available therapeutic avenues for patients carrying this mutation. This limitation presents a formidable challenge in managing the disease effectively and emphasizes the urgent need for innovative treatment strategies tailored to address such resistant mutations.

Over the past few decades, fungal metabolites have emerged as key players in the realm of cancer drug discovery, contributing significantly to the development of numerous therapeutic agents. 14 The captivating realm of fungus-derived secondary metabolites offers immense biomedical potential and chemical diversity. Exploring endophytic fungi for drug candidates remains an ongoing and promising endeavor within the domains of drug discovery and medicinal chemistry. 15 Derived from fungal secondary metabolites, numerous compounds offer essential alternatives to strengthen the arsenal of chemotherapeutic agents against cancer. This diverse array of compounds presents promising avenues for innovation in cancer treatment strategies. 16 In recent decades, research into fungal-derived materials to combat cancer has made significant progress in terms of drug delivery vehicles, active anticancer ingredients, and cancer immunotherapy. Various components of fungi have been effectively utilized to accomplish specific tasks. In addition to identifying novel anticancer compounds from fungi, their native capsular structures can also serve as drug carriers to achieve increased oral bioavailability. 17 At both preclinical and clinical trial stages, certain well-characterized fungal metabolites exhibit surprisingly potent antitumor properties. While their precise mechanisms of action are still under investigation, substantial evidence indicates that they functionally target core regulatory pathways and enzymes dysregulated during cancer pathogenesis. 18 Kuttuvan Valappil Sajna et al revealed that fungal secondary metabolites possess significant therapeutic potential as anticancer agents. The secondary metabolites derived from fungus exhibit an antiproliferative role by inducing intracellular ROS production and mitochondrial membrane potential loss, ultimately leading to apoptosis. 19 In silico studies on a fungal metabolite against skin cancer proteins have unveiled its potential activity in combating skin cancer. 20 The evaluation of secondary metabolites from an endophytic fungus through in vitro and in silico studies reveals promising anticancer properties. 21 Altogether, fungal metabolites collectively represent a promising class of potential anticancer agents for combating cancer.

Despite fungi producing a wide array of structurally diverse metabolites, many of which have proven to be valuable sources of pharmaceuticals, no fungal-derived agent has yet been approved as a cancer drug in CML. This is despite extensive research efforts aimed at identifying fungal secondary metabolites with promising anticancer activities. Our objective was to gather approximately two thousand fungal secondary metabolites for screening their inhibitory activity against the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). Subsequently, we conducted comprehensive analyses, including molecular docking, ADMET assessment, molecular dynamics simulations, principal components analysis, exploration of free energy landscapes, and per-residue decomposition, to elucidate the interactions between the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein and selected fungal metabolites.


## Methods


## Collection of fungal secondary metabolites

MeFSAT, the first comprehensive manually curated database on Medicinal Fungi Secondary Metabolites And Therapeutics, serves as a valuable resource, compiling information on 184 medicinal fungi, 1830 secondary metabolites, and 149 therapeutic uses. The Medicinal Fungi Secondary Metabolites and Therapeutics (MeFSAT) database ( https://cb.imsc.res.in/mefsat/home ) provided us with the 3D structures of 1830 fungal secondary metabolites. This extensively curated resource offers comprehensive details on the structures, properties, and diverse applications of these compounds derived from medicinal fungi. 22 We downloaded the 3D structure of secondary metabolites from the download module of MeFSAT. In addition, we downloaded the 3-dimensional structure of co-crystal Ponatinib (AP24534) from the collaboratory for structural bioinformatics protein data bank (RCSB PDB) ( https://www.rcsb.org/structure/3ik3 ).


## Ligand preparation for docking study

The ligands underwent energy minimization using the Universal Force Field (UFF) with the optimization algorithm set to Conjugate, comprising a total of 200 steps. This process was conducted within Open Babel, integrated into the PyRx 0.8 tool. Subsequently, all ligands subjected to energy minimization were converted into the PDBQT format.


## Downloading and preparation of T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein

The crystal structure of the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein (PDB ID: 3IK3) with a resolution of 1.90 Å was downloaded from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB) − Protein Data Bank ( https://www.rcsb.org ). 4 Utilizing high-resolution protein 3D structures as targets, we conducted screening and evaluation of inhibitors. The retrieved proteins underwent preparation, involving the removal of co-crystallized ligands, ions, and water molecules. Additionally, polar hydrogen atoms were added to enhance accuracy, utilizing BIOVIA Discovery Studio software ( https://discover.3ds.com/discovery-studio-visualizer-download ). Finally, this structure served as the receptor molecule for screening with secondary metabolites library.


## Molecular docking study

For our molecular docking study, we employed PyRx software ( https://pyrx.sourceforge.io/ ). Initially, we utilized the PrankWeb tool ( https://prankweb.cz/ ) to identify the binding pocket of the selected protein. PrankWeb utilizes a machine learning-based approach for predicting ligand binding sites from protein structures. Following this, we converted the protein into a protein data bank with charges and atom types (PDBQT) format and selected the amino acid residues associated with the binding site to generate a grid. Subsequently, we conducted the docking study to assess the docking score between the selected receptor and ligands.


## ADMET analysis of fungal secondary metabolites

We utilized the SwissADME web tool (( https://www.swissadme.ch )) and pkCSM, which offers free access to a wide range of predictive models for physicochemical properties, pharmacokinetics, drug-likeness, medicinal chemistry evaluation, toxicity. 23 , 24 This tool includes both fast and robust predictive methods, including in-house proficient techniques, providing comprehensive insights into drug-related properties and behavior.


## Molecular dynamics simulations

Utilizing GROMACS 2023.2, a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was conducted on the protein–ligand complexes. 25 The simulations were performed under the influence of the selected CHARMM27 force field, enabling a detailed investigation into their structural stability and dynamic behavior over an extended period. The topology for the protein was generated using the pdb2gmx command, ensuring accurate representation of its molecular structure and interactions. For the ligands, topologies were obtained from the SwissParam server ( https://www.swissparam.ch/ ). 26 The unit cells containing the native protein and protein–ligand complexes were defined using the gmx_editconf command, employing a cubic box of 10 Å dimensions and incorporating periodic boundary conditions (PBC) to simulate an infinite system. Subsequently, the solvation of the unit cells was carried out to mimic the physiological environment of the protein. This was achieved using the gmx_solvate module, which utilized the TIP3P water model to add water molecules to the system. The system underwent charge neutralization using the gmx_genion module, which replaced solvent molecules with appropriate counter-ions (Na + for positive and Cl- for negative charges) to achieve electrostatic neutrality. Following this, system minimization was performed using the steepest descent algorithm until the maximum force reached < 100.0 kJ/mol/nm within a maximum of 50,000 steps to alleviate potential stresses. Subsequently, solvent and ions were equilibrated at 300 K in two steps: first in the NVT ensemble maintaining constant particle number, volume, and temperature, followed by the NPT ensemble with constant particle number, pressure, and temperature. Finally, production molecular dynamics (MD) was conducted for 100 ns, with each step lasting 2 fs, using the gmx_mdrun module.


## Trajectory analysis of simulated complexes

Following the production MD run, the obtained trajectory file underwent correction to remove the periodic boundary conditions (PBC) using the gmx_trjconv module in GROMACS. This process yielded a new corrected trajectory file, which was subsequently utilized for further analysis using the built-in tools available in GROMACS. 25 Essential parameters, including root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), were computed using various GROMACS tools. Specifically, the gmx_rms, gmx_rmsf, gmx_gyrate, and gmx_SASA commands were employed for this purpose. The analysis of hydrogen bond formation was conducted using the gmx hbond tool, providing valuable insights into molecular interactions. Graphical representations of the data were visualized using Grace Software, a powerful tool for generating clear and visually appealing plots ( https://plasma-gate.weizmann.ac.il/Grace/ ).


## Principal components analysis

Following this, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted to analyze the protein dynamics. This involved utilizing the gmx_covar and gmx_anaeig modules to determine the principal components and eigenvalues essential for understanding the underlying motions. To further explore the conformational space, the gmx_anaeig module was utilized again, this time with the −extr command. This allowed for the calculation of extreme projections along the trajectory based on the eigenvalues, which were then exported in PDB format. These extreme PDB files were subsequently used to generate a visual representation of protein motion using the ModeVectors.py script in PyMOL, resulting in a porcupine plot.


## Exploring free energy landscape and dynamics cross-correlation matrix analysis (DCCM) analysis

The xvg file generated during the PCA analysis served as a foundation for assessing the free energy landscape (FEL) using the gmx_sham module. This analysis provided valuable information about the energetics of both native and protein–ligand complexes for exploring their dynamic behavior. In the final step, dynamics cross-correlation Matrix analysis (DCCM) was conducted using the Bio3D R package. 27 To conduct the DCCM analysis, GROMACS trajectory files (.xtc) were transformed into Nanoscale Molecular Dynamics (NAMD) format (.dcd). 28 This conversion process was executed using VMD software, ensuring compatibility between different molecular dynamics simulation platforms for subsequent analysis. 29 This analysis provides insights into the correlated and anti-correlated motions within the protein regions of interest in protein–ligand complexes.


## Binding free energy (MM-PBSA approach) and per-residue decomposition analysis

Employing the Molecular Mechanics/Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area (MM-PBSA) approach, 30 the g_mmpbsa package is integral to GROMACS for assessing the binding free energy (BE) of ligand-bound complexes. This crucial tool calculates BE accurately, shedding light on the energetic landscape of ligand–protein interactions. The representation of the binding energy (BE) for the lead-bound protein complex was calculated using the MD simulation trajectories spanning 100 ns. The BE of the test complexes was determined utilizing these trajectories, employing the following equation: ΔG binding = G complex − (G protein + G ligand )

In the given equation, G complex signifies the energy associated with the lead secondary compound or standard inhibitor bound to the test protein complex. Moreover, G protein and G ligand represent the energies of the protein and ligand, respectively, within an aqueous environment.


## Results


## Fungal secondary metabolites potentially interacted with T315I- BCR::ABL1 mutant protein

The study conducted virtual screening on a selection of 1830 fungal secondary metabolites sourced from the MeFSAT database. The aim was to evaluate their potential as inhibitors against the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein, a known oncogenic enzyme associated with chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). By employing molecular docking approach, we identified the most promising compounds capable of effectively inhibiting the activity of this mutant protein (3IK3) with appreciable binding affinity. Prior to conducting molecular docking, we employed the PrankWeb tool to predict the binding sites of a known inhibitor ( Fig. 1 A ) . This step was crucial for facilitating optimal interactions between the compounds and its target at the molecular level. In addition, we identified the interacting residues with co-crystal ligand (AP24534 or ponatinib) ( Fig. 1 B), and these were combined with PrankWeb identified residues to generate a grid box. Interestingly, within the identified top-ranked pocket, we found that the co-crystal ligand (AP24534 or ponatinib) is embedded ( Fig. 1 A). Consequently, we selected this pocket for the molecular docking study of the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein with fungal secondary metabolites. We discovered a range of binding affinities among fungal secondary metabolites, spanning from −11.2 kcal/mol to −2.90 kcal/mol ( Supplementary Table 1 ). Furthermore, upon re-docking the co-crystal ponatinib, we observed a binding affinity of −9.9 kcal/mol. Our analysis unveiled that 27 fungal secondary metabolites exhibited lower binding affinities (≤ −10.0) compared to the reference co-crystal ponatinib (−9.9 kcal/mol), as outlined in Table 1 . This computational technique offers valuable insights into the intricate interplay between molecules, exploring the precise binding sites and the specific configurations adopted during the binding process. By simulating the docking process, we can elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying biological functions and design more effective drugs or therapeutic interventions targeting specific protein–ligand interactions. ## Fungal secondary metabolites are drug-like compounds to inhibit T315I- BCR::ABL1 mutant protein

Drug-likeness prediction plays a crucial role in the virtual screening of drug candidates, as it is believed to be closely linked to the comprehensive array of properties required for successful progression through clinical trials. This association underscores the importance of accurately assessing drug-likeness in early stages of drug discovery. 31 We conducted an analysis employing Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge rules to assess the drug-likeness of selected compounds identified through molecular docking. Remarkably, our investigation unveiled that eight compounds, namely Phellifuropyranone A (MSID001271), (+)-(R)-grifolinone C (MSID000080), Meshimakobnol B (MSID001179), Inoscavin D (MSID001082), Methylinoscavin C (MSID001200), Phellibaumin A (MSID001266), Schizine B (MSID001385), and Ganocin D (MSID000814), exhibited favorable drug-likeness properties ( Table 2 ). In contrast, the co-crystal Ponatinib did not demonstrate drug-likeness upon evaluation using the Lipinski, Ghose, Veber, Egan, and Muegge rules, as indicated in Table 2 . We further present the comprehensive physicochemical properties, including lipophilicity, water solubility, pharmacokinetics, and drug-likeness characteristics, in Supplementary Table 2 . ## Discussion

In this study, our focus was on understanding the structural implications of fungal secondary metabolites binding to the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein, a significant mutation associated with chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). This specific mutation alters the BCR::ABL1 protein, making it resistant to tyrosine kinase inhibitors, which are a crucial class of drugs used to treat CML. To conduct our investigation, we retrieved the three-dimensional structure of the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein from the RCSB Protein Data Bank and meticulously prepared it for molecular docking experiments. Molecular docking allowed us to simulate and analyze how these fungal secondary metabolites interact with the mutant protein at a molecular level. Understanding these interactions is pivotal for potentially developing new therapeutic strategies or modifying existing drugs to overcome resistance mechanisms in CML treatment. Initially, we screened compounds based on their binding affinity, prioritizing those that exhibited superior binding affinity compared to the approved drug ponatinib ( Table 1 ). Through molecular docking and ADMET studies, we identified eight compounds with promising binding affinities ( Table 2 ) and drug-like properties ( Table 2 ). These compounds are Phellifuropyranone A (MSID001271), (+)-(R)-grifolinone C (MSID000080), Meshimakobnol B (MSID001179), Inoscavin D (MSID001082), Methylinoscavin C (MSID001200), Phellibaumin A (MSID001266), Schizine B (MSID001385), and Ganocin D (MSID000814). Our findings revealed that these compounds exhibit significant binding affinities comparable to or greater than ponatinib, an approved drug for targeting the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein in chronic myeloid leukemia. Importantly, these compounds also demonstrated acceptable toxicity levels and possess drug-like properties, making them potential candidates for further development. Based on these results, we selected the top three complexes formed between these compounds and the mutant protein, along with the standard protein-ponatinib complex, for molecular dynamics simulation studies. These simulations aim to investigate the stability and dynamic behavior of these complexes, providing insights into their potential efficacy as therapeutic agents against the resistant T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein.

Next, we conducted a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation on the complexes involving the free protein T315I-BCR::ABL1 bound to Phellifuropyranone A, (+)-(R)-grifolinone C, and Meshimakobnol B, alongside the control complex of protein-ponatinib. The RMSD graphs revealed that the trajectories of T315I-BCR::ABL1 bound to Phellifuropyranone A and Meshimakobnol B converged to stable and uniform patterns ( Fig. 4 A). T315I-BCR::ABL1-(+)-(R)-grifolinone C exhibited relatively less stable behavior throughout the simulation. In contrast, the RMSD curve of the standard protein-ponatinib complex showed noticeable fluctuations over the simulation period. These results suggest that Phellifuropyranone A and Meshimakobnol B may offer more stable binding interactions with the mutant protein T315I-BCR::ABL1 compared to both (+)-(R)-grifolinone C and the approved drug ponatinib. The RMSF analysis was performed to evaluate the inherent flexibilities of the protein, revealing a consistent trend in protein flexibility across all complexes studied upon binding with ligands. The observed RMSF values showed minor fluctuations but remained fairly similar to each other ( Fig. 4 B). Next, we employed Rg analysis to examine the overall compactness of the protein structures across the studied complexes ( Fig. 5 A). The analysis revealed a consistent pattern among the complexes, where the observed Rg values exhibited minor fluctuations but remained fairly similar to each other. In contrast, control protein-ponatinib showed a slightly higher Rg during the simulation period. This suggests localized changes in protein compactness upon ligand binding. Since the protein is present in a solvent for the simulation, we have studied the SASA ( Fig. 5 B) for the protein and its complexes which showed a trend of Protein-Meshimakobnol B > protein > Protein-(+)-(R)-grifolinone C > Protein-ponatinib > Protein-Phellifuropyranone A, which shows that upon ligand binding significant changes occur in the tertiary structure of the protein. The high SASA value of Protein-Meshimakobnol B is credited to the larger size of the ligand.

Next, principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to identify essential motions amidst global dynamics. The analysis revealed significant decreases in Cα fluctuations in ligand-bound complexes, indicating substantial alterations in protein structure upon ligand binding. Two-dimensional plots of PC1, PC2, and PC3 illustrated notable changes in essential mobilities within the ligand-bound complexes ( Fig. 7 ), suggesting distinct conformational shifts in the protein under investigation. Moreover, the contribution of PC1, PC2, and PC3 to residue movements was assessed, exploring specific regions of the protein that undergo essential motion in the presence of ligands. Dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) analysis further underscored changes in correlations and anti-correlations among residues in the complexes, aligning with observations from PCA regarding altered essential mobilities ( Fig. 8 ) . Additionally, Porcupine analysis consistently identified essential motions within the protein structures ( Fig. 9 ) . Taken together, PCA analysis indicates that ligand binding induces significant changes in the structural dynamics of the protein, particularly within its catalytic pocket.

The analysis of free energy landscapes through Gibbs free energy computations using the first two principal components revealed distinct energy basin distributions among mutant protein–ligand complexes ( Fig. 10 ). These contour plots illustrate transitions between high and low energy states, with colors shifting from red to blue denoting metastable states ( Fig. 10 ). These findings underscore the unique energy landscape characteristics of each mutant protein–ligand complex. They not only highlight varying stability levels induced by different ligands but also suggest diverse modes of interaction and conformational dynamics within the catalytic pocket of mutant proteins. Such insights are crucial for understanding how these ligands modulate protein function and for guiding future drug design efforts targeting mutant proteins in therapeutic contexts.

Finally, the results from binding free energy calculations support the findings observed in docking simulations. The protein-phellifuropyranone A complex exhibited a binding free energy of 22.88 ± 4.28 kcal/mol, while the protein-(+)-(R)-grifolinone C complex showed a value of –23.29 ± 12.82 kcal/mol, indicating instability in some instances ( Fig. 11 ). In contrast, the protein-Meshimakobnol B complex demonstrated a more favorable binding free energy of −25.86 ± 3.51 kcal/mol ( Fig. 11 ). The analysis of binding free energies further highlighted that the protein-(+)-(R)-grifolinone C complex displayed positive values in certain frames, suggesting potential instability ( Fig. 12 ). Furthermore, in our analysis of per-residue decomposition, we identified specific residues crucial for the binding interactions with Phellifuropyranone A, Meshimakobnol B, and Ponatinib. Notably, MET290, VAL299, ILE315, and PHE359 were found to contribute significantly to the binding energies across all three compounds ( Fig. 13 ). Interestingly, the mutant residue ILE315 was observed to contribute less binding free energies for Phellifuropyranone A, Meshimakobnol B, than the standard Ponatinib, underscoring their effective interaction with the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein ( Fig. 13 ). However, this residue showed minimal contribution to binding with (+)-(R)-grifolinone C, indicating a distinct binding profile for this compound. In addition, ASP381 (8.42 kcal/mol), ASP363 (6.50 kcal/mol), GLU286 (4.74 kcal/mol), HIS361 (3.47 kcal/mol), and ARG362 (1.23 kcal/mol) were the top residues contributing positive energy to the binding with −(+)-(R)-grifolinone C ( Fig. 13 ). These positive energy values suggest an unstable interaction and weak binding affinity of −(+)-(R)-grifolinone C with the target protein. These findings highlight specific molecular interactions crucial for the efficacy of Phellifuropyranone A, Meshimakobnol B, and Ponatinib against the mutant protein. Understanding these residue-specific interactions provides insights into the structural basis of their binding affinities and can inform further optimization of these compounds or the development of new therapeutics targeting the T315I mutation in chronic myeloid leukemia. When compared to the approved drug ponatinib, both phellifuropyranone A and Meshimakobnol B demonstrate notable potency as inhibitors of the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein in chronic myeloid leukemia. This observation suggests that these compounds could potentially offer effective therapeutic alternatives or complementary treatments for cases where ponatinib may not be fully effective or tolerated. Their ability to inhibit the mutant protein underscores their potential utility in overcoming drug resistance and improving treatment outcomes in clinical settings.

Previous studies have highlighted the medicinal potential of phellifuropyranone A and Meshimakobnol B across various diseases. For instance, phellifuropyranone A has been noted for its moderate BCR::ABL1 cytotoxic activity and its potential role in anti-tumor treatments. 32

Additionally, both phellifuropyranone A and Meshimakobnol B have demonstrated antiproliferative effects against mouse melanoma cells and human lung cancer cells in vitro. 33 Moreover, research has identified phellifuropyranone A as possessing cytotoxic activity against several human cancer cell lines, emphasizing its promise as a valuable compound for developing novel anticancer therapies. 34 Furthermore, studies have reported that both meshimakobnol B and phellifuropyranone A exhibit antitumor effects in both in vitro and in vivo models. 35 . Meshimakobnol B and phellifuropyranone A, both isolated from a Japanese mushroom, have been identified as effective growth inhibitors of cancer cells. Further biological evaluation revealed that the presence of specific functional groups in these compounds is crucial for their cytotoxic effects, indicating their importance in determining anticancer activity. 36 It has been reported that drug compounds exhibit synergistic cytotoxicity when combined with anticancer agents, particularly in BCR-ABL positive leukemia cells. 37 This synergy can enhance the effectiveness of treatment, providing a promising approach for overcoming resistance and improving patient outcomes. This indicates the importance of fungal metabolites as a promising source of novel anticancer agents. Fungi produce a diverse array of secondary metabolites, many of which exhibit bioactive properties that can target various cancer cell pathways. 38 The ability of these compounds to inhibit the proliferation of aggressive cancer types, such as melanoma and lung cancer, indicates their potential therapeutic value. Previous studies have shown that numerous fungal metabolites possess unique mechanisms of action, including the induction of apoptosis, disruption of cell cycle progression, and modulation of signaling pathways involved in tumor growth. Fungi generate a wide variety of bioactive compounds, such as terpenes, polyphenols, and alkaloids, many of which demonstrate notable anticancer properties and have the potential to be developed as candidates or lead compounds for new anticancer medications. 39 By isolating and characterizing these metabolites, researcher can explore new avenues for cancer treatment, particularly for patients who have developed resistance to conventional therapies. The ongoing investigation of fungal-derived compounds not only enriches our understanding of their pharmacological potential but also paves the way for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies that utilize the unique properties of these natural products.

Given these findings, the therapeutic potential of phellifuropyranone A, Meshimakobnol B, and other fungal secondary metabolites against chronic myeloid leukemia, particularly in targeting the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein, warrants further exploration through comprehensive in vivo and in vitro studies. First, it is essential to assess the activity of these compounds in various cancer cell lines, both individually and in combination with existing anticancer therapies, to evaluate their efficacy in relevant biological contexts. Additionally, a thorough investigation of potential adverse events will be crucial for establishing a comprehensive safety profile, especially in combination treatments. The importance of examining drug solubility and exploring formulation strategies to enhance bioavailability cannot be overstated, as these factors are vital for effective clinical application. Finally, understanding off-target effects will be critical to ensuring the safety and specificity of these compounds. Addressing these areas will provide a clearer pathway for translating our findings into viable cancer treatments and contribute to the ongoing search for novel therapeutic options.


## Conclusions

This study investigated the structural influences of ligand binding on the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein in chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). We conducted a comprehensive screening of 1,830 fungal secondary metabolites, comparing their effects with the approved drug ponatinib to identify potential inhibitors for the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein. Global dynamics studies, based on RMSD, RMSF, Rg analysis, and SASA, indicated that binding with Phellifuropyranone A and Meshimakobnol B stabilizes the protein. Additionally, PCA and FEL analyses revealed highly stable states in the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein complexes with these compounds, suggesting stable transitions during simulation. Binding free energy calculations showed comparable binding energies for the approved ponatinib and the potential inhibitors Phellifuropyranone A and Meshimakobnol B, indicating their promise as inhibitors against the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein in CML patients.

While these findings provide valuable insights into the structural dynamics of the T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein and suggest potential new therapeutic candidates, it is essential to consider the broader treatment landscape for CML. Given the effectiveness of existing therapies, including ponatinib, we must evaluate the clinical relevance and financial viability of developing new compounds. Future studies should investigate not only the efficacy of these metabolites in relevant cell lines and combination therapies but also address their safety profiles and potential adverse effects. Additionally, it is essential to evaluate drug solubility, formulation, and off-target effects, which are crucial for assessing the feasibility of these compounds as therapeutic options. Overall, these findings provide valuable insights into the structural dynamics of T315I-BCR::ABL1 mutant protein and suggest potential new therapeutic candidates for chronic myeloid leukemia.
